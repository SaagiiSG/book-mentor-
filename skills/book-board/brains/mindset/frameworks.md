# Frameworks: Mindset department

Built from the 15 Mindset books (M01 to M15), most useful first. Names come from the books where they have one; explanations are ours. Scores are guidance for the advisor, not from the books. Quote policy: verbatim quotes only from author talks and the public-domain Meditations text (T102), with a T id; copyrighted book text is never quoted.

## 1. The Story Check (event versus judgment)
**Sources:** judgments, not events [M02]; perception [M10]; responsibility for your response [M13]. Synthesis of M02, M10, M13.
**What it is:** splitting what actually happened from the story you are telling about it, then choosing a more useful story that still fits the facts.
**Steps:** 1) Write the event in one neutral sentence a camera could record. 2) Write the story you are telling ("this proves I am not cut out for this"). 3) Mark which parts are fact and which are judgment. 4) Write one alternative story that also fits the facts. 5) Name the next action the better story points to.
**When to use:** first move on almost any Mindset question: a setback, a sting, a fear, a stall.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = event and story fused; 3 = story named but still believed; 5 = facts separated, a fitting alternative written, and a next action set.
**Example:** a café owner gets a one-star review and reads it as "the business is failing". Fact: one customer waited 25 minutes on a Saturday. New story: Saturday staffing is thin. Action: add a second barista from 9 to 12.
**Quote:** "Let opinion be taken away, and no man will think himself wronged." (T102)

## 2. The Control Sort
**Sources:** what is in your power [M02]; externals versus internals [M10]; Let Them, Let Me [M05].
**What it is:** sorting a worry into what you control (your effort, choices, standards, responses), what you influence, and what you do not control, then spending energy only on the first two.
**Steps:** 1) List everything about the situation that is weighing on you. 2) Mark each C (control), I (influence) or N (not yours). 3) For each C, set one action. 4) For each I, set one ask or offer. 5) For each N, write how you will respond if it goes badly, then stop rehearsing it.
**When to use:** rumination about the economy, competitors, a client's decision, a staff member's attitude, a funding answer.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = all energy on N items; 3 = sorted but still stewing; 5 = actions on C and I items scheduled, N items released.
**Example:** a builder waiting on a big tender stops refreshing email, follows up once (I), and books three smaller quotes this week (C).
**Quote:** "stoicism is about control in that sense how do you how do you make the distinction between externals and internals and focus most of your energy on internals" (T104)

## 3. Let Them, Let Me
**Sources:** [M05]; what is in your power [M02].
**What it is:** when people do not behave as you want, stop forcing, chasing or rescuing; let them show you who they are. Then ask what you will do: a standard, a boundary, a consequence, a different choice. Exceptions where you speak up: danger, your pay or rights, repeated boundary crossing.
**Steps:** 1) Name the person and what you keep trying to make them do. 2) Say "let them" and accept that this is who they are right now. 3) Ask "let me what?" 4) Act once, clearly. 5) Stop rehearsing their side.
**When to use:** approval chasing, rescuing staff, family pressure on the business, envy of a competitor.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = rescues or chases every time; 3 = notices but still steps in; 5 = sets the standard once and lets results land.
**Example:** a founder stops trying to win over a cofounder's skeptical spouse and instead writes a clear plan with a review date she controls.
**Hand-off:** managing the team itself goes to Relationships and Leadership.

## 4. The Resistance Compass
**Sources:** Resistance and fear as a signal [M15]; avoidance of identity threats [M13].
**What it is:** using the strength of your avoidance as a guide. The project you keep dodging, and fear most, is often the one that matters most to your growth.
**Steps:** 1) List the three or four projects on your mind. 2) Rate each 1 to 5 on how much you avoid it and 1 to 5 on how much it would change things if done. 3) The high-high project is your Resistance target. 4) Schedule the first ugly hour on it within 48 hours.
**When to use:** the owner is busy all week but the one important thing (pricing change, book, launch, sales calls) never moves.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = avoided project unnamed; 3 = named, not scheduled; 5 = named and worked on at least three times a week.
**Example:** a consultant has been redesigning her logo for months; the feared task is calling ten past clients. She does two calls before 10am each day.
**Quote:** "if you want to know which one of three or four projects that you should do, you should do the one you're most afraid of, because that fear is a form of resistance with a capital R" (T109)
**Limit:** fear can also be a sound warning. Check the plan with Business before using fear as a compass for a risky bet.

## 5. Turning Pro
**Sources:** amateur versus professional [M15]; daily discipline [M12]; perform without purpose [M01].
**What it is:** treating the important work like a job you show up to regardless of mood, with fixed hours, no waiting for inspiration, and no taking a bad day personally.
**Steps:** 1) Pick the work. 2) Set a fixed daily or weekly session (even two focused hours). 3) Remove inputs during it (phone, email). 4) Judge the session only by "did I show up and put in the time?" 5) Stop at a point where you know what comes next.
**When to use:** the owner works only when inspired, or reopens the same project every few weeks.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = works when it feels right; 3 = sessions set, kept half the time; 5 = kept four or more days a week for a month.
**Example:** a bakery owner writing her wholesale proposal works 6 to 8am on weekdays, phone in the van, and judges the week by hours logged.
**Quote:** "all I ask myself is did I put in the time and did I work as hard as I can. Quality will take care of itself later in the next draft and the next draft after that" (T109)
**Hand-off:** calendars and habit systems to Productivity; this frame is the identity behind them.

## 6. Fixed Mindset Trigger Log
**Sources:** [M09].
**What it is:** noticing the moments your fixed mindset voice wakes up (a big challenge, a setback, criticism, someone better than you), naming it, and answering it with a growth move.
**Steps:** 1) For two weeks, just log triggers and what the voice said; do not try to fix it yet. 2) Give the voice a name so you can spot it. 3) After two weeks, for each trigger write a growth response: what can I learn, who can teach me, what strategy can I try? 4) Pick one challenge you would normally avoid and take it.
**When to use:** the owner avoids skills the business needs, gets defensive with feedback, or envies peers.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = no awareness; 3 = triggers logged; 5 = triggers answered and at least one avoided challenge taken each month.
**Example:** a technical founder logs that every pricing call triggers "I'm not a sales person", then books a sales course and practices with a mentor.
**Quote:** "developing a growth mindset is really a journey it's a lifelong journey of monitoring your trigger points" (T103)

## 7. Not Yet
**Sources:** [M09]; long movie, not a snapshot [M12].
**What it is:** reframing a failure or a gap as a point on a learning path. "I can't do this" becomes "I can't do this yet, and here is the next step".
**Steps:** 1) Write the skill gap in plain words. 2) Add "yet". 3) Name the strategy, help or practice that would close it. 4) Set the first practice and a date to check progress.
**When to use:** the owner says "I'm just not a numbers person" or "I'm bad at selling".
**Score (guidance):** 1 = trait language ("I'm not"); 3 = "yet" said, no plan; 5 = a strategy and practice scheduled.
**Example:** a salon owner who "can't read a P&L" books four one-hour sessions with her bookkeeper and reviews the next month's numbers herself.

## 8. Process Praise
**Sources:** praise strategies, not traits [M09]; demanding and supportive [M12].
**What it is:** giving recognition for what a person did (strategies, persistence, learning, choices) rather than for talent or results alone, and never praising effort that was not there.
**Steps:** 1) Before praising, name what the person actually did. 2) Tie the good result to that action. 3) When work misses, ask what they tried and what they will try next. 4) Model it on yourself: describe your own wins by process.
**When to use:** staff fear mistakes, hide problems, or stop taking initiative; the owner beats themselves up over results they could not control.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = praise is "you're a natural"; 3 = mixed; 5 = praise names actions and misses become learning talks.
**Example:** instead of "great job, you're a star", a shop owner says "you called back all five unhappy customers within a day, and three reordered".
**Hand-off:** wider team culture to Relationships and Leadership.

## 9. Perception, Action, Will
**Sources:** [M10], built on [M02].
**What it is:** a three-step way through any obstacle. Perception: see it clearly without panic. Action: work it with energy, persistence and small steps. Will: accept what cannot be changed and keep going.
**Steps:** 1) Perception: write the facts and steady yourself (Story Check). 2) Find the opportunity inside it. 3) Action: list the smallest next step, then the next. 4) Iterate: try, learn, try again. 5) Will: decide what you will accept if it still does not work, and how you will carry on.
**When to use:** a setback has frozen the owner: a lost contract, a failed launch, a supplier failure.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = stuck in perception (panic or blame); 3 = acting but scattered; 5 = clear view, steady steps, and a plan for the worst case.
**Example:** after a key supplier closes, a restaurant owner states the facts, sees a chance to simplify the menu, trials two new suppliers in a week, and accepts a short dip in margin.
**Quote:** "the impediment to action advances action what stands in the way becomes the way" (T104)

## 10. Premeditation of Adversity
**Sources:** imagined loss and preparing for setbacks [M10]; morning premeditation [M02].
**What it is:** deliberately imagining what could go wrong, today or in a project, so it does not knock you over when it comes, and so you prepare for it.
**Steps:** 1) Before a launch, a hard meeting or each morning, list three things that could go wrong. 2) For each, write your planned response. 3) Note what you would still have if it happened. 4) Go ahead.
**When to use:** the owner is blindsided by normal setbacks, or dreads a big step.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = no preparation, every setback a shock; 3 = worst cases thought about but not written; 5 = written with responses before each big step.
**Example:** before raising prices, a gym owner expects some members to leave, writes the reply script, and decides how many cancellations she can absorb.
**Limit:** for an anxious owner who already catastrophizes, this can feed worry. Keep it short and end on the responses, or hand off to Psychology and Mental Health.

## 11. Aspire, Succeed, Fail (Ego Check)
**Sources:** [M11].
**What it is:** asking which phase you are in and how ego is likely to hurt you there. Aspiring: talking instead of doing. Succeeding: believing your own legend and stopping learning. Failing: blaming others or collapsing.
**Steps:** 1) Name your phase. 2) Read the risk for that phase. 3) Pick one counter: do the work quietly (aspire), stay a student and check luck (succeed), use the time and own your part (fail). 4) Ask one honest outsider where ego is showing.
**When to use:** feedback stings too much, a past win has made the owner careless, or a failure has become an identity.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = cannot name a phase or a risk; 3 = named; 5 = counter in place and outside feedback sought monthly.
**Example:** a founder after a strong year notices he has stopped visiting customers. He books two customer visits a week and asks his manager what he is missing.
**Quote:** "the idea is you're aspiring you get some Taste of success then you succeed maybe you plateau then you begin to get complacent and you fail and then you're back to aspiration" (T105)

## 12. The Failure Inside the Success
**Sources:** [M11]; luck and risk [M07].
**What it is:** after a win, reviewing what was luck, what you got wrong, and what you could do better, so a lucky break is not mistaken for a repeatable skill.
**Steps:** 1) Write the win. 2) List what you did that caused it. 3) List what was luck or timing. 4) List the mistakes that did not hurt you this time. 5) Plan the next round as if the luck will not repeat.
**When to use:** after a record month, a big client, a viral post, a funding round.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = the win is all skill; 3 = luck noted; 5 = luck and mistakes listed and next plan built on the repeatable part.
**Example:** a shop has its best month after a local news story. The owner budgets next quarter on normal months and works on the repeatable part: email to new customers.
**Quote:** "it's not looking backwards to see what you're proud of it's looking backwards to see what mistakes you might have made in the things that you could do better" (T105)

## 13. Choose Your Struggle
**Sources:** [M13]; Resistance proportional to importance [M15]; Hard Thing Rule [M12].
**What it is:** every goal comes with a kind of pain. Instead of asking what you want, ask what struggle you are willing to live with to get it.
**Steps:** 1) Write the goal. 2) List the daily pains it will bring (calls, rejection, long hours, money risk, boredom). 3) Ask honestly which of those you can accept or even enjoy. 4) If you cannot accept them, change the goal or the path. 5) If you can, stop waiting for it to feel easy.
**When to use:** the owner wants the result but keeps avoiding the work it needs, or is choosing between paths.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = wants the result, ignores the cost; 3 = costs listed; 5 = costs accepted and the path adjusted to them.
**Example:** a designer wants a seven-person agency but hates managing. She chooses a two-person studio with higher prices instead.

## 14. Values Audit
**Sources:** good values versus bad values [M13]; purpose over passion [M11]; three avenues to meaning [M03].
**What it is:** checking what you are actually measuring yourself by. Good values are based on reality, help others, and are in your control. Status, being liked by everyone, and always being right are poor ones.
**Steps:** 1) List what you check to see if you are doing well (revenue, followers, peer praise). 2) For each, ask: is it real, does it help others, is it in my control? 3) Replace one poor value with a better one (for example, "customers helped well" instead of "beating a rival"). 4) Change one weekly measure to match.
**When to use:** the owner feels hollow despite success, or is driven by comparison.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = measures are all status; 3 = mixed; 5 = main measures pass the three tests.
**Example:** an e-commerce founder stops tracking a rival's follower count and tracks repeat purchase rate.

## 15. Responsibility versus Fault
**Sources:** [M13]; owning the truth [M01]; the last freedom [M03].
**What it is:** separating whose fault something was from who is responsible for what happens next. A setback may not be your fault; the response is always yours.
**Steps:** 1) Write what happened and whose fault it was, honestly. 2) Write what is now your responsibility. 3) Choose one action that takes responsibility. 4) Drop the case for blame.
**When to use:** the owner is stuck blaming a partner, a market, a landlord, or themselves.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = stuck on fault; 3 = responsibility named; 5 = an action taken on it this week.
**Example:** a client goes bankrupt owing a printer 30 percent of a month's revenue. Not his fault. His responsibility: deposits on new jobs and a credit check from now on.

## 16. The Do Something Principle
**Sources:** [M13]; Turning Pro [M15]; perform without purpose [M01].
**What it is:** action comes first and produces inspiration and motivation, not the other way round. When stuck, do the smallest piece of the task.
**Steps:** 1) Name the stuck task. 2) Shrink it to something you can start in five minutes. 3) Do it now. 4) Notice whether the next step is clearer.
**When to use:** the owner is waiting to feel ready, motivated or confident.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = waiting for motivation; 3 = starts some days; 5 = starts with a small action by default.
**Example:** a founder dreading investor updates writes just the first line of the numbers, then finishes the update in 20 minutes.
**Hand-off:** if even small starts are impossible for weeks, consider low mood or burnout and hand off to Psychology and Mental Health.

## 17. Embarrassment Reps (spotlight test)
**Sources:** spotlight effect and tolerating embarrassment [M13]; seeking challenge [M09].
**What it is:** small, planned acts of mild public discomfort that prove people pay far less attention to you than you fear, and build tolerance for looking foolish.
**Steps:** 1) List three things you avoid because you might look silly (posting, asking for the sale, a video). 2) Pick the mildest. 3) Do it and note what actually happened. 4) Move up one step each week.
**When to use:** fear of judgment blocks visibility, selling or asking for help.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = avoids all; 3 = one rep done; 5 = a weekly rep habit for a month.
**Example:** a physio who never posts records one 60-second tip a week. Most get a handful of views and nobody mocks him; two lead to bookings.
**Quote:** "now the spotlight effect says that we all tend to assume that people are paying far more attention to us than they actually are" (T107)

## 18. Self-Sabotage Map
**Sources:** [M14]; avoidance of identity threats [M13].
**What it is:** treating a repeated self-defeating behavior as a sign of an unmet need, not a lack of willpower. Find the need, meet it another way, and the block loosens.
**Steps:** 1) Name the behavior (underpricing, not following up, picking fights before a big deal). 2) Ask what it protects you from: rejection, failure, being disliked, losing control, outgrowing people. 3) Name the belief behind it. 4) Find a safer way to meet the need. 5) Take one small step toward the goal while the need is met.
**When to use:** "I know what to do and I keep not doing it", after simple habits have failed.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = blames willpower; 3 = need named; 5 = need met another way and the behavior easing.
**Example:** a coach keeps undercharging because raising prices feels like being greedy. She names the belief, decides that a fair price funds better service, and raises prices for new clients only.
**Quote:** "what self-sabotage actually is is a self-preservation technique it's when we have two co-existing but needs one is conscious and one is unconscious and they're in direct opposition of one another" (T108)
**Hand-off:** if the roots are trauma or the pattern brings real distress, Psychology and Mental Health.

## 19. Feelings as Signals
**Sources:** anger and envy as information [M14]; comparison as information [M05].
**What it is:** reading strong feelings as messages. Anger often marks a crossed boundary; envy points at something you want; dread can mark a growth edge.
**Steps:** 1) Name the feeling. 2) Ask what it is telling you: what boundary, what want, what fear. 3) Decide one action that respects the message (state the boundary, pursue the want, take the small step). 4) Do not act on the raw feeling.
**When to use:** the owner snaps at people, resents a peer's success, or feels stuck in a mood.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = acts on raw feeling or suppresses it; 3 = names it; 5 = turns it into a clear action.
**Example:** a founder envies a peer's podcast. The envy says she wants a voice in her industry, so she pitches herself to two podcasts.
**Quote:** "anger shows up when a boundary has been crossed that we didn't know was there anger says you went too far" (T108)

## 20. The Believable Story Ladder
**Sources:** believable reframes and building a new self [M14]; Not Yet [M09].
**What it is:** replacing a harsh self-story not with a grand affirmation but with the next believable step toward a better one.
**Steps:** 1) Write the current story ("I'm terrible with money"). 2) Write the ideal story you do not believe yet. 3) Write a rung between them you can believe today ("I am willing to learn to be strong with money"). 4) Act on that rung this week. 5) Climb a rung when it feels true.
**When to use:** affirmations feel fake; the owner's self-talk is harsh.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = harsh story unexamined; 3 = believable rung written; 5 = acting on it and climbing.
**Example:** an owner who thinks "I'm a fraud" writes "I have delivered for 40 clients, and I'm still learning", then books a skill course.

## 21. The Accountability Mirror
**Sources:** [M01]; owning your part [M13].
**What it is:** blunt written truths about where you are and what you will do, put where you see them daily.
**Steps:** 1) Write three honest truths about what you avoid and why. 2) Write one commitment per truth, small and specific. 3) Put them where you will see them each morning. 4) Tick each day it is kept. 5) Replace a note when it is done.
**When to use:** the system exists, but the owner keeps excusing themselves.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = excuses unexamined; 3 = truths written; 5 = reviewed daily with commitments kept most days.
**Example:** an owner writes "I avoid sales calls because I fear rejection" and commits to five calls before email each weekday.
**Limit:** honest, not cruel. If the notes turn into self-hate, stop and use Not Yet or the Believable Story Ladder instead.

## 22. The Cookie Jar
**Sources:** [M01]; earned confidence [M11].
**What it is:** a written list of hard things you have already done and wins you earned, read when you want to quit.
**Steps:** 1) List ten hard things you got through, in life or business. 2) Add one line on what each took. 3) Keep it on your phone. 4) Read it before a hard call or when you want to stop. 5) Add new entries monthly.
**When to use:** self-doubt, impostor feelings, or a wobble before a big step.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = no list; 3 = list written; 5 = read and added to regularly.
**Example:** before a bank meeting, a founder reads that she rebuilt the business after a flood and that she won a contract against two bigger firms.
**Quote:** "I think what you're trying to develop as a human being confidence then is a midpoint between you know egotism and delusion and on the other hand sort of self-loathing and crippling self-doubt" (T105)

## 23. The Defined Push
**Sources:** the 40 percent rule and callousing the mind [M01]; Hard Thing Rule [M12]; limits from [M03].
**What it is:** a bounded period of chosen extra effort on one hard target, with a start, an end, and checks for health, instead of a permanent all-out mode.
**Steps:** 1) Pick one target that a push would move. 2) Set the length (two to six weeks). 3) Set the daily hard action. 4) Set guardrails: sleep, family time, a rest day. 5) Review at the end and return to a normal pace.
**When to use:** a launch, a sales drought, a deadline, or a restart after drift, when the plan is sound and the owner is healthy.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = endless grind or no push; 3 = push with no end date; 5 = bounded push with guardrails kept and a review held.
**Example:** a roofer facing a slow quarter does 30 days of 20 door-knocks a day, keeps Sundays off, and reviews leads at day 30.
**Quote:** "it's all stick all stick no carrot think about that" (T070)
**Wrong answer when:** the owner is exhausted, not sleeping, numb or hopeless, or the plan itself is broken. Then pushing harder makes it worse; hand off to Psychology and Mental Health or Business.

## 24. Goal Hierarchy
**Sources:** [M12]; definite purpose [M04].
**What it is:** one top-level goal that gives meaning to many smaller ones. Low-level goals are means and can be swapped when they fail; the top goal holds.
**Steps:** 1) Write the top goal in one line (what the work is ultimately for). 2) List mid-level goals that serve it. 3) List current low-level tasks. 4) Cross out tasks that serve nothing above them. 5) When a task fails, swap it, not the top goal.
**When to use:** the owner switches direction every few months, or quits a whole venture because one tactic failed.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = no top goal; 3 = top goal written but tasks unlinked; 5 = all main tasks trace to the top goal.
**Example:** a founder's top goal is helping small farms sell direct. When the market stall fails, she switches to a delivery box, not to a new industry.

## 25. Deliberate Practice Loop
**Sources:** [M12]; growth strategies [M09].
**What it is:** improving a skill with a specific stretch goal, full focus, fast and detailed feedback, and reflection before trying again.
**Steps:** 1) Pick one weak spot in one skill. 2) Set a precise goal for the session. 3) Work on it with full attention. 4) Get feedback right away (recording, coach, numbers). 5) Reflect, adjust, repeat.
**When to use:** the owner has done something for years without getting better (pitching, writing, negotiating).
**Score (guidance):** 1 = repetition with no goal or feedback; 3 = some feedback; 5 = all four parts in place weekly.
**Example:** a founder records every sales call, reviews one objection a week with a mentor, and rewrites the answer.
**Quote:** "the first thing when you're doing truly deliberate practice is that it's extremely intentional it's problem-solving something in particular" (T106)

## 26. The Hard Thing Rule
**Sources:** [M12]; Choose Your Struggle [M13].
**What it is:** everyone does one hard thing that needs practice, chooses it themselves, and does not quit until a natural stopping point (a season, a term, a set date).
**Steps:** 1) Choose the hard thing. 2) Set the natural stopping point before you start. 3) Do not quit before it, even on bad weeks. 4) At the stopping point, decide freely to continue or switch.
**When to use:** the owner quits at the first plateau, or wants to build persistence in a team or family business.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = quits mid-stretch; 3 = stopping point set; 5 = reached and then a free choice made.
**Example:** an owner commits to a weekly newsletter until the end of the quarter, then decides based on replies and sales.

## 27. Meaning Check
**Sources:** [M03]; purpose as a grit asset [M12]; good values [M13].
**What it is:** naming what the work is for: a task to finish, people served, a stance worth taking. Happiness follows from that; it cannot be aimed at.
**Steps:** 1) Ask who the work serves and what would be lost if it stopped. 2) Write a one-line why. 3) Connect this week's hardest task to it. 4) Cut one thing that serves no part of it. 5) Revisit the why each quarter.
**When to use:** the owner is efficient but empty, or the drive has gone after years in the business.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = cannot say why; 3 = vague why; 5 = one-line why tied to people served and used to choose work.
**Example:** a burned-out accountant names the family businesses she keeps alive as her why, and drops two corporate clients that do not fit it.
**Quote:** "the question was not just survival but there had to be a why of survival the question was survival for what" (T073)

## 28. Choose Your Stance (the last freedom)
**Sources:** [M03]; amor fati and will [M10]; [M02].
**What it is:** for a loss that cannot be undone, choosing the attitude you take toward it and what you will make of it. If the cause can be removed, remove it first; suffering is not a virtue.
**Steps:** 1) Ask: can this be changed? If yes, change it. 2) If no, name what is gone. 3) Choose the stance you want to take (what you want to be able to say later about how you met it). 4) Find one deed or person the loss can serve.
**When to use:** a business closing, a partner leaving, a health or family blow that hits the work.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = stuck or denying; 3 = accepted but adrift; 5 = a chosen stance and a next deed.
**Example:** after closing her first shop, an owner decides to use what she learned to help two new owners avoid the same lease mistake, and starts a smaller online version.
**Quote:** "if we cannot change a situation we have always the last freedom to change our attitude to that situation" (T072)
**Hand-off:** grief that does not lift, or signs of depression, go to Psychology and Mental Health.

## 29. Definite Major Purpose
**Sources:** [M04]; goal hierarchy [M12].
**What it is:** one written statement of what you want, by when, and what you will give in return, read often and backed by a plan.
**Steps:** 1) Write what you want and the date. 2) Write what you will give (service, skill, effort). 3) Write the first steps of a plan. 4) Read it daily or weekly. 5) When the plan fails, change the plan, not the purpose.
**When to use:** the owner drifts between ideas or has no clear target.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = no written target; 3 = written, not reviewed; 5 = reviewed weekly and backed by a working plan.
**Example:** a cleaner writes: 20 commercial contracts in 18 months, in return for the most reliable service in town, and starts with five local offices.
**Quote:** "on page two of your notebook write down a clear statement of precisely what you intend to give in return for that which you desire from life" (T074)
**Limit:** keep the writing and planning; drop Hill's claim that belief alone attracts results.

## 30. Mastermind
**Sources:** [M04]; long-term people [M08].
**What it is:** two or more people meeting regularly in harmony toward clear goals, lending experience and judgment and holding each other to commitments.
**Steps:** 1) Find two to four peers at a similar stage. 2) Agree a rhythm (weekly or every two weeks). 3) Each shares one goal, one result and one problem. 4) Others give experience, not lectures. 5) Commitments are checked next time.
**When to use:** the owner is isolated, plans fade with nobody watching, or the owner's own story goes unchallenged.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = no peers; 3 = informal chats; 5 = regular meetings with commitments tracked.
**Example:** three local owners meet for 45 minutes every other Monday; one fixes her overdue invoicing after the others share their methods.
**Quote:** "it consists of two or more people who work in perfect harmony for the attainment of a definite purpose" (T074)

## 31. The Enough Number
**Sources:** [M07]; know what you want and what is enough [M11]; happiness and desire [M08].
**What it is:** deciding in advance what enough looks like (income, hours, size, savings) so the goalposts stop moving and ambition serves your life rather than eating it.
**Steps:** 1) Write the income, hours and lifestyle that would be enough. 2) Write what you would do with time once there. 3) Check the current plan against it: does this move get you there, or move the target? 4) Decide what you will refuse. 5) Revisit once a year, not at every milestone.
**When to use:** the owner keeps raising the target, compares with peers, or feels behind despite success.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = no defined enough; 3 = written, not used; 5 = used to turn down moves that do not serve it.
**Example:** a dental practice owner writes that two sites and four days a week is enough, and turns down a third lease.
**Quote:** "if your expectations rise faster than your income, you're never going to be happy with your money." (T022)
**Hand-off:** the cash plan and reserve itself to Business.

## 32. Money Story Audit
**Sources:** money beliefs [M14]; fear and the rat race, ask how not whether [M06]; behavior over knowledge [M07].
**What it is:** surfacing the beliefs about money learned growing up (money is bad, rich people are greedy, I will lose it, I do not deserve it) that quietly drive pricing, spending and risk.
**Steps:** 1) Write what your family said and did about money. 2) List three beliefs you still carry. 3) For each, note one business decision it shapes (underpricing, hoarding cash, overspending to look successful). 4) Test each belief against facts. 5) Write a believable replacement and one action that follows from it.
**When to use:** the owner underprices, cannot ask for payment, spends to impress, or freezes on any risk.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = beliefs invisible; 3 = beliefs named; 5 = one decision changed because of it.
**Example:** a contractor raised to see debt as shameful refuses all credit, even a cheap equipment loan that would pay back in six months. He separates the belief from the numbers and asks his accountant.
**Quote:** "I think the most valuable financial skill that anybody can have is not needing to impress other people." (T022)

## 33. Long-Term Games
**Sources:** [M08]; compounding and staying in the game [M07]; long movie [M12].
**What it is:** choosing work, people and habits you would happily repeat for ten years or more, because compounding only pays those who stay in long enough.
**Steps:** 1) List your main projects and partners. 2) Ask of each: would I play this for ten years? 3) Ask: does any move risk knocking me out of the game? 4) Drop short-term games that cost long-term trust. 5) Set your expectations to years, not months.
**When to use:** the owner chases quick wins, jumps between ideas, or is tempted by a shortcut that risks reputation.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = all short-term; 3 = mixed; 5 = main bets and partners pass the ten-year test.
**Example:** an agency owner turns down a lucrative client who wants misleading ads, protecting relationships with the clients who have stayed for years.
**Quote:** "So it might be 10 to 30 years. It's not going to be two years. There's no get-rich quick schemes." (T024)
