# Frameworks: Sales department

Built from the 25 Sales books in BOOKS.md (S01 to S28, retired ids skipped), merged where books teach the same idea, most useful first. Scoring guides are guidance, not rules from the books.

Quote policy: verbatim quotes appear only from the author talks and public-domain texts listed in talks.md, always with a T id. Text of copyrighted books is never quoted. Everything else is paraphrase cited by book id.

## Gap Analysis (Current State, Future State, Gap)
**Sources:** [S11], with the same logic in [S01] and the doctor model in [S12] (synthesis of S01, S11, S12).
**What it is.** A discovery map of where the buyer is, where they want to be, and what the distance costs them.
**Parts.** Current state: facts, problems, impact, root cause, kept separate. Future state: new facts, desired outcomes (the opposite of today's impact), emotions, possible solutions across the root causes. The gap: size, cost, and urgency of moving.
**In the author's words.** "impact is where the motivation to buy exists" (T085)
**When to use.** Every discovery call for a considered purchase.
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: you know their industry only. 3: you know their problem and roughly what it costs. 5: the buyer has stated the cost, root cause, and desired outcome in their own words.
**Example.** A bookkeeping firm learns a client spends ten owner hours a month on books and missed a tax deadline, so the fee is compared against that, not cheaper software.

## SPIN Questioning
**Sources:** [S01].
**What it is.** A question sequence that grows an implied complaint into an explicit need.
**Parts.** Situation, Problem, Implication, Need-payoff, in a broad order rather than a fixed script. Stay on the problem and its consequences before any solution. Whether questions are open or closed matters less than their type and order.
**In the author's words.** "top salespeople didn't talk about solutions until very late in the sales process" (T075)
**When to use.** Larger sales where buyers do not yet feel the cost of the problem.
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: mostly Situation questions. 3: Problems found but no Implications. 5: buyer describes consequences and states the value of a fix.
**Example.** A commercial cleaner asks what happens when an audit finds a hygiene lapse; the client manager realizes the risk is to a major contract, not just tidiness.

## Value Equation
**Sources:** [S07].
**What it is.** A way to raise perceived value without cutting price.
**Parts.** Up: dream outcome and perceived likelihood of success. Down: time delay and effort or sacrifice.
**When to use.** Low close rates across reps, price objections, designing packages.
**Scoring (guidance).** Rate each of the four levers 1 to 5 from the buyer's view. Fix the lowest first.
**Example.** A personal trainer adds weekly check-ins and meal templates, cutting effort and raising likelihood, and moves from hourly sessions to a higher priced twelve-week package.

## Grand Slam Offer
**Sources:** [S07].
**What it is.** A method to build an offer that is hard to compare on price.
**Parts.** List every obstacle the buyer faces; turn each into a solution; pick delivery vehicles; trim costly, low value items; stack the rest; add enhancers.
**When to use.** Commodity pressure, new product launches, service businesses.
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: same as competitors at a lower price. 3: some unique extras. 5: buyer cannot find a like-for-like comparison.
**Example.** A roofer bundles inspection report, financing help, insurance claim support, and a workmanship guarantee instead of quoting per square meter.

## Money Model (Attraction, Upsell, Downsell, Continuity)
**Sources:** [S28], with offer building in [S07] and option framing in [S19].
**What it is.** A planned sequence of offers so each new customer pays back what it cost to win and serve them, ideally within 30 days.
**Parts.** Attraction offer to start the relationship; upsell right after the yes; downsell when the buyer says no to price, instead of a discount on the main offer; continuity offer for recurring revenue. Check 30-day gross profit per customer against the cost to acquire and serve them.
**When to use.** One-product businesses, rising acquisition costs, buyers who walk away at the price. Check delivery capacity before stacking offers.
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: one offer, one price, nothing after. 3: an upsell or payment plan exists but is not offered every time. 5: every buyer hears a planned next offer, every "no" hears a planned downsell, and 30-day profit covers acquisition.
**Example.** A dental clinic runs a low-cost first visit, offers a cleaning plan at checkout, a payment plan to those who decline full treatment, and a yearly membership.

## Tactical Empathy Toolkit (Mirroring, Labeling, Accusation Audit)
**Sources:** [S03], with listening roots in [S04, S25] and reading people in [S27].
**What it is.** Techniques to show understanding so the other side relaxes and reveals more.
**Parts.** Mirror the last words; label the emotion; open with an audit of their likely negative thoughts, or state your best guess of their view and let them correct it; use a calm, slow voice; pause.
**In the author's words.** "here's what I think you're thinking here's how I think you're approaching this here's what I think you're wanting out of this" (T077)
**When to use.** Tense price talks, angry clients, guarded prospects.
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: you defend and explain. 3: you label once but rush. 5: the other side says "that's right" and adds new information.
**Example.** A contractor opens with "you probably think we're going to blow the budget like the last firm," and the client explains exactly what went wrong before.

## Calibrated Questions
**Sources:** [S03], close cousins in [S01, S11].
**What it is.** Open "how" and "what" questions that ask the other side to solve your problem.
**Parts.** Avoid "why" (feels accusatory); ask "how am I supposed to do that," "what would need to be true."
**When to use.** Pushback on price or terms, uncovering decision process.
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: yes or no questions. 3: open questions but self-focused. 5: the other side volunteers constraints and options.
**Example.** Asked for a 30 percent discount, a print shop owner asks how they are supposed to hit the deadline at that rate; the buyer offers a longer timeline instead.

## Three Tens and the Action Threshold (Certainty)
**Sources:** [S15], related belief ideas in [S21, S23].
**What it is.** Buyers act when certain about the product, the seller, and the company, or when the step to act is small enough.
**Parts.** Rate the buyer's certainty on each; find the lowest; address it. Or lower the action threshold: a smaller first step, a trial, a guarantee. Treat "let me think about it" as low certainty, not a dead deal.
**In the author's words.** "What no is it's almost a euphemism for saying what no means I am NOT certain That's all it means" (T090)
**When to use.** One-call closes, consumer sales, diagnosing hesitation. Use for diagnosis, not pressure.
**Scoring (guidance).** Ask the buyer to rate each 1 to 10, or estimate it. Below 7 on any is the blocker.
**Example.** A home security buyer doubts the company's support, so the rep shows local service reviews and a 30-day cancel option.

## Trust Equation
**Sources:** [S25], with related ideas in [S04, S13].
**What it is.** Trust equals credibility plus reliability plus intimacy, divided by self-orientation.
**Parts.** Credibility (what you know and say), reliability (what you do), intimacy (emotional safety to share), self-orientation (focus on yourself). Green says intimacy and self-orientation weigh most.
**In the author's words.** "credibility + reliability + intimacy all divided by self-orientation" (T098)
**When to use.** Professional services, long-term accounts, lost deals with no clear reason.
**Scoring (guidance).** Score each part 1 to 5; self-orientation counts against. Check intimacy and self-orientation first, since sellers tend to over-invest in credibility.
**Example.** An agency losing pitches cuts its credentials section and spends the meeting on the client's worries.

## Balanced Prospecting and the Law of Replacement
**Sources:** [S10], with time blocking in [S18, S09] and persistence in [S21].
**What it is.** Daily multi-channel prospecting to keep the pipeline full.
**Parts.** Phone, email, social, in person, referrals; daily blocks in prime hours, even 15-minute bursts, done before email and admin; a replacement rate above your close and loss rate. For broader time problems, hand off to Productivity.
**When to use.** Thin pipeline, revenue swings, reps who wait for leads.
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: prospect only when desperate. 3: weekly but one channel. 5: daily blocks across channels with tracked replacement.
**Example.** A staffing agency owner blocks 8 to 9 each morning for calls and LinkedIn messages and stops feast-or-famine months.

## Commercial Teaching (Teach, Tailor, Take Control)
**Sources:** [S02], with problem finding in [S13].
**What it is.** Lead with an insight that reframes the buyer's business and points to your unique strength.
**Parts.** Teach a reframe; tailor to each stakeholder; take control of money and process. The company, not each rep, should build the insight.
**When to use.** Commoditized markets, buyers who think they already know what they need. Building insight is easier with marketing help.
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: you lead with product features. 3: insight is interesting but not tied to your strength. 5: the insight leads only to you.
**Example.** A packaging supplier shows a food brand that damaged goods in transit cost more than packaging, then shows its tested boxes.

## Advances and Micro-Commitments
**Sources:** [S01], with the same rule in [S22, S18].
**What it is.** Judge every call by whether the buyer committed to a specific step that moves the deal.
**Parts.** Plan the best and minimum acceptable advance before each call; ask for it; put it on both calendars. Define each step by buyer action, not by CRM stage names.
**When to use.** Long cycles, forecasting, coaching reps.
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: "send me info." 3: a vague follow-up. 5: a dated meeting with the decision maker or a signed step.
**Example.** A solar installer ends each visit by booking the roof survey with both homeowners present.

## Stalled Deal Diagnosis (No Impact or No Certainty)
**Sources:** synthesis of [S11, S15, S02].
**What it is.** Decide why a deal stalled: the buyer does not feel enough impact to change, or feels it but is not certain enough to act.
**Parts.** No impact: go back to the gap, quantify the cost, and challenge the status quo [S11, S02]. No certainty: find the weakest of the three tens, recommend one path, and lower the action threshold [S15].
**When to use.** Any stalled deal, "let me think about it," "no decision" losses.
**Scoring (guidance).** If the buyer cannot state the cost of the problem, score it no impact; if they state it and still delay, score it no certainty.
**Example.** A clinic agrees scheduling losses cost two staff days a week but keeps asking for demos, so the seller recommends one plan and a one-site start.

## Principles of Persuasion
**Sources:** [S05], with liking in [S04].
**What it is.** Seven triggers that make agreement easier.
**Parts.** Reciprocity (go first), commitment and consistency, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity, unity.
**In the author's words.** "the key is to go first with reciprocity it's the opposite of the usual commercial exchange sequence" (T078)
**When to use.** Proposals, follow-ups, websites, renewals.
**Scoring (guidance).** Mark which principles appear truthfully in your sales process. 1 to 2: thin. 5 or more: well supported. Fake use scores zero.
**Example.** A dentist adds before-and-after photos of local patients and a short wait-list note when slots are truly limited.

## Frame Control and the STRONG Method
**Sources:** [S06], with absence and saying less in [S26].
**What it is.** Keep the meeting on your terms and pitch in a tight sequence.
**Parts.** Set the frame, tell the story, reveal the intrigue, offer the prize, nail the hookpoint, get a decision. Be new, be brief, and be willing to walk.
**In the author's words.** "if you're not new and you're not dangerous you get ignored" (T080)
**When to use.** Investor and partner pitches, powerful buyers. Assumes you have real leverage.
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: you chase and over-explain. 3: you hold the agenda. 5: the buyer is qualifying themselves to you.
**Example.** A founder pitching a distributor sets a firm end time, explains the two other regions in talks, and asks for a decision on a pilot.

## Reading Power in the Account
**Sources:** [S26], with reading people in [S27] and frame in [S06].
**What it is.** A lens for the politics around a deal: who holds power, who feels threatened, and what each person stands to gain.
**Parts.** Never outshine your contact or their boss; frame requests around their self-interest; say less than you know; offer options that all work for you; learn what each person needs or fears; stop once you have the yes. Read and defend; do not deceive.
**In the author's words.** "the laws are never meant to apply to every situation" (T099)
**When to use.** Big accounts with internal rivals, or buyers playing games. Not a default stance with small, repeat clients [S25].
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: you only know your contact. 3: you know who decides but not what each person gains. 5: you know each stakeholder's interest and fear, and your plan makes your contact look good.
**Example.** An IT consultant lets the client's operations head present the joint plan to the CEO.

## Confirm Their Self-Opinion
**Sources:** [S27], with open-minded framing in [S19] and making people feel important in [S04].
**What it is.** People resist being told they are wrong, so frame the change as fitting the smart, independent person they believe they are.
**Parts.** Never attack the buyer's past decision; credit their judgment; ask if they would be open to an idea; let the conclusion be theirs.
**When to use.** Replacing a competitor the buyer chose, defensive committees, prospects who feel judged.
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: you tell them their current choice is bad. 3: neutral framing. 5: the buyer argues for the change themselves.
**Example.** A seller says the buyer's old system was the right call five years ago and asks how open they would be to seeing what changed.

## Subtract the Emotion (Rosy Scenario Check)
**Sources:** [S27], with forecasting by buyer commitment in [S01, S22].
**What it is.** Strip hope and fear out of your own deal decisions before you act on them.
**Parts.** Pause before reacting to a win or a loss; list the worst plausible case next to the best; count only buyer actions as evidence.
**When to use.** Forecasts, hiring, a deal you badly want.
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: forecast by feel. 3: you note risks but keep the number. 5: each forecast deal has a dated buyer commitment and a named risk.
**Example.** A founder sure of a big contract finds no dated next step and no meeting with the signer, and moves it out of this quarter.

## Ackerman Bargaining
**Sources:** [S03].
**What it is.** A planned concession pattern for price negotiation.
**Parts.** Set your target; start well short of it; make shrinking concessions; use empathy and calibrated questions between moves; finish with a precise number and a small non-cash item.
**When to use.** Buying from suppliers, negotiating one-off contracts. Less suited to ongoing relationships where trust matters more.
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: you split the difference. 3: you planned concessions but not the end. 5: you closed near target with the relationship intact.
**Example.** A café owner buying an espresso machine lands a precise figure plus free servicing, rather than a round discount.

## The Sales Story
**Sources:** [S18], related to [S02, S13].
**What it is.** A client-focused message in place of a product pitch.
**Parts.** The issues buyers face that you solve; the outcomes you deliver; why you are different.
**When to use.** Cold calls, networking, website copy, sales decks.
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: starts with your company history. 3: lists benefits. 5: buyers nod and say "that's us."
**Example.** A manufacturer's rep opens with "plant managers tell us their biggest headache is unplanned downtime," not with the firm's founding year.

## Strategic Target Account List
**Sources:** [S18], with the Ideal Customer Profile in [S09] and market selection in [S07].
**What it is.** A short, chosen list of accounts you proactively pursue.
**Parts.** Define the ideal customer from your best current customers by profit and retention, with written red and green flags [S09]; pick a list small enough to work; plan touches; block time; review monthly. Weinberg says the usual failure is time spent, not skill.
**In the author's words.** "it's not because of selling skills it's because the sales people don't spend any time working on it" (T092)
**When to use.** Firms that rely on referrals and inbound and want new logos.
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: "anyone who needs us." 3: a list with no plan. 5: a list of fit accounts with weekly time blocked.
**Example.** An HVAC firm targets 40 property managers in its city and calls five a week.

## Qualify Out Early
**Sources:** [S09, S03], with walking away in [S06] and market fit in [S07]. Voss: a slow no costs more than a fast one.
**What it is.** Drop poor-fit or unwinnable deals fast.
**Parts.** Set must-have criteria from your ICP red and green flags [S09]; test them in the first call; walk away politely and quickly [S03, S06].
**When to use.** Packed pipelines, long cycles, low win rates.
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: chase everything. 3: drop deals only after they die. 5: clear exit rules used in the first two calls.
**Example.** A web agency declines projects under its minimum budget and win rate rises.

## Specialized Sales Roles
**Sources:** [S09], with [S08] and the hunter versus caretaker split in [S18].
**What it is.** Split prospecting, closing, and account management into separate roles.
**Parts.** Outbound prospecting, inbound response, closers who carry quota, post-sales account management and success.
**When to use.** Teams of roughly four or more reps with proven demand. Assumes a team.
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: everyone does everything. 3: closers have some prospecting help. 5: each role has its own metric.
**Example.** A growing IT firm hires one appointment setter so its two closers stop skipping outreach.

## Cold Calling 2.0
**Sources:** [S09].
**What it is.** Outbound that asks senior contacts for a referral to the right person.
**Parts.** Short, plain, phone-readable email; ask who handles the topic; follow up using the referrer's name; route to discovery. Count call conversations, not dials.
**When to use.** B2B outbound into mid-size companies. Inboxes are now crowded, so personalize.
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: long pitch emails. 3: short but generic. 5: short, relevant, with referrals coming back.
**Example.** A logistics broker emails operations VPs asking who manages carrier contracts and gets passed to the right buyer.

## Prospecting Turnarounds (Reflex, Brush-off, Objection)
**Sources:** [S22, S10].
**What it is.** Prepared short responses to early resistance.
**Parts.** Identify whether it is a reflex, brush-off, or real objection; use an anchor statement; disrupt the expected pattern; ask again with confidence. For red herrings early in a call: pause, acknowledge, then ignore or save it.
**When to use.** Cold calls, door to door, first meetings.
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: you hang up at "not interested." 3: you have one response. 5: practiced turnarounds for your top five brush-offs.
**Example.** When told "we already have a vendor," a rep says that is exactly why she called, and asks for a short comparison meeting.

## Objection Handling Sequence
**Sources:** synthesis of [S03, S15, S21, S22, S23].
**What it is.** A shared pattern for later-stage objections.
**Parts.** Prevent what you can in discovery; pause; acknowledge or label; clarify the real concern; address it or loop back to certainty; ask again.
**In the author's words.** "if you're talking about it in Discovery it's not an objection it's a conversation" (T096)
**When to use.** "Too expensive," "need to think," "need to talk to my partner."
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: you argue or discount. 3: you clarify but forget to ask again. 5: you find the real concern and secure a next step.
**Example.** "Too expensive" turns out to mean cash flow; a landscaping firm offers monthly billing at the same total.

## Hiring Formula
**Sources:** [S08], with the hunter profile in [S18] and character reading in [S27].
**What it is.** Hire salespeople to a profile of traits that predict success in your business.
**Parts.** Define about ten traits; score candidates; test coachability with a role play and feedback; for new-business roles, look for a track record of winning new deals; track which traits predicted results. Closing polish in the interview predicted poorly at HubSpot.
**In the author's words.** "the ideal sales hiring formula is different for every company" (T082)
**When to use.** First and later sales hires. Small teams use judgment more than statistics.
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: hire on charm. 3: structured interview. 5: structured scoring plus a coachability test and review after six months.
**Example.** A wholesaler gives each candidate feedback mid role play and hires the one who improved most.

## Metrics-Driven Coaching
**Sources:** [S08], with practice of one skill at a time in [S12].
**What it is.** Use funnel data to find each rep's single biggest skill gap and coach only that.
**Parts.** Track conversion by stage; start as high in the funnel as the problem appears; compare to team; pick one or two skills; coach and recheck.
**When to use.** Varied rep performance, a manager with three or more reps.
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: generic pep talks. 3: data reviewed, many fixes at once. 5: one skill per rep per month, measured.
**Example.** A rep with strong meeting volume but weak conversion from demo to proposal works only on discovery for a month.

## Constructive Tension
**Sources:** [S02], with [S11].
**What it is.** Respectfully making the buyer uncomfortable with staying put.
**Parts.** Break down the current state before building up the new one; show a cost they missed; hold the point without apologizing; offer a way out.
**When to use.** "We're fine," status quo deals.
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: you agree to avoid tension. 3: you raise it but retreat. 5: the buyer re-evaluates the status quo.
**Example.** A security consultant shows a retailer its shrinkage is above peers and lets the silence work.

## The New ABCs (Attunement, Buoyancy, Clarity)
**Sources:** [S13].
**What it is.** Three qualities for modern, honest selling.
**Parts.** Attunement: take the buyer's perspective, their thoughts and interests, not only feelings. Buoyancy: manage rejection, using questioning self-talk ("can I do this?") before calls. Clarity: help buyers find the right problem.
**When to use.** Founders who dislike selling, building a sales culture.
**Scoring (guidance).** Rate each 1 to 5 for the seller; train the lowest.
**Example.** A software engineer turned founder prepares by writing down the buyer's goals first and asks one reframing question per call.

## Guarantees and Risk Reversal
**Sources:** [S07], with lowering the action threshold in [S15] and downsells in [S28].
**What it is.** Reduce buyer risk so the decision feels safe.
**Parts.** Unconditional, conditional, and other guarantee types; pilots; phased or small starts; trials with conditions.
**When to use.** Hesitant buyers, new firms without proof. Check your delivery margin first.
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: buyer carries all risk. 3: a weak, vague guarantee. 5: a specific guarantee you can afford to honor.
**Example.** A cleaning company offers to reclean free within 24 hours if a checklist item is missed.

## Price Anchoring and Option Framing
**Sources:** synthesis of [S05, S07, S19, S28], with controlling the options in [S26].
**What it is.** Order and frame options so the right one looks clearly sensible.
**Parts.** Show a high-value option first (contrast); offer a small number of clear choices; recommend one.
**When to use.** Proposals and pricing conversations.
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: one price, take it or leave it, or a long menu. 3: options but no recommendation. 5: few options, a clear recommendation, value anchored.
**Example.** An event planner shows a full-service package first, then two simpler ones, and recommends the middle.

## Commitment and Consistency Ladder
**Sources:** [S05], with early agreement in [S04] and advances in [S01].
**What it is.** Secure small, real agreements that lead toward the big one.
**Parts.** Agree on the problem; agree on criteria; agree on a small step; then the purchase. Spoken or written commitments hold best.
**In the author's words.** "it is the desire to be consistent with what we have already said or done especially in public" (T078)
**When to use.** Long or nervous decisions.
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: one big ask at the end. 3: some agreements, unstated. 5: written agreements at each stage.
**Example.** A consultant sends a recap after discovery and asks the client to confirm the goals in reply.

## Low-Pressure Language Patterns
**Sources:** [S19], related to [S04, S13].
**What it is.** Short phrasing, prepared in advance, that lowers defenses and invites a decision.
**Parts.** Openers that allow a no; open-minded framing; imagining the outcome; assumptive timing of next steps.
**In the author's words.** "if you say to somebody I'm not sure if it's for you their little voice kicks in" (T094)
**When to use.** Calls, follow-ups, closing moments.
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: pushy or vague asks. 3: some good phrasing. 5: every call ends with a clear, easy next step.
**Example.** A gym owner asks whether a new member would be open to trying the program for a month, rather than pushing an annual contract.

## Compensation Aligned to Customer Success
**Sources:** [S08].
**What it is.** Pay plans that reward reps for customers who stay and succeed.
**Parts.** Commission tied to retention or paid in stages; clawbacks for early churn.
**When to use.** Subscription or repeat-revenue businesses with rep churn or bad-fit deals.
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: paid on signing only. 3: some delayed payment. 5: pay reflects retained revenue.
**Example.** A managed IT firm pays half of commission at signing and half after month four.

## Self-Concept and Rejection Reframe
**Sources:** [S12], with [S13, S17, S22].
**What it is.** Address the seller's beliefs and fears that limit activity.
**Parts.** Separate rejection from identity; set activity goals; build habits; track progress.
**When to use.** Call reluctance, founders avoiding the ask.
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: avoids calls. 3: calls but avoids asking for money. 5: asks every time with calm.
**Example.** A founder sets a daily goal of three asks, regardless of outcome, and tracks asks rather than wins.

## Personal Brand and Give Value First
**Sources:** [S14], with reciprocity in [S05], credibility in [S25], and reputation in [S26].
**What it is.** Make buyers come to you by being known for useful help.
**Parts.** Share expertise publicly; help before selling; ask for referrals.
**When to use.** Local and relationship markets, solo professionals. Large scale content strategy belongs to Marketing.
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: unknown. 3: known in a small circle. 5: buyers arrive referred and pre-sold.
**Example.** An accountant runs a free quarterly tax session for local trades and gets most new clients from attendees.

## Keep Serving After the Sale
**Sources:** [S23], with continuity offers in [S28] and reliability in [S25].
**What it is.** Treat the first sale as the start of the account, not the end.
**Parts.** Deliver and check results; plan the next offer; stay in touch on a schedule; ask for referrals once results show.
**When to use.** Repeat-purchase and service businesses.
**Scoring (guidance).** 1: no contact after delivery. 3: occasional check-ins. 5: scheduled reviews and a planned next offer for every client.
**Example.** A landscaper books a spring review with every autumn client and offers a yearly care plan.
