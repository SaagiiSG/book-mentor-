# Books: Sales department

Built from the 25 Sales books in BOOKS.md (S01 to S28, retired ids skipped), one entry each, in id order.

Quote policy: verbatim quotes appear only from the author talks and public-domain texts listed in talks.md, always with a T id. Text of copyrighted books is never quoted. Everything else is paraphrase cited by book id.

## [S01] SPIN Selling, Neil Rackham (1988)

**Core idea.** Large, complex sales succeed through better questions, not better closing. Rackham, a research psychologist, led a study of 35,000 sales calls over 12 years and found that small-sale tactics often hurt big sales. The buyer has to discover, through questions, that a problem is serious enough to pay to fix.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **SPIN questions:** Situation (facts and context), Problem (difficulties and dissatisfactions), Implication (the consequences and costs of those problems), Need-payoff (the value of solving them, stated by the buyer). They come in a broad sequence, not a rigid script.
- **Type and sequence, not open versus closed:** whether questions were open or closed did not predict success; their kind and order did.
- **Implied versus explicit needs:** an implied need is a complaint; an explicit need is a clear want or intent.
- **Advances versus continuations:** a call succeeds only if the buyer commits to a concrete next step that moves the sale forward. A friendly "keep in touch" is not progress.
- **Features, Advantages, Benefits:** in Rackham's sense, a benefit is only a benefit when it meets an explicit need the buyer has voiced.
- **Closing techniques backfire in large sales:** pressure closes reduce success as deal size grows.

**In the author's words:** "top salespeople didn't talk about solutions until very late in the sales process" (T075). "very skilled sales people prevent objections they don't handle objections" (T075).

**Use it when.** Deals are considered purchases with several stakeholders, or when reps pitch too early and hear "send me something."

**Watch out.** Too many Situation questions bore senior buyers. Written for large B2B field sales; transactional sales need less depth.

## [S02] The Challenger Sale, Matthew Dixon and Brent Adamson (2011)

**Core idea.** In complex B2B sales, top performers are less often the friendly relationship builders and more often reps who teach the customer something new about their business, tailor it, and keep control of the sale.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Five rep profiles:** Hard Worker, Relationship Builder, Lone Wolf, Reactive Problem Solver, Challenger. Challengers were overrepresented among stars in complex sales; Relationship Builders finished far behind.
- **Teach, Tailor, Take Control:** offer a new insight, fit it to each stakeholder, and be willing to push back on price and process.
- **Commercial Teaching:** the insight should lead the customer back to a strength only you have. Building and codifying the insight is the company's job, not each rep's.
- **Constructive tension:** break down the status quo before building up the new state, so the pain of staying put looks larger than the pain of change.

**In the author's words:** "they don't want a sales person to come in and ask them what's keeping them up at night they want a sales person who will come in and show them what should be keeping them up at night" (T076).

**Use it when.** Buyers see you as a commodity, deals stall at "we're fine as is," or reps get pushed around on price.

**Watch out.** It is easy to misread as permission to be aggressive; Dixon says Challengers are empathetic and build relationships on business value. Building real insight takes marketing support that small firms may lack.

## [S03] Never Split the Difference, Chris Voss and Tahl Raz (2016)

**Core idea.** Negotiation is driven by emotion, so the skilled negotiator uses deliberate empathy to uncover what the other side feels and needs, then shapes the deal. Voss draws on his years as an FBI hostage negotiator.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Tactical empathy:** show you understand the other side's view without agreeing to it.
- **Mirroring and labeling:** repeat their last few words to draw them out; name the emotion you sense ("it seems like...") to defuse it.
- **Accusation audit:** say the worst things they might think about you before they do. Voss also states his best guess of their view and lets them correct it.
- **Calibrated questions:** open "how" and "what" questions that make the other side solve your problem.
- **"That's right" over "you're right":** the first signals real agreement; the second is a brush-off.
- **Ackerman bargaining:** a planned sequence of shrinking concessions ending in a precise, non-round number, often with a non-monetary add.
- **Calm voice and fast exits:** a calm tone settles both sides; spot a bad deal early and leave. Voss distrusts people who open with "win-win," but does not reject mutual gain.

**In the author's words:** "it's not a sin to not get the deal it's a sin to take a long time to not get the deal or it's a sin to take a long time to get a bad deal" (T077).

**Use it when.** Price talks, contract terms, collections, partner disputes, or any conversation where emotions are running high.

**Watch out.** Hostage stories do not map neatly to repeat business relationships. Overused techniques feel scripted; experienced buyers notice mirroring.

## [S04] How to Win Friends and Influence People, Dale Carnegie (1936)

**Core idea.** People act from their own interests and their need to feel important. If you show genuine interest, avoid criticism, and let others own ideas, they cooperate far more than when you argue or push.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Do not criticize, condemn, or complain:** criticism triggers defense, not change.
- **Become genuinely interested in other people:** listen, remember names, talk about their interests.
- **Arouse an eager want:** frame the request around what the other person wants.
- **Avoid arguments:** you cannot win one; even a win costs goodwill.
- **Let the idea be theirs:** people support what they feel they helped create.

**Use it when.** Building rapport with prospects, repairing an account, managing reps, or handling a stubborn stakeholder.

**Watch out.** Dated examples and tone. Used cynically, it reads as flattery. It is a rapport book, not a sales process.

## [S05] Influence, Robert Cialdini (1984, expanded 2021)

**Core idea.** People use mental shortcuts to decide quickly, and a small set of principles reliably triggers agreement. Knowing them helps you persuade ethically and defend against misuse.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **The principles of persuasion:** Reciprocity, Commitment and Consistency, Social Proof, Authority, Liking, Scarcity, and Unity (added in the 2021 edition).
- **Go first with reciprocity:** give something useful, ideally unexpected, before you ask.
- **Consistency is about what people said or did, especially in public:** a small spoken or written commitment makes a larger consistent yes more likely.
- **Rejection then retreat:** a large request first raises agreement to a smaller one.

**In the author's words:** "the key is to go first with reciprocity it's the opposite of the usual commercial exchange sequence" (T078). "it is the desire to be consistent with what we have already said or done especially in public" (T078).

**Use it when.** Designing proposals, pricing pages, follow-ups, case studies, and any step where trust or urgency is missing.

**Watch out.** Some studies in this tradition face replication questions. False scarcity or fake proof destroys trust and can be illegal.

## [S06] Pitch Anything, Oren Klaff (2011)

**Core idea.** Pitches are won by controlling the frame, the underlying assumptions of the meeting, and by keeping status. The buyer's quick, survival-minded brain filters out boring, complex, or needy pitches, and you get only about twenty minutes of real attention.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Frame control:** the stronger frame absorbs the weaker one. Named frames include power, time, analyst, intrigue, prize, and moral authority frames.
- **Prizing:** position yourself as the prize the buyer must qualify for, not the other way around. Their money is a commodity.
- **STRONG method:** Set the frame, Tell the story, Reveal the intrigue, Offer the prize, Nail the hookpoint, Get a decision.
- **Neediness kills deals:** supplicating signals weakness; people chase what moves away from them, so be willing to walk away early.

**In the author's words:** "if you're not new and you're not dangerous you get ignored" (T080).

**Use it when.** Pitching investors, large partners, or senior buyers who hold power, or when you are being treated as a vendor to squeeze.

**Watch out.** The "croc brain" model is a practical metaphor, not biology; Klaff says he is not a biologist. Built for investment banking pitches; the style can read as arrogant in relationship-driven sales.

## [S07] $100M Offers, Alex Hormozi (2021)

**Core idea.** Most sales problems are offer problems. Build an offer so good that people feel foolish saying no, sell it to a market that is starving for it, and charge more, not less.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Value Equation:** perceived value rises with the dream outcome and the perceived likelihood of achieving it, and falls with time delay and effort or sacrifice.
- **Grand Slam Offer:** list every problem the buyer faces, turn each into a solution, then trim and stack the most valuable, low cost ones.
- **Starving crowd:** pick a market with real pain, money to spend, easy to reach, and growing. Market beats offer beats persuasion.
- **Charge what it is worth:** higher prices fund better delivery, which creates better results and more demand.
- **Enhancers:** scarcity, urgency, bonuses, guarantees, and naming make a good offer convert better.

**In the author's words:** "ideally you want the offer to have as much leverage as possible" (T081).

**Use it when.** Close rate is low across all reps, buyers keep comparing you on price, or you sell services, coaching, or local offers.

**Watch out.** Tilted to services and info products. Strong guarantees can sink a small firm if delivery is not reliable. Claims rest on his own experience.

## [S08] The Sales Acceleration Formula, Mark Roberge (2015)

**Core idea.** Drawing on his time at HubSpot, Roberge treats building a sales team like an engineering problem: hire to a data-backed profile, train to a repeatable process, coach on metrics, and generate demand predictably.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Hiring formula:** score every candidate on about ten defined criteria, then check which predicted results. At HubSpot the predictors were coachability (the top one), curiosity, intelligence, work ethic, and prior success. Closing, convincing, and objection-handling skill in the interview correlated negatively with success there.
- **Training formula:** document the buyer journey and sales process, then certify reps on it, rather than having new reps shadow a top rep.
- **Metrics-driven coaching:** read the funnel from the top, find each rep's one or two weakest skills, and coach on those alone.
- **Compensation aligned to customer success:** pay for retained customers, not just signed ones.
- **Demand generation formula:** inbound content, lead scoring, and a marketing and sales agreement on lead volume and quality.

**In the author's words:** "the ideal sales hiring formula is different for every company" (T082). "convincing objection handling those were actually negatively correlated with success in our model" (T082).

**Use it when.** Moving from a founder-led sale to your first sales hires, or when rep performance varies wildly.

**Watch out.** Assumes a SaaS company with inbound volume and enough reps to have data. A team of two will not have statistically useful hiring data.

## [S09] Predictable Revenue, Aaron Ross and Marylou Tyler (2011)

**Core idea.** Revenue becomes predictable when you specialize sales roles and build a dedicated outbound engine, rather than asking closers to also prospect. Based on Ross's work building outbound at Salesforce.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Specialization:** four core roles: outbound prospecting, inbound lead response that pre-qualifies, closers who carry quota, and a separate post-sales group for account management and success.
- **Cold Calling 2.0:** short emails to senior people asking who the right contact is, rather than cold pitching. Success is measured in call conversations, not dials.
- **Seeds, nets, and spears:** three lead types: referral and word of mouth, marketing driven, and targeted outbound. Spears give the most control over filling the pipeline.
- **Ideal Customer Profile:** define who you sell to, with red and green flags, before scaling outreach.
- **Closers should not prospect:** prospecting work gets skipped by busy closers.

**In the author's words:** "If you have a predictable way to generate qualified leads, you will generate predictable revenue." (T083). "people don't care what you do. They only care about what you can do for them." (T083).

**Use it when.** You have a proven offer, need more qualified meetings, and can afford at least one dedicated prospector.

**Watch out.** The email tactics are dated and inboxes are now saturated. Specialization assumes team size; a solo founder plays every role.

## [S10] Fanatical Prospecting, Jeb Blount (2015)

**Core idea.** Empty pipelines are the root cause of most sales failure, and the cure is disciplined, daily, multi-channel prospecting. Prospecting is the job, not a side task.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Law of Replacement:** you must add prospects faster than deals close or die out.
- **The 30-Day Rule:** the prospecting you do this month pays off over the next ninety days.
- **Golden Hours and time blocking:** protect selling hours for selling and prospect first thing in the morning, before email. Blocks can be short power bursts of 15 minutes; daily is what matters.
- **Balanced prospecting:** combine phone, email, social, in person, and referrals rather than relying on one.
- **Turnarounds:** sort the response into reflex, brush-off, or objection, then use an anchor statement, disrupt the expected pattern, and ask again with confidence.
- **Universal Law of Need:** the more desperate you are for a deal, the less likely you are to get it.

**In the author's words:** "the cumulative impact of every single day prospecting is exponential and in terms of filling your pipeline up and filling your bank account up" (T084).

**Use it when.** The pipeline is thin, revenue swings month to month, or reps "don't have time" to prospect.

**Watch out.** Heavy on phone and volume, which some markets now resist. Activity without targeting burns lists and brand.

## [S11] Gap Selling, Keenan (2018)

**Core idea.** People buy change, not products. Selling means diagnosing the gap between the buyer's current state and desired future state, and making the size and cost of that gap clear.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Current state, future state, the gap:** today's facts, problems, impact, and root cause; then the future facts, desired outcomes (the opposite of today's impact), emotions, and possible solutions across the root causes.
- **Separate problem, root cause, and impact:** Keenan says this is where Gap Selling differs from SPIN, which blends them.
- **Root cause:** you cannot solve a problem directly, only the root causes you are able to fix.
- **Impact is the motivation to buy:** a big problem with a small impact will not close, and "we want to do better" is not a buying reason.
- **Deal-review test:** the reason a buyer must buy should start with what they are currently struggling with, quantified, not with features.
- **Discovery drives everything:** without it you are an order taker.

**In the author's words:** "impact is where the motivation to buy exists" (T085).

**Use it when.** Reps demo too early, deals die without a reason, or buyers say "it's nice but not a priority."

**Watch out.** Largely a sharp restatement of consultative selling. The author's confrontational tone and disdain for rapport do not suit every market.

## [S12] The Psychology of Selling, Brian Tracy (1985)

**Core idea.** Sales results follow the seller's self-concept, confidence, and habits. Tracy combines a mindset program with a practical selling process and closing methods.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Self-concept:** you rarely outperform your own belief about what you can earn and do.
- **Fear of rejection and failure:** the main barriers to selling; reframe rejection as not personal.
- **Seven-step process:** prospect, build rapport and trust, identify needs accurately, present, answer objections, close, get resales and referrals. Identifying needs is the hinge.
- **Doctor model:** examine and diagnose before you prescribe.
- **Listening builds trust:** listen intently, pause before replying, ask "how do you mean," and feed back what you heard.
- **Goal setting:** written income and activity goals drive daily behavior.
- **Closing techniques:** a set of classic closes for asking for the order.

**In the author's words:** "in a sales conversation the last thing that comes up is the prescription the course of treatment the recommendation to purchase the product or service selling out a sequence kills the sale" (T086).

**Use it when.** A rep or founder avoids asking for the sale, call reluctance is the bottleneck, or a new seller needs a simple process.

**Watch out.** Motivational and dated. Some closes feel manipulative to modern buyers. Not a process for complex deals.

## [S13] To Sell Is Human, Daniel H. Pink (2012)

**Core idea.** Nearly everyone now spends much of their work moving other people, and the end of information asymmetry means sellers can no longer win by knowing more than buyers. Honesty and service matter more than ever.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Non-sales selling:** persuading, influencing, and convincing are part of most jobs.
- **From buyer beware to seller beware:** buyers can check everything, so deception gets found out.
- **The new ABCs:** Attunement (taking the other's perspective, which Pink separates from empathy for their feelings), Buoyancy (staying afloat amid rejection), Clarity (moving from problem solving to problem finding). They replace "always be closing."
- **Interrogative self-talk:** asking yourself "can I do this?" works better than telling yourself you can, because it prompts you to rehearse reasons.
- **Ambiverts:** Pink cites research that people between introvert and extrovert often sell best.
- **Pitch formats:** short forms such as the one-word, question, and Pixar pitch.

**In the author's words:** "you're more useful if I don't know what my problem is or if I'm wrong about my problem and so this move from problem-solving to problem finding is clarity" (T087).

**Use it when.** A founder or technical team "hates selling," or you need a humane sales culture.

**Watch out.** Light on process and pipeline. Some cited research is single studies with modest effects.

## [S14] The Little Red Book of Selling, Jeffrey Gitomer (2004)

**Core idea.** People dislike being sold but love to buy. Sell by being someone people want to buy from: give value first, ask about them, and build reputation.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Why people buy matters more than how to sell:** understand buying motives.
- **Personal brand:** your reputation should bring buyers to you.
- **Give value first:** share useful ideas before asking for anything.
- **Questions over pitches:** ask questions that make buyers think about themselves.

**Use it when.** Small firms selling through relationships, referrals, and local networks.

**Watch out.** Anecdotal and rah-rah in style. Little structure for multi-stakeholder deals.

## [S15] Way of the Wolf, Jordan Belfort (2017)

**Core idea.** The Straight Line System holds that every sale moves from open to close along a line, and the seller's job is to keep the buyer on it while transferring certainty.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Three Tens:** the buyer needs certainty about the product, about you, and about your company.
- **Action threshold:** a buyer says yes either when certainty rises or when the bar to act is lowered, for example by starting small.
- **A no means not certain:** "let me think about it" signals low certainty, not a dead sale; it often takes several nos to reach a yes.
- **The first four seconds:** you must come across as sharp, enthusiastic, and an expert in your field.
- **Tonality:** how you sound shapes whether you seem certain.
- **Looping:** when the buyer objects, return to build more certainty rather than argue the objection.

**In the author's words:** "What no is it's almost a euphemism for saying what no means I am NOT certain That's all it means" (T090). "You have four seconds to establish three crucial things or else you're done" (T090).

**Use it when.** High-volume phone sales or one-call closes, or when diagnosing why buyers lack confidence.

**Watch out.** The author was convicted of securities fraud tied to high-pressure selling. Use the certainty model, not the pressure. Unsuitable for complex B2B.

## [S17] The Greatest Salesman in the World, Og Mandino (1968)

**Core idea.** A parable about a camel boy who becomes a great merchant by following ten scrolls of principles about habit, persistence, love, gratitude, and self-mastery. Its lesson is that character and daily discipline make a seller.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Habits make the person:** repeat good habits until they become automatic.
- **Persistence:** keep going past the point others quit.
- **Treat each day as a fresh start.**
- **Goodwill toward others:** genuine care for buyers sustains you.

**In the author's words:** "Never treat time as if you had an unlimited supply. None of us have a contract with life." (T091).

**Use it when.** Motivation and resilience are the problem, not skill.

**Watch out.** No tactics, no process, no data. Inspirational only.

## [S18] New Sales. Simplified., Mike Weinberg (2012)

**Core idea.** Getting new business is not complicated. Pick target accounts, build a compelling sales story about the client's issues, and proactively attack those accounts with time-blocked effort.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Strategic target account list:** a short, chosen list of accounts you will pursue.
- **The sales story:** a client-focused message built on the issues you solve, the outcomes you deliver, and your differentiators, used in place of a product pitch.
- **Sales weapons:** story, proactive calls, email, social, networking, presentations, and discovery meetings.
- **Proactive phone call:** a prepared cold call is still a core weapon.
- **Why new business efforts fail:** mostly time, not skill. Reps drift into caretaking favorite accounts, and managers let them. Weinberg separates hunters from account caretakers he calls zookeepers, and warns against putting caretakers in hunting roles.

**In the author's words:** "it's not because of selling skills it's because the sales people don't spend any time working on it" (T092). "management loses sight of the salesperson's primary job which is to create new revenue not to play territory caretaker" (T092).

**Use it when.** A small or mid-size firm relies on referrals and needs a plan to win new logos.

**Watch out.** Plain and practical but light on pricing and negotiation. Written from a B2B services and distribution lens.

## [S19] Exactly What to Say, Phil M. Jones (2017)

**Core idea.** Small changes in wording change how people respond. The book offers short phrase patterns, prepared in advance, that open minds, lower pressure, and invite decisions.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Low pressure openers:** framing that the offer may not be for them, which disarms resistance and triggers curiosity.
- **Open-minded framing:** asking how open-minded someone would be to an idea, since most people like to see themselves that way.
- **Imagination prompts:** inviting the buyer to picture the result, or how they would feel.
- **Option framing:** presenting a few clear choices, such as two appointment days, to make deciding easier.
- **Assumptive next steps:** asking when, not whether, to schedule or start.

**In the author's words:** "we know the worst time to think about the thing you're gonna say is in the moment when you're saying it" (T094).

**Use it when.** Your offer is fine but follow-ups, calls, and closes stall on awkward wording.

**Watch out.** Phrases work only on top of a good offer and real discovery. Repeated mechanically they sound canned.

## [S21] Sell or Be Sold, Grant Cardone (2011)

**Core idea.** Everyone sells, and the seller who believes most, persists longest, and refuses to accept a first no usually wins. Selling is framed as a life skill.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Agree with the buyer:** avoid arguing; agree, then guide.
- **Commitment and belief:** total commitment to your product and to selling.
- **Price is rarely the real issue:** most price objections hide something else; the buyer knew the price before they called.
- **Persistence and follow-up:** most sales happen after several contacts.
- **Dominate, do not compete:** the same work and rejection go into small and large targets, so aim bigger.

**In the author's words:** "every business person has to sell you show me one business that doesn't have to sell" (T095).

**Use it when.** A founder or rep is passive, gives up early, or fears asking.

**Watch out.** Aggressive style. Persistence can become pressure and harm reputation, especially in small, connected markets.

## [S22] Objections, Jeb Blount (2018)

**Core idea.** Objections are a natural part of selling, and handling them is mostly about managing your own emotions and asking confidently. Most objections can be anticipated, prevented in discovery, or prepared for.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Four places objections occur:** prospecting objections, red herrings, next-step (micro-commitment) objections, and buying-commitment objections. Each needs a different technique.
- **Prospecting turnarounds:** short prepared responses to reflexes, brush-offs, and real objections that anchor, disrupt the expected pattern, and ask again.
- **Red herrings:** pause, acknowledge, then ignore or save the remark for later, so it does not burn the call.
- **Micro-commitments:** define each next step that moves a deal and get it on both calendars; most stalls trace to a missing next step.
- **The ledge:** a deliberate short pause, which Blount calls a magic quarter of a second, before responding to an objection (detail of steps uncertain).
- **Prevent in discovery, ask with confidence:** concerns raised in discovery are conversation; relaxed, assumptive confidence matters most at the close.

**In the author's words:** "if you're talking about it in Discovery it's not an objection it's a conversation" (T096).

**Use it when.** Reps freeze at "not interested" or "too expensive," prospecting calls die in the first seconds, or deals stall without a next step.

**Watch out.** Assumes phone heavy outreach. Scripts help only when practiced.

## [S23] Secrets of Closing the Sale, Zig Ziglar (1984)

**Core idea.** Closing is the natural end of a well-run sale, and the seller's integrity, belief, and ability to transfer feeling matter as much as technique. Ziglar offers many classic closes and stories.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Selling is a transfer of feeling:** if you believe in it, the buyer can sense it.
- **Selling means serving:** pride in the profession shapes whether you close the close ones.
- **The sale is a foot in the door:** keep serving the account after the sale, or a competitor takes it.
- **Many named closes:** such as the alternate choice and the weighing of pros and cons.
- **Questions guide the sale.**

**In the author's words:** "We go make the sale, we don't service the account and somebody else then ends up getting the business." (T097).

**Use it when.** Retail, home services, and direct sales where the close happens in one or two meetings.

**Watch out.** Many closes are dated and feel manipulative to informed buyers. Rackham [S01] warns against close-heavy selling in large deals.

## [S25] The Trusted Advisor, David Maister, Charles Green and Robert Galford (2000)

**Core idea.** In professional services, clients buy from people they trust. Trust can be understood, measured, and built deliberately, and it comes from focusing on the client more than yourself.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Trust Equation:** trustworthiness equals credibility plus reliability plus intimacy, divided by self-orientation. Green says intimacy (emotional safety to share) is the strongest factor, followed closely by self-orientation; the rational pair, credibility and reliability, matter less than people assume.
- **Trust building process:** engage, listen, frame the issue, envision a better state, commit to steps.
- **Earn the right to advise:** listen so the other person feels heard before you recommend.
- **Low self-orientation:** the more you focus on yourself, the less you are trusted. Candor and plain words such as "I don't know" lower it.
- **Emotional middle:** buyers shortlist rationally, decide emotionally, then justify rationally.

**In the author's words:** "I think of candor as the antidote to self-orientation." (T098).

**Use it when.** You sell consulting, agency, legal, accounting, or other expertise, or want long-term accounts.

**Watch out.** Slow by design. Assumes repeat relationships, not one-time transactions.

## [S26] The 48 Laws of Power, Robert Greene (1998)

**Core idea.** Power is a social game as old as royal courts, and people who pretend not to play it get played. Greene distills 48 laws from history, each with examples of people who broke or kept it and a reversal for when the opposite works. Greene says power begins with self-control, not domination.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Never outshine the master (Law 1):** make those above you feel secure. In a sale, never make your contact or their boss look foolish.
- **Say less than necessary (Law 4):** talking too much makes you look common and leaks information.
- **Win through actions, not argument (Law 9):** demonstrate; a won argument breeds resentment.
- **Appeal to self-interest, not mercy or gratitude (Law 13):** frame every request around what the other side gains.
- **Use absence to raise value (Law 16):** being always available lowers your worth.
- **Control the options (Law 31):** offer choices that all work for you.
- **Find each person's thumbscrew (Law 33):** learn the need or insecurity that moves them.
- **Know when to stop (Law 47):** after the win, do not push past what you aimed for.

**In the author's words:** "the laws are never meant to apply to every situation" (T099).

**Use it when.** Navigating politics in a big account, reading power plays in a negotiation, or spotting these moves used on you.

**Watch out.** Amoral by design. Concealment and manipulation destroy trust in repeat and referral selling [S25, S13]. Greene himself says context is everything, so treat it as a lens for reading situations and defending yourself, not a script.

## [S27] The Laws of Human Nature, Robert Greene (2018)

**Core idea.** People are driven by deep, mostly unconscious forces they rarely see in themselves. Greene sets out 18 laws of human nature so you can master your own reactions first and then read and influence others more calmly.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Irrationality:** emotion steers decisions, including buying. Leave a gap before reacting, and strip the emotion out of big decisions, especially the habit of imagining only the best case.
- **Narcissism and empathy:** everyone is somewhere on the self-absorption spectrum; turn attention outward to read others.
- **Role-playing:** people wear masks; read tone, gestures, and other nonverbal cues.
- **Compulsive behavior:** judge character by repeated patterns, not by words or first impressions.
- **Covetousness:** people want what is scarce, absent, or out of reach.
- **Shortsightedness:** people overreact to the immediate; step back and look further ahead.
- **Defensiveness:** people resist being told; soften resistance by confirming their good opinion of themselves.
- **Conformity:** groups spread emotions and habits by contagion, so culture shapes behavior.

**In the author's words:** "we humans are deeply irrational and what I mean by that is that we are governed by our emotions more than anything else" (T100).

**Use it when.** Reading a hard buyer or committee, managing your reactions in tense deals, judging character, or checking a forecast for wishful thinking.

**Watch out.** Long, literary, and built on historical stories rather than sales cases. Figures such as the share of behavior that is unconscious are estimates Greene repeats, not measurements. Not a sales process.

## [S28] $100M Money Models, Alex Hormozi (2025)

**Core idea.** A business should not rely on one offer. A money model is a planned sequence of offers built so that a new customer pays back the cost to acquire and serve them quickly, ideally within the first 30 days, which lets the business fund its own growth.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Four offer types:** attraction offers that bring customers in, upsell offers that add more value right after a yes, downsell offers that save the sale when the buyer says no, and continuity offers that create recurring revenue.
- **Upsells:** offer the next thing the buyer needs right after the yes, or show a premium option first as an anchor.
- **Downsells:** payment plans, trials with conditions, or a lighter version, used instead of cutting the main price.
- **Cash first, then scale:** grow ad spend and outreach only when 30-day gross profit per customer covers what it costs to get and serve them.

**Use it when.** Getting customers costs too much, the business sells one product once, buyers who say no to price walk away with nothing, or a sales team closes the first deal and stops.

**Watch out.** Drawn mainly from the author's own companies. Stacked offers can feel like a trap if delivery is weak, and fast-cash thinking can clash with advisory selling [S25]. Recent and little tested.
