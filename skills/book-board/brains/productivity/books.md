# Books: Productivity department

Built from the 25 Productivity books (P01 to P25) in BOOKS.md. One entry per book, in id order, written in our own words.

## [P01] Getting Things Done, David Allen (2001, rev. 2015)

**Core idea.** Your head is for having ideas, not holding them. Every unfinished commitment is an "open loop" that drains attention until it is captured in a trusted system outside your head. With everything captured and sorted, you can choose what to do with a clear mind.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **The five steps (Capture, Clarify, Organize, Reflect, Engage):** collect everything, decide what each item means, put it in the right list, review the lists, then act.
- **Next action:** every project needs a single, visible, physical next step. Vague items stall; concrete next actions move.
- **Two-minute rule:** if an item takes less than about two minutes, do it now instead of tracking it.
- **Weekly Review:** a regular session to empty inboxes, update project lists, and get current. Allen treats it as the habit that keeps the system trusted.

**Use it when:** the owner feels overwhelmed, drops balls, or carries everything in their head and email inbox.

**Watch out:** strong on handling inputs, weak on deciding what matters. It can turn into an elaborate list hobby. Context lists (calls, at computer) feel dated when everything happens on one device.

## [P02] Deep Work, Cal Newport (2016)

**Core idea.** The ability to focus without distraction on cognitively hard work is becoming rare and valuable. Shallow, logistical work expands to fill the day unless you deliberately protect blocks for deep work.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Deep work versus shallow work:** deep work creates new value and is hard to copy; shallow work is logistical and easy to replicate.
- **Four depth philosophies:** monastic (eliminate most shallow work), bimodal (alternate deep periods with normal ones), rhythmic (a daily deep block), journalistic (fit deep work in whenever possible, for experienced people).
- **Attention residue:** switching tasks leaves part of your attention on the previous one, so fragmented days lower quality.
- **Drain the shallows:** schedule every minute of the workday in blocks, ask how long a task would take a smart new graduate to learn, and agree a shallow work budget.

**Use it when:** the owner's most valuable work (strategy, writing, product, hard selling prep) keeps getting crowded out by messages and meetings.

**Watch out:** written from an academic's schedule. Owners in service businesses, retail or sales cannot go monastic; rhythmic or bimodal fits them better.

## [P03] Atomic Habits, James Clear (2018)

**Core idea.** Results come from systems, and systems are made of small habits that compound. Change is easier when you build identity ("I am the kind of person who...") and design the environment so good habits are easy and bad ones are hard.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Four Laws of Behavior Change:** make it obvious, attractive, easy, satisfying. Invert each to break a bad habit.
- **Identity-based habits:** focus on who you want to become; each repetition is a vote for that identity.
- **Habit stacking and implementation intentions:** tie a new habit to an existing one, and state when and where you will do it.
- **Two-minute rule (Clear's version):** scale a new habit down to a version that takes two minutes to start.
- **Environment design:** change cues in your surroundings rather than relying on self-control.
- **Systems over goals and the Plateau of Latent Potential:** goals set direction, systems produce progress, and results often lag effort before they show.

**Use it when:** the owner knows what to do but cannot do it consistently (prospecting daily, bookkeeping weekly, exercise, writing).

**Watch out:** synthesizes research from others; some of the habit-science claims are simplified. Habits cannot fix a wrong strategy.

## [P04] The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People, Stephen R. Covey (1989)

**Core idea.** Effectiveness starts with character and principles, not tactics. You move from dependence to independence to interdependence by mastering yourself first, then working well with others.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Be Proactive, Circle of Influence versus Circle of Concern:** spend energy on what you can affect.
- **Begin with the End in Mind:** write a personal (or company) mission statement and judge activity against it.
- **Put First Things First, the Time Management Matrix:** urgent/important quadrants; effective people invest in Quadrant II (important, not urgent).
- **Weekly planning by roles and goals:** plan the week around your roles, scheduling Quadrant II priorities first.
- **Stewardship delegation versus gofer delegation:** delegate results with guidelines, resources, accountability and consequences, not step-by-step tasks.

**Use it when:** the owner is stuck firefighting, needs a value-based way to set priorities, or delegates badly.

**Watch out:** long and moralistic; some readers find it slow. Weekly role planning can be heavy for a solo owner.

## [P05] Essentialism, Greg McKeown (2014)

**Core idea.** Less, but better. Most things are trivial and a few are vital, so the job is to identify the vital few, eliminate the rest, and build systems that make execution easy.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Explore, Eliminate, Execute:** discern what matters, cut what does not, then remove friction from doing it.
- **Trade-offs:** you cannot have it all; ask which problem you want rather than pretending there is none.
- **Extreme criteria (90 percent rule):** if something is not a clear yes, treat it as a no.
- **The graceful no:** separate the decision from the relationship and decline kindly.

**Use it when:** the owner says yes to everything, runs too many initiatives, or feels busy but not effective.

**Watch out:** assumes you have the power to say no. Early-stage owners who need every customer may have to say yes more often than the book suggests.

## [P06] The ONE Thing, Gary Keller and Jay Papasan (2013)

**Core idea.** Extraordinary results come from narrowing focus to the single most important thing at each level, then protecting time for it. Success is sequential: one domino knocks over the next.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **The Focusing Question:** what is the one thing I can do such that by doing it everything else becomes easier or unnecessary?
- **The six lies:** the book argues against beliefs such as everything matters equally, multitasking works, and willpower is always available.
- **Goal setting to the now:** cascade from a someday goal down to what you do right now.
- **Time blocking:** block time for the one thing first, protect it, and block planning and rest too. The authors recommend a large daily block (about four hours).
- **Four thieves:** inability to say no, fear of chaos, poor health habits, and an environment that does not support your goals.

**Use it when:** the owner has many good options and needs to pick one lever for this quarter or this week.

**Watch out:** the four-hour daily block is unrealistic for many operators. Single focus can neglect necessary maintenance work.

## [P07] Eat That Frog!, Brian Tracy (2001)

**Core idea.** Procrastination is beaten by doing your most important, most dreaded task first. Plan in advance, rank tasks by consequence, and work on one thing until it is done.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Eat the frog:** start the day with the biggest, most important task.
- **ABCDE method:** label tasks by consequence (A must do, B should do, C nice to do, D delegate, E eliminate) and never do a B while an A is undone.
- **80/20 rule:** a small share of tasks produces most of the value.
- **Consider the consequences:** a task's importance is measured by the long-term effect of doing it or not doing it.

**Use it when:** the owner procrastinates on the hard call, the proposal, or the difficult hire.

**Watch out:** motivational and simple. It does not address why a task is avoided (ambiguity, fear) as well as P25 or P10.

## [P08] The 4-Hour Workweek, Tim Ferriss (2007)

**Core idea.** Time and mobility are the real wealth. By eliminating low-value work, automating income, and removing yourself from operations, you can work far less and live on your own terms.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **DEAL:** Definition, Elimination, Automation, Liberation.
- **Pareto and Parkinson:** cut to the 20 percent that matters, and shorten deadlines so work shrinks to fit.
- **Low-information diet and batching:** consume less news and email, check messages at set times.
- **Outsourcing and virtual assistants:** hand off repetitive work, with clear rules for when assistants can act on their own.
- **The muse:** a small, automated business that funds your life without your daily involvement.

**Use it when:** the owner is the bottleneck, drowns in email, or wants a business that runs without them.

**Watch out:** written for an early-internet, low-cost outsourcing era; some tactics are dated or overpromise. Most real businesses need more than four hours.

## [P09] Make Time, Jake Knapp and John Zeratsky (2018)

**Core idea.** Busyness and endless apps are default settings that steal time. Instead of a rigid system, pick one daily Highlight, protect it, and run small experiments to see what works for you.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Highlight:** choose one thing each day that you want to look back on as done.
- **Laser:** defend focus against the "Busy Bandwagon" and "Infinity Pools" (endless feeds, email).
- **Energize:** move, eat, sleep and connect in ways that keep energy up.
- **Reflect:** each night, note what worked and adjust.

**Use it when:** the owner wants a light, flexible system and hates heavy planning tools.

**Watch out:** tactics are tested on the authors (design sprint background at Google Ventures), not studied broadly. Light on team and delegation issues.

## [P10] Indistractable, Nir Eyal (2019)

**Core idea.** Distraction starts inside: it is usually an escape from discomfort. Become indistractable by handling internal triggers, scheduling time for what you value, removing external triggers, and using precommitments.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Traction versus distraction:** traction moves you toward what you planned; distraction is anything else, even "productive" work done instead of the plan.
- **Master internal triggers:** notice the uncomfortable feeling, explore it, and wait (a short "surf the urge" delay of about ten minutes) before giving in.
- **Make time for traction:** timebox your calendar from your values, and sync the schedule with your manager or team.
- **Hack back external triggers:** cut notifications, meetings, and messages that do not serve you.
- **Prevent distraction with pacts:** effort pacts, price pacts and identity pacts.

**Use it when:** the owner plans well but gets pulled into phone, email, Slack or busywork.

**Watch out:** Eyal earlier wrote Hooked (M09) on building habit-forming products; some critics see tension. Places a lot of responsibility on the individual rather than the work culture.

## [P11] The Power of Habit, Charles Duhigg (2012)

**Core idea.** Habits run on a loop of cue, routine and reward, driven by craving. You change a habit by keeping the cue and reward and swapping the routine. Organizations have habits too, and keystone habits can trigger wider change.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **The habit loop:** cue, routine, reward, with craving as the engine.
- **Golden Rule of Habit Change:** keep the old cue and reward, insert a new routine.
- **Keystone habits:** a few habits that start chain reactions (the book's example is Alcoa's focus on worker safety).
- **Organizational habits and crises:** routines act as truces between groups; crises can open space to change them.

**Use it when:** a team behavior or the owner's own routine needs changing, or a company needs one cultural lever.

**Watch out:** journalism built on stories; some anecdotes are simplified and the science has moved on. Use the loop as a lens, not a law.

## [P12] Four Thousand Weeks, Oliver Burkeman (2021)

**Core idea.** A human life is about four thousand weeks, so you will never get everything done. Productivity techniques often make it worse by inviting more work. Accept finitude, choose what to neglect, and commit to a few things.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **The efficiency trap:** getting faster invites more demands; the inbox refills.
- **Choose what to neglect:** decide in advance what you will fail at, so failure is deliberate.
- **Fixed-volume productivity:** keep a short, closed list of active tasks and only add when one is done (detail of list sizes uncertain).
- **One big project at a time:** finish before starting the next.

**Use it when:** the owner is burning out chasing an endless list or feels guilty about everything undone.

**Watch out:** a philosophy, not a system. Pair it with an operating method like P18 or P01.

## [P13] The Effective Executive, Peter F. Drucker (1967)

**Core idea.** Effectiveness, doing the right things, can be learned. Knowledge workers must manage their time, focus on contribution, build on strengths, concentrate on first things, and make few but sound decisions.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Know thy time:** record where time actually goes, cut waste, then consolidate the rest into large uninterrupted blocks.
- **What can I contribute?:** focus on outward results, not effort or position.
- **Make strengths productive:** staff and organize around strengths rather than avoiding weaknesses.
- **First things first and posteriorities:** concentrate on one thing at a time and decide what to stop doing.
- **Effective decisions:** define the problem and the boundary conditions, consider what is right before what is acceptable, build in action and feedback.
- **Time wasters:** recurring crises, overstaffing, too many meetings, and poor information flow signal organizational problems.

**Use it when:** an owner moving from doer to manager needs to decide what only they should do.

**Watch out:** written for large-company executives of the 1960s. Language is dated but the core ideas hold for small teams.

## [P14] Buy Back Your Time, Dan Martell (2023)

**Core idea.** Founders get stuck doing low-value work themselves. Treat hiring and delegation as buying back time, then reinvest that time into the high-value, energizing work only you can do.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Buyback Principle:** hire to buy back time, not just to grow.
- **Buyback Rate:** a calculated hourly threshold; tasks you could pay someone less than this to do are candidates to hand off (formula detail: roughly a quarter of your hourly value, detail uncertain).
- **DRIP Matrix:** sort tasks by value and energy into Delegation, Replacement, Investment and Production quadrants.
- **Buyback Loop (Audit, Transfer, Fill):** audit your calendar, transfer tasks, fill freed time with high-value work.
- **Camcorder Method:** record yourself doing a task once, turning the recording into a playbook for the person who takes it over.
- **Replacement Ladder and the Perfect Week:** hand off roles in a rough order (admin first), and design an ideal weekly calendar.

**Use it when:** a founder of a growing small business is the bottleneck and can afford at least a part-time assistant.

**Watch out:** assumes cash to hire. Pre-revenue or thin-margin owners must adapt (cheaper help, automation, elimination first).

## [P15] Slow Productivity, Cal Newport (2024)

**Core idea.** Knowledge work has confused visible busyness with productivity. A sustainable alternative: do fewer things, work at a natural pace, and obsess over quality.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Pseudo-productivity:** using visible activity as a stand-in for real output.
- **Do fewer things:** limit missions, projects, and daily goals; each commitment carries an overhead tax of coordination.
- **Pull-based workload:** keep a holding tank of work and pull in only what you have capacity for, making the queue visible.
- **Work at a natural pace:** use longer timelines, seasonal variation, and lighter periods.
- **Obsess over quality:** raising your quality bar justifies doing less, and makes your work worth more.

**Use it when:** the owner or team is overloaded with projects and quality is slipping.

**Watch out:** many examples are writers, artists and academics. Owners with payroll and customers must adapt the pace advice.

## [P16] Hyperfocus, Chris Bailey (2018)

**Core idea.** Attention is limited and your attentional space is small. Use it in two modes: hyperfocus on one important task, and scatterfocus to let the mind wander and connect ideas.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Attentional space:** a small capacity for what you can hold in mind at once; overloading it lowers performance.
- **Four types of tasks:** sorted by whether they are productive and whether they are attractive (purposeful, necessary, distracting, unnecessary work).
- **Hyperfocus:** choose a task, remove distractions, focus, and pull attention back when it wanders.
- **Scatterfocus modes:** capture mode, problem-crunching mode, and habitual mode (mind wandering during simple tasks).

**Use it when:** the owner needs both execution focus and creative space for strategy.

**Watch out:** largely a synthesis of research and the author's experiments; practical but not deep.

## [P17] Building a Second Brain, Tiago Forte (2022)

**Core idea.** Use a digital system to capture and organize information so your ideas can be found and reused to create output. The goal is expression, not hoarding.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **CODE:** Capture, Organize, Distill, Express.
- **PARA:** organize by actionability into Projects, Areas, Resources, Archives.
- **Progressive Summarization:** highlight notes in layers so the key points surface over time.
- **Intermediate Packets:** build work in small reusable pieces (notes, drafts, outlines).

**Use it when:** the owner creates content, proposals, training or SOPs and keeps reinventing them.

**Watch out:** easy to over-engineer note systems and confuse collecting with doing. Less relevant for owners whose bottleneck is not knowledge reuse.

## [P18] The 12 Week Year, Brian P. Moran and Michael Lennington (2013)

**Core idea.** Annual goals create false comfort because deadlines feel far away. Treat each 12 weeks as a full year, with a tight plan, weekly scoring, and accountability, so urgency becomes constant.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **The 12 week year:** set a vision, then a 12-week plan of a few goals and the weekly tactics for each.
- **Weekly scorecard and execution score:** measure the share of planned tactics completed; the authors treat about 85 percent as a strong week.
- **Lead and lag indicators:** track actions you control, not just outcomes.
- **Performance time:** strategic blocks (about three hours, uninterrupted), buffer blocks for admin, and breakaway blocks for rest.
- **Weekly Accountability Meeting (WAM):** a small peer group reviews scores each week.

**Use it when:** the owner sets goals every January and drifts by March, or a team needs execution rhythm.

**Watch out:** can become a points game if tactics are wrong. Pair with good strategy from the Growth department.

## [P19] Mini Habits, Stephen Guise (2013)

**Core idea.** Make the daily requirement so small it is impossible to fail (one push-up, one sentence). Starting is the hardest part; once started, you often do more.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Stupidly small requirements:** set the bar far below your capacity.
- **Willpower over motivation:** rely on a tiny amount of willpower, which is more dependable than mood.
- **Bonus reps:** anything beyond the minimum is a bonus, never the new requirement.

**Use it when:** the owner keeps failing at ambitious routines (daily outreach, writing, exercise).

**Watch out:** based mainly on the author's experience. Tiny habits may never grow if the minimum becomes the ceiling.

## [P20] Willpower, Roy F. Baumeister and John Tierney (2011)

**Core idea.** Self-control works like a limited resource that tires with use, so smart people rely on routines, precommitment and monitoring rather than constant willpower.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Ego depletion:** self-control draws on a shared resource that can be depleted.
- **Decision fatigue:** many decisions in a row worsen later decisions.
- **Precommitment:** lock in choices in advance to remove later temptation.
- **Monitoring:** tracking behavior is one of the strongest supports for self-control.
- **Plans quiet unfinished goals:** making a concrete plan reduces the mental nagging of open tasks (linked to the Zeigarnik effect, discussed alongside P01).

**Use it when:** the owner makes poor late-day decisions or relies on grit for every routine.

**Watch out:** ego depletion has failed several large replication attempts and the glucose explanation is contested. Treat routines and precommitment as the useful takeaway, not the resource model.

## [P21] Flow, Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi (1990)

**Core idea.** People are happiest and perform best in flow: deep absorption in an activity with clear goals, immediate feedback, and a challenge matched to skill. You can shape work so flow happens more often.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Conditions for flow:** clear goals, immediate feedback, challenge balanced with skill.
- **Challenge-skill balance:** too hard creates anxiety, too easy creates boredom.
- **Transforming work:** redesign tasks to add goals, feedback and stretch.

**Use it when:** the owner or team finds work dull or anxious, or needs to design roles people can get absorbed in.

**Watch out:** academic and philosophical, not a how-to. Flow does not guarantee you are working on the right thing.

## [P22] The Compound Effect, Darren Hardy (2010)

**Core idea.** Small, smart choices repeated consistently over time produce large results. Most people quit because early results are invisible.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **The compound effect:** small choices plus consistency plus time.
- **Track everything:** writing down behavior reveals the true pattern.
- **Habits and routines:** rhythms carry you when motivation fails.

**Use it when:** the owner is impatient for results from a sound plan, or needs to restart a lapsed routine.

**Watch out:** motivational and anecdotal. Compounding only works if the activity is the right one.

## [P23] Peak, Anders Ericsson and Robert Pool (2016)

**Core idea.** Expert performance comes from deliberate practice, not innate talent or hours alone. Practice must target specific weaknesses, push beyond comfort, use feedback, and build mental representations.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Naive, purposeful and deliberate practice:** repetition alone plateaus; purposeful practice has goals, focus, feedback and stretch; deliberate practice adds expert-designed training.
- **Mental representations:** experts build rich internal models of their domain.
- **Feedback and coaching:** a teacher or clear signal shows what to fix.
- **The 10,000-hour rule is a misreading:** the number varies and hours without deliberate practice do not create expertise.

**Use it when:** the owner or staff need to get better at a skill fast (selling, pitching, estimating, managing).

**Watch out:** cleanest in fields with established training (music, chess, sport). Business skills need you to design your own feedback loops.

## [P24] Who Not How, Dan Sullivan and Benjamin Hardy (2020)

**Core idea.** When you want a result, ask "who can do this?" instead of "how do I do this?". Finding the right people expands what you can achieve and frees your time.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Who not How:** default to finding a capable person rather than learning every skill.
- **Impact Filter:** a one-page tool to define a project's purpose, importance, ideal outcome and success criteria before handing it to a Who.
- **Win-win collaboration:** good Whos must benefit too; pay well and respect their expertise.

**Use it when:** the owner is learning yet another skill themselves or delegating without a clear brief.

**Watch out:** assumes access to money and capable people, and comes partly from a coaching program. Can encourage avoiding skills the owner should actually learn.

## [P25] The Productivity Project, Chris Bailey (2016)

**Core idea.** Productivity is how much you intentionally accomplish, managed through three resources: time, attention and energy. Run experiments on yourself and keep what works.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Time, attention, energy:** manage all three, not just the calendar.
- **Rule of 3:** choose three outcomes for the day and three for the week.
- **Highest-impact tasks:** list tasks and identify the few that bring the most value.
- **Biological Prime Time:** track energy to find when you are sharpest and put important work there.
- **Procrastination triggers:** tasks are avoided when they are boring, frustrating, difficult, ambiguous, unstructured, or lacking meaning; fix the trigger.

**Use it when:** the owner wants a simple daily method and a way to understand why they avoid tasks.

**Watch out:** built on one person's year of experiments; results vary by person.
