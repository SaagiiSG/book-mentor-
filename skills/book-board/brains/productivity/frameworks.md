# Frameworks: Productivity department

Built from the 25 Productivity books (P01 to P25), most useful first. Names are the books' names; explanations are ours. Scores are guidance for the advisor, not from the books.

## The Focusing Question
**Sources:** [P06], related to Explore, Eliminate, Execute [P05] and Rule of 3 [P25].
**What it is:** asking which single action would make everything else easier or unnecessary, at the level of this year, this quarter, this week and right now.
**Parts:** 1) Pick the time horizon. 2) List candidate actions. 3) For each, ask what it unlocks. 4) Choose the one with the widest knock-on effect. 5) Block time for it first.
**When to use:** too many good options, scattered effort, an owner who starts many things and finishes few.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = the task unlocks nothing else; 3 = helps one other area; 5 = makes several other problems easier or removes them.
**Example:** a bakery owner choosing between a new website, a loyalty app and a wholesale deal picks the wholesale deal because it funds the next hire, which frees time for the rest.

## Time Management Matrix (Quadrant II)
**Sources:** [P04]; consequence thinking from [P07].
**What it is:** a two-by-two of urgent versus important. Effective people move time from urgent-not-important and not-urgent-not-important into important-not-urgent work (planning, prevention, relationships, capability).
**Parts:** Quadrant I crises; Quadrant II prevention and building; Quadrant III interruptions; Quadrant IV time wasters.
**When to use:** the owner lives in firefighting mode.
**Score (guidance):** rate a task 1 to 5 on long-term consequence; Quadrant II work that scores 4 or 5 goes on the calendar before Quadrant III work of any score.
**Example:** an agency owner who handles every client email moves two hours a week into writing an onboarding process, which cuts the emails.

## Know Thy Time (time audit)
**Sources:** [P13]; audit step of the Buyback Loop [P14]; energy tracking in [P25].
**What it is:** record where time actually goes before trying to manage it, then cut waste and consolidate what remains into large blocks.
**Steps:** 1) Log time for one to two weeks in small slots. 2) Mark each block by value and energy. 3) Ask of each activity: what happens if it is not done at all? 4) Delegate what others can do. 5) Consolidate the remaining discretionary time into long blocks.
**When to use:** first step for almost any "no time" complaint.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = owner guesses; 3 = one week logged; 5 = two weeks logged with value and energy tags.
**Example:** a contractor finds he spends nine hours a week driving to quote small jobs; he sets a minimum job size and quotes by video.

## DRIP Matrix
**Sources:** [P14].
**What it is:** a two-by-two of tasks by money value (high/low) and energy (lights you up/drains you).
**Parts:** Delegation (low value, draining), Replacement (high value, draining: hire someone to own it), Investment (low current value, energizing: time spent on the future), Production (high value, energizing: your zone).
**When to use:** deciding what to hand off and in what order.
**Score (guidance):** for each task score value 1 to 5 and energy 1 to 5; anything with value 3 or below and energy 2 or below goes first.
**Example:** a clinic owner keeps patient consults (production), hands billing to a bookkeeper (delegation), and plans to hire a practice manager (replacement).

## Buyback Rate
**Sources:** [P14], supported by Who Not How [P24].
**What it is:** a threshold price for your time; tasks you could pay someone less than this rate to do are candidates to hand off. Martell computes it from your hourly value (detail of the exact fraction uncertain; treat as roughly a quarter).
**Steps:** 1) Estimate your effective hourly value. 2) Set the buyback threshold below it. 3) List tasks below the threshold. 4) Hand off the most frequent ones first.
**When to use:** the owner refuses to hire because "I can do it cheaper myself".
**Score (guidance):** 1 = task worth far above your rate (keep); 5 = task worth far below it (hand off now).
**Example:** an owner earning the equivalent of 100 per hour stops doing 20-per-hour data entry and hires a part-time assistant.

## Who Not How and the Impact Filter
**Sources:** [P24]; delegation logic also in [P08], [P14].
**What it is:** asking "who can get this result?" instead of "how do I do this?", then briefing that person with a one-page Impact Filter (purpose, why it matters, ideal outcome, success criteria).
**Steps:** 1) Name the result. 2) Write the Impact Filter. 3) Find a capable Who (staff, contractor, partner). 4) Agree the win for them. 5) Step back and review results.
**When to use:** the owner is about to learn a new skill for a one-off need.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = core skill the owner must master; 3 = useful but someone else does it better; 5 = rare need, clear specialist exists.
**Example:** instead of learning ad platforms, a gym owner briefs a freelancer with an Impact Filter defining a cost per lead target.
**Note:** assumes budget. With no budget, eliminate or simplify first.

## Stewardship Delegation
**Sources:** [P04]; contribution focus in [P13]; Impact Filter in [P24].
**What it is:** delegating results rather than methods. Covey's parts: desired results, guidelines, resources, accountability, and consequences.
**When to use:** delegated work keeps coming back, or the owner micromanages.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = task handed over with steps only; 3 = result stated, no check-in; 5 = all five parts agreed and a review date set.
**Example:** a shop owner tells a manager the result (stock-outs below two per month, inventory within budget) and lets her choose the method.

## Time Blocking
**Sources:** [P06], [P02] (schedule every minute), timeboxing [P10], performance time [P18], consolidated time [P13]. Synthesis of [P02, P06, P10, P13, P18].
**What it is:** assigning work to calendar blocks in advance, with the most important work blocked first and protected.
**Parts:** a priority block (deep or "one thing" work), buffer blocks for email and admin, recovery or breakaway blocks. P18 suggests about three hours for a strategic block; P06 argues for about four hours a day.
**When to use:** the owner has priorities but never gets to them.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = no blocks; 3 = blocks exist but get moved more than half the time; 5 = priority block kept four or more days a week.
**Example:** a consultant blocks 8 to 10am for proposals, keeps calls after 11, and checks email at noon and 4pm.
**Note:** owners in shift, retail or field work may need shorter blocks (60 to 90 minutes) set before opening.

## The 12 Week Year Plan
**Sources:** [P18]; supported by goal cascade in [P06].
**What it is:** treating 12 weeks as a full planning year so urgency stays high.
**Steps:** 1) Write a long-term vision. 2) Choose one to three goals for 12 weeks. 3) List weekly tactics for each. 4) Block performance time. 5) Score weekly. 6) Review at week 13.
**When to use:** annual goals that drift; teams with no execution rhythm.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = goals with no tactics; 3 = tactics but no weekly score; 5 = tactics, score and accountability meeting all running.
**Example:** a landscaping firm sets a 12-week goal of 15 new maintenance contracts, with weekly tactics of 30 doorstep visits and 10 follow-up calls.

## Weekly Scorecard and Lead Indicators
**Sources:** [P18]; tracking in [P22]; monitoring in [P20]. Synthesis of [P18, P20, P22].
**What it is:** measuring the actions you control each week (lead indicators), not only the results (lag indicators).
**Steps:** 1) List the few tactics. 2) Each week, record done or not done. 3) Compute the share completed. 4) Fix the system when the score drops, not the goal.
**When to use:** whenever a plan exists; this is how you know it is being run.
**Score (guidance):** execution under 65 percent = 1 to 2; 65 to 84 percent = 3; 85 percent or more = 4 to 5 (P18 treats about 85 percent as strong).
**Example:** a recruiter tracks calls made and candidate screens, not just placements, and sees placements follow two months later.

## Weekly Review
**Sources:** [P01]; weekly planning by roles [P04]; WAM in [P18]; Reflect in [P09]. Synthesis of [P01, P04, P18].
**What it is:** a fixed weekly session to get current and plan the next week.
**Steps:** 1) Empty inboxes and notes. 2) Update project and next-action lists. 3) Review the calendar for last and next week. 4) Score last week. 5) Choose next week's priorities and block them.
**When to use:** every week; the single habit that keeps any system alive.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = never; 3 = monthly; 5 = weekly at a fixed time, under 60 minutes.
**Example:** a plumber spends Friday 3 to 4pm clearing paperwork and booking next week's quoting blocks.

## Capture, Clarify, Organize, Reflect, Engage
**Sources:** [P01]; plans quieting unfinished goals in [P20].
**What it is:** GTD's workflow for getting every commitment out of your head into trusted lists.
**Steps:** capture all inputs; clarify each (is it actionable, what is the next action); organize into projects, next actions, waiting-for, calendar, someday; reflect weekly; engage by choosing from the lists. Two-minute rule: do tiny items now.
**When to use:** overwhelm, dropped balls, a full inbox.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = commitments live in head and email; 3 = one list exists; 5 = all inputs captured and every project has a next action.
**Example:** an owner dumps 140 items onto paper, finds 30 are projects, and gives each a next action.

## Explore, Eliminate, Execute
**Sources:** [P05]; posteriorities in [P13]; Pareto in [P08].
**What it is:** McKeown's three-stage path: discern the vital few, cut the trivial many, make execution effortless.
**Steps:** 1) List commitments. 2) Apply extreme criteria (a clear yes, else no). 3) Cut or renegotiate. 4) Add buffer and routines to what remains.
**When to use:** too many projects, clients or commitments.
**Score (guidance):** for each commitment, 1 = clearly vital, 5 = clearly trivial; anything 3 or above is a candidate to cut.
**Example:** a marketing agency drops two low-margin service lines and stops sponsoring local events that never produced a client.

## Posteriorities and Choosing What to Neglect
**Sources:** [P13], [P12], [P05]. Synthesis of [P05, P12, P13].
**What it is:** deciding in advance what you will not do, so neglect is a choice rather than an accident.
**Steps:** 1) List what is getting partial attention. 2) Name what you will consciously let slide this quarter. 3) Tell the people affected. 4) Revisit next quarter.
**When to use:** guilt about an endless list; trying to do everything at a mediocre level.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = nothing deliberately dropped; 5 = a written "not this quarter" list shared with the team.
**Example:** a founder decides the company blog will stay untouched for 12 weeks while she fixes cash collection.

## Fixed-Volume Lists and Pull-Based Work
**Sources:** [P12] (limit work in progress), [P15] (pull-based workload, holding tank).
**What it is:** capping how many projects are active at once; new work waits in a visible queue and is pulled in only when capacity frees up.
**Steps:** 1) Set a cap on active projects. 2) Put everything else in a holding list. 3) Make the queue visible to the team or clients. 4) Pull the next item only when one finishes.
**When to use:** constant context switching, missed deadlines, a team that says yes to everything.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = no cap; 3 = cap exists but is ignored; 5 = cap held and queue visible.
**Example:** a web studio limits itself to three active builds and gives new clients a start date from the queue.

## Do Fewer Things (overhead tax)
**Sources:** [P15]; [P05]; [P12].
**What it is:** every commitment carries coordination overhead (meetings, emails, check-ins), so total output drops when too many are active.
**Parts:** limit missions (big aims), projects, and daily goals.
**When to use:** a schedule full of status updates about work that is not moving.
**Score (guidance):** count active projects per person: 1 to 2 = 5; 3 to 4 = 3; 5 or more = 1.
**Example:** an operations manager cuts from eight open initiatives to three and the weekly status meeting drops from 90 to 30 minutes.

## Four Depth Philosophies
**Sources:** [P02].
**What it is:** four ways to fit deep work into a life: monastic, bimodal, rhythmic, journalistic.
**When to use:** choosing a deep-work schedule that fits the business.
**Score (guidance):** fit, not quality: rhythmic suits most owners (daily block); bimodal suits owners who can take whole days away; monastic rarely fits an operator; journalistic only for experienced, disciplined people.
**Example:** a restaurant owner uses a rhythmic block every morning before prep starts.

## Single-Tasking and Attention Residue
**Sources:** [P02], one thing at a time [P13], the multitasking lie [P06], attentional space [P16]. Synthesis of [P02, P06, P13, P16].
**What it is:** switching tasks leaves residue that lowers quality, so do one important thing at a time.
**When to use:** the owner works with email and chat open while doing hard work.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = constant switching; 3 = some focused blocks; 5 = notifications off during every priority block.
**Example:** a finance lead closes email during the monthly close and finishes a day earlier.

## Drain the Shallows (shallow work budget)
**Sources:** [P02].
**What it is:** measuring and capping shallow work. Newport suggests asking how long a smart recent graduate would need to learn a task to judge its depth, and agreeing a budget for shallow work.
**When to use:** meetings and admin eat the day.
**Score (guidance):** shallow share of the week: over 70 percent = 1; 50 to 70 = 3; under 50 = 5.
**Example:** an owner sets a 40 percent shallow budget and moves internal updates to a written weekly note.

## Four Laws of Behavior Change
**Sources:** [P03]; habit stacking and implementation intentions are part of it.
**What it is:** make a habit obvious, attractive, easy and satisfying; invert each to break one.
**Steps:** 1) Name the habit and the identity behind it. 2) Set a cue with time and place ("after X, I will Y"). 3) Pair it with something enjoyable. 4) Reduce friction. 5) Track it and reward completion.
**When to use:** any routine the owner or team keeps skipping.
**Score (guidance):** rate each law 1 to 5 for the habit; the lowest score is the fix to make first.
**Example:** a salesperson sets up the CRM call list the night before (obvious, easy) and ticks a visible board after each call (satisfying).

## The Habit Loop and the Golden Rule
**Sources:** [P11]; extended in [P03].
**What it is:** cue, routine, reward driven by craving. Keep the cue and reward and change the routine.
**Steps:** 1) Identify the routine. 2) Experiment with rewards to learn the real craving. 3) Isolate the cue (time, place, emotion, people, previous action). 4) Plan a replacement routine.
**When to use:** breaking a bad routine (checking email at 7am, late-night scrolling, snacking at the desk).
**Score (guidance):** 1 = cue unknown; 3 = cue known; 5 = cue, reward and replacement routine written down and tested a week.
**Example:** an owner who opens email when stuck learns the craving is relief from uncertainty and replaces it with writing one next step.

## Keystone Habits
**Sources:** [P11]; small wins in [P05], [P11].
**What it is:** a few habits that set off chain reactions across a person or company.
**When to use:** a team needs one cultural change that pulls others along.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = isolated habit; 5 = habit forces better communication, measurement or standards elsewhere.
**Example:** a manufacturer makes a daily safety and quality huddle the keystone; it also surfaces supply issues early.

## Mini Habits
**Sources:** [P19]; two-minute rule in [P03].
**What it is:** setting a requirement so small you cannot fail, then counting anything more as a bonus.
**When to use:** the owner has failed at an ambitious routine more than once.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = requirement needs motivation; 5 = requirement takes under two minutes and is done daily.
**Example:** an owner who never prospects commits to one outreach message a day; most days she sends five.

## Environment Design and Precommitment
**Sources:** [P03] environment design, [P10] pacts, [P20] precommitment, [P08] batching rules. Synthesis of [P03, P08, P10, P20].
**What it is:** shaping surroundings and locking in choices in advance so willpower is not needed.
**Parts:** remove cues for bad habits, add cues for good ones; effort pacts (add friction), price pacts (cost of failing), identity pacts (self-image).
**When to use:** the owner knows what to do but gives in to temptation.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = relies on willpower; 3 = some friction added; 5 = at least one pact and environment change for each key habit.
**Example:** an owner uses a site blocker 8 to 11am and gives a friend a deposit she forfeits if she misses her weekly finance review.

## The Indistractable Model
**Sources:** [P10].
**What it is:** four parts: master internal triggers, make time for traction, hack back external triggers, prevent distraction with pacts. Traction is what you planned; distraction is anything else.
**When to use:** distraction is the main complaint.
**Score (guidance):** score each of the four parts 1 to 5; fix the lowest first. Internal triggers usually come first.
**Example:** a founder who checks Slack when anxious notes the feeling, waits ten minutes, and turns off badges outside two set check times.

## Daily Highlight and Rule of 3
**Sources:** Highlight [P09]; Rule of 3 [P25]; eat the frog [P07]. Synthesis of [P07, P09, P25].
**What it is:** picking one (Highlight) or three (Rule of 3) outcomes for the day before starting, and doing them first or in your best hours.
**When to use:** days disappear without the important thing done.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = no daily choice; 3 = chosen but done less than half the time; 5 = done on four of five days.
**Example:** an owner's Highlight is "send the bank the revised forecast" and it goes out by 10am.

## ABCDE Method and Eat That Frog
**Sources:** [P07].
**What it is:** ranking tasks by consequence (A must, B should, C nice, D delegate, E eliminate), then starting with the most important A.
**When to use:** a long daily list and procrastination on the hard task.
**Score (guidance):** the A rating itself is the score; never work on B while an A is open.
**Example:** a manager labels "difficult conversation with underperforming employee" as A1 and does it at 9am.

## Procrastination Triggers
**Sources:** [P25]; internal triggers in [P10].
**What it is:** tasks are avoided when boring, frustrating, difficult, ambiguous, unstructured, or lacking meaning. Find which apply and reverse them.
**Steps:** 1) Name the avoided task. 2) Score which triggers apply. 3) Reverse them (add structure, define the first step, shrink it, add reward).
**When to use:** a single important task keeps slipping.
**Score (guidance):** count the triggers present: 0 to 1 = 5; 2 to 3 = 3; 4 or more = 1, redesign the task.
**Example:** the annual pricing review feels ambiguous and unstructured; the owner writes three questions and books 45 minutes with the bookkeeper.

## Biological Prime Time and Energy Management
**Sources:** [P25]; Energize [P09]; protect the asset [P05]; health thief [P06]. Synthesis of [P05, P06, P09, P25].
**What it is:** tracking energy across the day to place important work in peak hours, and protecting sleep, exercise and food.
**When to use:** the owner has the time but not the energy.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = priority work in low-energy hours; 5 = priority work in peak hours and sleep protected.
**Example:** a founder finds his peak is 9 to noon and moves all sales calls to the afternoon.

## DEAL
**Sources:** [P08]; elimination in [P05].
**What it is:** Definition (what you actually want), Elimination (cut low-value work), Automation (systems and outsourcing), Liberation (freedom from location and presence).
**When to use:** the owner wants a business that runs with less of their time.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = owner involved in every transaction; 3 = some systems; 5 = core operations run for two weeks without the owner.
**Example:** an online retailer automates order confirmations, hires a VA for returns, and checks the business twice a week.
**Note:** do elimination before automation; automating waste only makes it faster.

## Pareto and Parkinson
**Sources:** [P08]; 80/20 in [P07].
**What it is:** most value comes from a minority of inputs (Pareto), and work expands to fill the time given (Parkinson). Use them together: find the vital few, then give them short deadlines.
**When to use:** analyzing customers, products, tasks, or meetings.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = time spread evenly; 5 = time concentrated on the inputs that produce most results, with tight deadlines.
**Example:** an accountant finds a fifth of clients bring most of the fees and most of the complaints come from the bottom fifth; she raises prices on the bottom group.

## Batching and the Low-Information Diet
**Sources:** [P08]; email batching in [P02]; maintenance blocks in [P25].
**What it is:** grouping similar shallow tasks into fixed slots and cutting unnecessary information intake.
**When to use:** email, messages and news fragment the day.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = check messages constantly; 3 = a few set times; 5 = two or three set times and an autoresponder or team rule.
**Example:** an owner checks email at 11am and 4pm and tells staff to call for anything truly urgent.

## Deliberate Practice
**Sources:** [P23]; challenge-skill balance in [P21].
**What it is:** improving a skill with specific goals, full focus, feedback, and steady stretch beyond comfort, ideally designed by a coach.
**Steps:** 1) Pick the sub-skill. 2) Define a measurable target. 3) Practice it in short focused sessions. 4) Get feedback (recording, coach, data). 5) Adjust and raise difficulty.
**When to use:** a skill gap is limiting the business (pitching, negotiating, estimating, managing).
**Score (guidance):** 1 = just doing the job more; 3 = goal and feedback; 5 = goal, feedback, coach, and weekly stretch.
**Example:** an owner records her sales calls, reviews them with a mentor weekly, and practices the price conversation until it is smooth.

## Flow Conditions
**Sources:** [P21]; hyperfocus [P16]; deep work [P02].
**What it is:** flow happens with clear goals, immediate feedback, and a challenge matched to skill.
**When to use:** designing roles or work sessions people find absorbing.
**Score (guidance):** rate goals, feedback and challenge-skill match each 1 to 5; any 1 or 2 is the redesign target.
**Example:** a warehouse lead adds a live board of orders picked per hour; the team gets clear goals and feedback.

## Hyperfocus and Scatterfocus
**Sources:** [P16].
**What it is:** two modes of attention: concentrate on one chosen task, or let the mind wander deliberately (on a walk, during simple chores) to connect ideas and plan.
**When to use:** the owner is busy executing but has no ideas or strategy.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = no unfocused time; 5 = daily focus block plus regular wander time.
**Example:** an owner takes a phone-free walk after the morning block and returns with the fix for a scheduling problem.

## CODE and PARA
**Sources:** [P17].
**What it is:** Capture, Organize, Distill, Express for notes; organized by Projects, Areas, Resources, Archives.
**When to use:** the owner rewrites the same proposals, training and content from scratch.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = notes scattered; 3 = one place; 5 = reusable packets used in at least one output per week.
**Example:** a trainer saves every workshop exercise as a reusable packet and builds a new course in a week.
**Note:** stop at "good enough"; the system is for output, not collecting.

## The Compound Effect
**Sources:** [P22]; 1 percent improvements and the Plateau of Latent Potential in [P03].
**What it is:** small, smart choices repeated consistently over time yield large results.
**When to use:** the owner is impatient or keeps changing plans before results show.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = new plan every month; 5 = same sound routine run and tracked for 12 weeks or more.
**Example:** a dentist sends five referral thank-you notes a week for a year and referrals become the main source of patients.

## Circle of Influence
**Sources:** [P04].
**What it is:** separate what you care about (concern) from what you can affect (influence) and spend energy on the second.
**When to use:** the owner spends time worrying about the economy, competitors or regulation.
**Score (guidance):** share of worry time on things you control: under 25 percent = 1; over 75 percent = 5.
**Example:** a retailer stops tracking every rate rumor and spends that hour renegotiating supplier terms.

## Fear-Setting
**Sources:** [P08].
**What it is:** writing down the worst case of a decision, how to prevent it, how to repair it, and the cost of not acting.
**When to use:** the owner is stuck on a hard decision such as hiring, raising prices, or stepping back.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = worst case unknown; 5 = worst case, prevention, repair and cost of inaction all written.
**Example:** an owner afraid to take two weeks off writes the worst case, sets up a manager and phone rule, and goes.

## Camcorder Method
**Sources:** [P14]; playbook thinking in [P08].
**What it is:** record yourself doing a task once, then turn the recording into a written process for the person who takes it over.
**When to use:** the owner says delegating takes longer than doing.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = no documentation; 5 = recorded, written, handed off, and reviewed once.
**Example:** a store owner screen-records the weekly ordering routine; a new hire runs it within two weeks.

## Effective Decisions
**Sources:** [P13].
**What it is:** Drucker's approach to few but important decisions: classify the problem (generic or unique), define boundary conditions, decide what is right before what is acceptable, build the action in, and test against results.
**When to use:** a big or recurring decision.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = decided on the spot; 3 = boundary conditions written; 5 = right answer, compromise, action owner and review date all written.
**Example:** an owner facing repeat late deliveries treats it as a generic problem and writes a policy instead of fixing each case.
