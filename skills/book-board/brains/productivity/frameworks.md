# Frameworks: Productivity department

Built from the 25 Productivity books (ids P01 to P30, with gaps), most useful first. Names are the books' names; explanations are ours. Scores are guidance for the advisor, not from the books.

Quote policy: verbatim quotes appear only from the author talks and public-domain texts listed in talks.md, always with a T id. Copyrighted book text is never quoted. Everything else is paraphrase cited by book id.

## The Focusing Question
**Sources:** [P06], related to Explore, Eliminate, Execute [P05] and eat the frog [P07].
**What it is:** asking which single action would make everything else easier or unnecessary, at the level of this year, this quarter, this week and right now.
**Parts:** 1) Pick the time horizon. 2) List candidate actions. 3) For each, ask what it unlocks. 4) Choose the one with the widest knock-on effect. 5) Block time for it first.
**When to use:** too many good options, scattered effort, an owner who starts many things and finishes few.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = the task unlocks nothing else; 3 = helps one other area; 5 = makes several other problems easier or removes them.
**Example:** a bakery owner picks a wholesale deal over a new website and a loyalty app because it funds the next hire.

## Time Management Matrix (Quadrant II)
**Sources:** [P04]; consequence thinking from [P07].
**What it is:** a two-by-two of urgent versus important. Move time from the not-important quadrants into important-not-urgent work (planning, prevention, relationships, capability).
**Parts:** Quadrant I crises; Quadrant II prevention and building; Quadrant III interruptions; Quadrant IV time wasters.
**When to use:** the owner lives in firefighting mode.
**Score (guidance):** rate a task 1 to 5 on long-term consequence; Quadrant II work that scores 4 or 5 goes on the calendar before Quadrant III work of any score.
**Example:** an agency owner moves two hours a week from client email into an onboarding process, which cuts the emails.

## Know Thy Time (time audit)
**Sources:** [P13]; audit step of the Buyback Loop [P14]; time awareness first [P08].
**What it is:** record where time goes, cut waste, consolidate the rest into large blocks.
**Steps:** 1) Log time for one to two weeks in small slots. 2) Mark each block by value and energy. 3) Ask of each activity: what happens if it is not done at all? 4) Delegate what others can do. 5) Consolidate the remaining discretionary time into long blocks.
**When to use:** first step for any "no time" complaint.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = owner guesses; 3 = one week logged; 5 = two weeks logged with value and energy tags.
**Example:** a contractor finds nine weekly hours go to driving to quote small jobs; he sets a minimum job size and quotes by video.

## DRIP Matrix
**Sources:** [P14].
**What it is:** a two-by-two of tasks by money value (high/low) and energy (lights you up/drains you).
**Parts:** Delegation (low value, draining), Replacement (high value, draining: hire someone to own it), Investment (low current value, energizing: time spent on the future), Production (high value, energizing: your zone).
**When to use:** deciding what to hand off and in what order.
**Score (guidance):** for each task score value 1 to 5 and energy 1 to 5; anything with value 3 or below and energy 2 or below goes first.
**Example:** a clinic owner keeps consults (production), hands billing to a bookkeeper (delegation), and plans a practice manager (replacement).

## Buyback Rate
**Sources:** [P14], supported by Who Not How [P24] and Ferriss's rough hourly value [P08].
**What it is:** a threshold price for your time; tasks you could pay someone less than this rate to do are candidates to hand off. Martell computes it from your hourly value (detail of the exact fraction uncertain; treat as roughly a quarter). Ferriss's quick hourly value: annual income minus three zeros, halved.
**Steps:** 1) Estimate your effective hourly value. 2) Set the buyback threshold below it. 3) List tasks below the threshold. 4) Hand off the most frequent ones first.
**When to use:** the owner refuses to hire because "I can do it cheaper myself".
**Score (guidance):** 1 = task worth far above your rate (keep); 5 = task worth far below it (hand off now).
**Example:** an owner earning the equivalent of 100 per hour stops doing 20-per-hour data entry and hires a part-time assistant.

## Who Not How and the Impact Filter
**Sources:** [P24]; delegation logic also in [P08], [P14].
**What it is:** asking "who can get this result?" instead of "how do I do this?", then briefing that person with a one-page Impact Filter (purpose, why it matters, ideal outcome, success criteria). Keep your unique ability; hand off the rest.
**Steps:** 1) Name the result. 2) Write the Impact Filter. 3) Find a capable Who (staff, contractor, partner). 4) Agree the win for them. 5) Step back and review results.
**When to use:** the owner is about to learn a new skill for a one-off need.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = core skill the owner must master; 3 = useful but someone else does it better; 5 = rare need, clear specialist exists.
**Example:** a gym owner briefs an ads freelancer with an Impact Filter and a cost per lead target.
**Note:** assumes budget. With no budget, eliminate or simplify first.

## Stewardship Delegation
**Sources:** [P04]; contribution focus in [P13]; Impact Filter in [P24]; stop rescuing [P27].
**What it is:** delegating results rather than methods. Covey's parts: desired results, guidelines, resources, accountability, and consequences. Then let the person own the result, including a first miss.
**When to use:** delegated work keeps coming back, or the owner micromanages.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = task handed over with steps only; 3 = result stated, no check-in; 5 = all five parts agreed and a review date set.
**Example:** a shop owner sets the result (under two stock-outs a month, within budget) and lets the manager choose the method.

## Let Them, Let Me
**Sources:** [P27]; Circle of Influence (concern versus influence) [P04]; what is in your power [P28].
**What it is:** when staff, clients or partners do not behave as you want, stop forcing it. Let them show you who they are, then act on what you control: a clear standard, a consequence, a different hire, or less worry. Exceptions where you speak up: danger, your own pay or rights, repeated boundary crossing.
**Quote:** "you can guilt them you can shame them you can complement them you can love them but you can't control them" (T071)
**When to use:** hours lost redoing staff work, chasing approval, or worrying about competitors, regulation or other people.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = owner rescues or chases every time; 3 = notices but still steps in; 5 = sets the standard once, then lets the result land.
**Example:** an owner stops rewriting a rep's proposals, states the quality bar and a review date, and lets results show whether the role fits.

## Time Blocking
**Sources:** [P06], [P02] (schedule every minute), timeboxing [P10], performance time [P18], consolidated time [P13]. Synthesis of [P02, P06, P10, P13, P18].
**What it is:** assigning work to calendar blocks in advance, most important work first and protected.
**Parts:** a priority block (deep or "one thing" work), buffer blocks for email and admin, recovery or breakaway blocks. P18 suggests about three hours for a strategic block; P06 argues for about four hours a day.
**When to use:** the owner has priorities but never gets to them.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = no blocks; 3 = blocks exist but get moved more than half the time; 5 = priority block kept four or more days a week.
**Example:** a consultant blocks 8 to 10am for proposals, keeps calls after 11, and checks email at noon and 4pm.
**Note:** owners in shift, retail or field work may need shorter blocks (60 to 90 minutes) set before opening.

## The 12 Week Year Plan
**Sources:** [P18]; supported by goal cascade in [P06].
**What it is:** treating 12 weeks as a full year, not a quarter, so urgency stays high. Five disciplines: vision, planning, process control, scorekeeping, time use.
**Steps:** 1) Write a long-term vision. 2) Choose one to three goals for 12 weeks (in a first cycle, one goal and no more than three actions). 3) List weekly tactics for each. 4) Block performance time. 5) Score weekly. 6) Use week 13 to celebrate, review and reload.
**Quote:** "you literally want to treat every 12 weeks as a year" (T065)
**When to use:** annual goals that drift; teams with no execution rhythm.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = goals with no tactics; 3 = tactics but no weekly score; 5 = tactics, score and accountability meeting all running.
**Example:** a landscaper sets 15 new maintenance contracts in 12 weeks, with 30 doorstep visits and 10 follow-up calls a week.

## Weekly Scorecard and Lead Indicators
**Sources:** [P18]; tracking in [P22]. Synthesis of [P18, P22].
**What it is:** measuring the actions you control (lead indicators), not only results (lag).
**Steps:** 1) List the few tactics. 2) Each week, record done or not done. 3) Compute the share completed. 4) Fix the system when the score drops, not the goal.
**Quote:** "80% or higher with the execution, in most cases, you accomplish the goals" (T065)
**When to use:** whenever a plan exists; this is how you know it is being run.
**Score (guidance):** execution under 65 percent = 1 to 2; 65 to 79 percent = 3; 80 percent or more = 4 to 5 (Moran's observed pattern, not a law).
**Example:** a recruiter tracks calls and screens, not just placements, and sees placements follow.

## Weekly Review
**Sources:** [P01]; weekly planning by roles [P04]; WAM in [P18]. Synthesis of [P01, P04, P18].
**What it is:** a fixed weekly session to get current and plan the next week. Covey plans weekly because daily planning alone becomes crisis management.
**Steps:** 1) Empty inboxes and notes. 2) Update project and next-action lists. 3) Review the calendar for last and next week. 4) Score last week. 5) Choose next week's priorities and block them.
**Quote:** "put that on a project list that you look at, at least once a week" (T043)
**When to use:** every week; the single habit that keeps any system alive.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = never; 3 = monthly; 5 = weekly at a fixed time, under 60 minutes.
**Example:** a plumber spends Friday 3 to 4pm clearing paperwork and booking next week's quoting blocks.

## Capture, Clarify, Organize, Reflect, Engage
**Sources:** [P01].
**What it is:** getting every commitment out of your head into trusted lists. Allen says you need not finish an open loop to clear it, only identify it and trust you will see it.
**Steps:** capture all inputs; clarify each (what outcome are you committed to, what is the very next action); organize into projects, next actions, waiting-for, calendar, someday; reflect weekly; engage by choosing from the lists. Two-minute rule: do tiny items now.
**When to use:** overwhelm, dropped balls, a full inbox.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = commitments live in head and email; 3 = one list exists; 5 = all inputs captured and every project has a next action.
**Example:** an owner dumps 140 items onto paper, finds 30 are projects, and gives each a next action.

## Explore, Eliminate, Execute
**Sources:** [P05]; posteriorities in [P13]; Pareto in [P08]; necessary actions in [P28].
**What it is:** McKeown's three-stage path: discern the vital few, cut the trivial many, make execution effortless.
**Steps:** 1) List commitments. 2) Apply extreme criteria (a clear yes, else no). 3) Cut or renegotiate. 4) Add buffer and routines to what remains.
**Quote:** "you can either do many many things averagely well or you can do a few things superbly well" (T050)
**When to use:** too many projects, clients or commitments.
**Score (guidance):** for each commitment, 1 = clearly vital, 5 = clearly trivial; anything 3 or above is a candidate to cut.
**Example:** an agency drops two low-margin service lines and event sponsorships that never produced a client.

## The Necessary Actions Test
**Sources:** [P28]; elimination in [P05], [P08].
**What it is:** before each act, ask whether it is necessary; drop, shorten or hand off what is not, including worries and talk about other people.
**Quote:** "may not this that now I go about, be of the number of unnecessary actions?" (T102)
**When to use:** a busy day with little output; an owner who joins every thread.
**Score (guidance):** tasks dropped or shortened per day: 0 = 1; 1 to 2 = 3; 3 or more = 5.
**Example:** a retailer asks the question at each inbox check and finds half the threads need nothing from her.

## Posteriorities and Choosing What to Neglect
**Sources:** [P13], [P12], [P05]. Synthesis of [P05, P12, P13].
**What it is:** deciding in advance what you will not do, so neglect is a choice. Burkeman's point: there are too many important things, so good things must go to make room for the best.
**Steps:** 1) List what is getting partial attention. 2) Name what you will consciously let slide this quarter. 3) Tell the people affected. 4) Revisit next quarter.
**Quote:** "you're gonna have to not do lots of things that do matter" (T059)
**When to use:** guilt about an endless list; trying to do everything at a mediocre level.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = nothing deliberately dropped; 5 = a written "not this quarter" list shared with the team.
**Example:** a founder decides the company blog will stay untouched for 12 weeks while she fixes cash collection.

## Fixed-Volume Lists and Pull-Based Work
**Sources:** [P12] (limit work in progress), [P15] (pull-based workload, holding tank).
**What it is:** capping active projects; new work waits in a visible queue until capacity frees up.
**Steps:** 1) Set a cap on active projects. 2) Put everything else in a holding list. 3) Make the queue visible to the team or clients. 4) Pull the next item only when one finishes.
**When to use:** constant context switching, missed deadlines, a team that says yes to everything.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = no cap; 3 = cap exists but is ignored; 5 = cap held and queue visible.
**Example:** a web studio limits itself to three active builds and gives new clients a start date from the queue.

## Do Fewer Things (overhead tax)
**Sources:** [P15]; [P05]; [P12].
**What it is:** every commitment carries coordination overhead (meetings, emails, check-ins), so total output drops when too many are active. Newport means fewer things at once, not less overall; finishing speed rises.
**Parts:** limit missions (big aims), projects, and daily goals.
**Quote:** "When you agree to do something, it brings with it administrative overhead" (T063)
**When to use:** a schedule full of status updates about work that is not moving.
**Score (guidance):** count active projects per person: 1 to 2 = 5; 3 to 4 = 3; 5 or more = 1.
**Example:** an operations manager cuts eight initiatives to three; the status meeting drops from 90 to 30 minutes.

## Four Depth Philosophies
**Sources:** [P02].
**When to use:** choosing a deep-work schedule that fits the business.
**Score (guidance):** fit, not quality: rhythmic (daily block) suits most owners; bimodal suits those who can take whole days away; monastic rarely fits; journalistic only for experienced people.
**Example:** a restaurant owner uses a rhythmic block every morning before prep starts.

## Single-Tasking and Attention Residue
**Sources:** [P02], one thing at a time [P13], the multitasking lie [P06]. Synthesis of [P02, P06, P13].
**What it is:** switching tasks leaves residue that lowers quality, so do one important thing at a time.
**When to use:** the owner works with email and chat open while doing hard work.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = constant switching; 3 = some focused blocks; 5 = notifications off during every priority block.
**Example:** a finance lead closes email during the monthly close and finishes a day earlier.

## Drain the Shallows (shallow work budget)
**Sources:** [P02].
**What it is:** measuring and capping shallow work. Judge depth by how long a smart graduate would need to learn the task, and agree a shallow budget.
**When to use:** meetings and admin eat the day.
**Score (guidance):** shallow share of the week: over 70 percent = 1; 50 to 70 = 3; under 50 = 5.
**Example:** an owner sets a 40 percent shallow budget and moves internal updates to a written weekly note.

## Four Laws of Behavior Change
**Sources:** [P03]; habit stacking and implementation intentions are part of it.
**What it is:** every habit runs cue, craving, response, reward. Make it obvious, attractive, easy and satisfying; invert each to break one. Clear says make it easy matters most.
**Steps:** 1) Name the habit and the identity behind it. 2) Set a cue with time and place ("after X, I will Y"). 3) Pair it with something enjoyable. 4) Reduce friction. 5) Track it and reward completion.
**When to use:** any routine the owner or team keeps skipping.
**Score (guidance):** rate each law 1 to 5 for the habit; the lowest score is the fix to make first.
**Example:** a salesperson sets the call list the night before and ticks a visible board after each call.

## Two-Minute Rule and Habit Shaping
**Sources:** [P03]; small wins in [P05].
**What it is:** scale a new habit down to a start of two minutes or less, done on schedule even on a bad day, then grow it in small steps. Reduce the scope, keep the schedule, never post a zero.
**Quote:** "a habit must be established before it can be improved" (T047)
**When to use:** the owner has failed at an ambitious routine more than once.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = requirement needs motivation; 5 = requirement takes under two minutes and is done daily.
**Example:** an owner who never prospects commits to one outreach message a day; most days she sends five.

## The Habit Loop and the Golden Rule
**Sources:** [P11]; extended in [P03].
**What it is:** cue, routine, reward driven by craving. You cannot erase a habit; keep the cue and reward and change the routine.
**Steps:** 1) Identify the routine. 2) Experiment with rewards to learn the real craving. 3) Isolate the cue by logging the five types each time. 4) Plan a replacement routine.
**When to use:** breaking a bad routine (checking email at 7am, late-night scrolling, snacking at the desk).
**Score (guidance):** 1 = cue unknown; 3 = cue known; 5 = cue, reward and replacement routine written down and tested a week.
**Example:** an owner who opens email when stuck finds the craving is relief from uncertainty and writes one next step instead.

## Keystone Habits
**Sources:** [P11]; small wins in [P05], [P11].
**What it is:** a few habits that set off chain reactions, often by changing self-image.
**When to use:** a team needs one cultural change that pulls others along.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = isolated habit; 5 = habit forces better communication, measurement or standards elsewhere.
**Example:** a manufacturer's daily safety huddle also surfaces supply issues early.

## Environment Design and Precommitment
**Sources:** [P03] environment design, [P10] pacts, [P08] batching rules. Synthesis of [P03, P08, P10].
**What it is:** shaping surroundings and locking in choices so willpower is not needed.
**Parts:** remove cues for bad habits, add cues for good ones; effort pacts (add friction), price pacts (cost of failing), identity pacts (self-image). Eyal puts pacts last, after internal triggers, planning and external triggers.
**When to use:** the owner knows what to do but gives in to temptation.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = relies on willpower; 3 = some friction added; 5 = at least one pact and environment change for each key habit.
**Example:** an owner blocks sites 8 to 11am and forfeits a deposit to a friend if she skips her finance review.

## The Indistractable Model
**Sources:** [P10].
**What it is:** four steps in order: master internal triggers, make time for traction, hack back external triggers, prevent distraction with pacts. Eyal says about 90 percent of distraction starts inside. The 10-minute rule: you may give in, but not yet.
**When to use:** distraction is the main complaint.
**Score (guidance):** score each of the four parts 1 to 5; fix the lowest first. Internal triggers usually come first.
**Example:** a founder who checks Slack when anxious names the feeling, waits ten minutes, and turns off badges.

## ABCDE Method and Eat That Frog
**Sources:** [P07].
**What it is:** ranking tasks by consequence (A must, B should, C nice, D delegate, E eliminate), then starting with the most important A.
**When to use:** a long daily list and procrastination on the hard task.
**Score (guidance):** the A rating itself is the score; never work on B while an A is open.
**Example:** a manager labels "difficult conversation with underperforming employee" as A1 and does it at 9am.

## Procrastination Diagnosis
**Sources:** internal triggers and the distraction notebook [P10]; procrastination as a signal [P24]; discomfort of uncertain work [P12]; consequences [P07]. Synthesis of [P07, P10, P12, P24].
**What it is:** treating avoidance as information. Find the cause and match the fix: a feeling to escape (define the first step, start for 10 minutes), a planning gap (write the outcome and next action), or a task that is not yours (hand it off).
**When to use:** a single important task keeps slipping.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = cause unknown; 3 = cause named; 5 = cause named and the matching fix applied within 48 hours.
**Example:** the annual pricing review feels uncertain; the owner writes three questions and books 45 minutes with the bookkeeper.

## Energy and Renewal
**Sources:** protect the asset [P05]; health thief [P06]; Sharpen the Saw [P04]; first-thing frog [P07]. Synthesis of [P04, P05, P06, P07].
**What it is:** placing important work in your sharpest hours and protecting sleep, exercise and food as the base of output.
**When to use:** the owner has the time but not the energy.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = priority work in low-energy hours; 5 = priority work in peak hours and sleep protected.
**Example:** a founder finds his peak is 9 to noon and moves all sales calls to the afternoon.

## DEAL
**Sources:** [P08]; elimination in [P05].
**What it is:** Definition (what you want), Elimination (cut low-value work), Automation (systems, outsourcing), Liberation (freedom from location and presence).
**Quote:** "if you don't eliminate first and you get very good at the processes what happens is you become extremely efficient but not effective" (T053)
**When to use:** the owner wants a business that runs with less of their time.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = owner involved in every transaction; 3 = some systems; 5 = core operations run for two weeks without the owner.
**Example:** an online retailer automates order confirmations, hires a VA for returns, and checks the business twice a week.
**Note:** do elimination before automation; automating waste only makes it faster. Plan what the freed time is for.

## Pareto and Parkinson
**Sources:** [P08]; 80/20 in [P07].
**What it is:** most value comes from a minority of inputs (Pareto), and work expands to fill the time given (Parkinson). Use them together: find the vital few, then give them short deadlines.
**When to use:** analyzing customers, products, tasks, or meetings.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = time spread evenly; 5 = time concentrated on the inputs that produce most results, with tight deadlines.
**Example:** an accountant finds the bottom fifth of clients cause most complaints and raises their prices.

## Batching and the Low-Information Diet
**Sources:** [P08]; email batching in [P02].
**What it is:** grouping shallow tasks into fixed slots and cutting information intake.
**When to use:** email, messages and news fragment the day.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = check messages constantly; 3 = a few set times; 5 = two or three set times and an autoresponder or team rule.
**Example:** an owner checks email at 11am and 4pm and tells staff to call for anything truly urgent.

## Deliberate Practice
**Sources:** [P23]; challenge-skill balance in [P21].
**What it is:** improving a skill with specific goals, full focus, feedback, and steady stretch beyond comfort. Ericsson separates purposeful practice (self-directed, with goals and feedback) from deliberate practice (guided by a coach who has taken others to the level you want).
**Steps:** 1) Pick the sub-skill. 2) Set a measurable target. 3) Practice in short sessions, apart from live work. 4) Get feedback (recording, coach, data). 5) Raise difficulty.
**Quote:** "if you're just doing what you're able to do already then why would you expect to get any better" (T067)
**When to use:** a skill gap is limiting the business (pitching, negotiating, estimating, managing).
**Score (guidance):** 1 = just doing the job more; 3 = goal and feedback; 5 = goal, feedback, coach, and weekly stretch.
**Example:** an owner records sales calls, reviews them weekly with a mentor, and drills the price conversation.

## Flow Conditions
**Sources:** [P21]; deep work [P02].
**What it is:** flow happens with attention on a narrow field, clear next-step goals, immediate feedback, and a challenge matched to skill. Raise the challenge as skill grows.
**When to use:** designing roles or work sessions people find absorbing.
**Score (guidance):** rate goals, feedback and challenge-skill match each 1 to 5; any 1 or 2 is the redesign target.
**Example:** a warehouse lead adds a live board of orders picked per hour; the team gets clear goals and feedback.

## Productive Meditation and Thinking Time
**Sources:** productive meditation [P02]; inner retreat [P28].
**What it is:** protected time to think: a walk holding one specific problem in mind, pulling attention back when it drifts, or a short retreat into your own mind.
**Quote:** "At what time soever thou wilt, it is in thy power to retire into thyself" (T102)
**When to use:** the owner is busy executing but has no ideas or strategy.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = no thinking time; 5 = daily focus block plus at least one weekly problem walk.
**Example:** an owner walks without a phone on one question (why quotes stall after site visits) and returns with a fix.

## CODE and PARA
**Sources:** [P17].
**What it is:** Capture, Organize, Distill, Express for notes; organized by Projects, Areas, Resources, Archives.
**When to use:** the owner rewrites the same proposals, training and content from scratch.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = notes scattered; 3 = one place; 5 = reusable packets used in at least one output per week.
**Example:** a trainer saves every workshop exercise as a reusable packet and builds a new course in a week.
**Note:** stop at "good enough"; Forte himself prefers messy, quick notes.

## The Compound Effect
**Sources:** [P22]; 1 percent improvements and the Plateau of Latent Potential in [P03].
**What it is:** small, smart choices repeated consistently over time yield large results.
**When to use:** the owner is impatient or keeps changing plans before results show.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = new plan every month; 5 = same sound routine run and tracked for 12 weeks or more.
**Example:** a dentist sends five referral thank-you notes a week for a year; referrals become the main source of patients.

## Accountability Mirror and Cookie Jar
**Sources:** [P26]; identity votes [P03].
**What it is:** the mirror: blunt written truths about where you are and what you will do, faced daily. The jar: a list of hard things you have already done, read when you want to quit.
**Quote:** "the choice I made was what are you willing to sacrifice and what are you willing to give up" (T070)
**When to use:** the system exists, but the owner quits when it gets uncomfortable.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = excuses unexamined; 3 = mirror list written; 5 = reviewed daily and the jar used to push through.
**Example:** an owner writes "I avoid sales calls because I fear rejection" on the mirror and makes ten calls before email.
**Note:** a short push, not a permanent mode; watch for burnout (P15, P12).

## Reframe the Setback
**Sources:** the obstacle becomes the way [P28]; the last freedom [P29]; Circle of Influence [P04].
**What it is:** for a setback that cannot be undone, separate the fact from your judgment, choose your attitude, and turn the obstacle into this week's task. If the cause can be fixed, fix it first.
**Quote:** "if we cannot change a situation we have always the last freedom to change our attitude to that situation" (T072)
**When to use:** the owner is stuck ruminating after a lost client or failed launch.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = still replaying it; 3 = judgment named; 5 = a next task drawn from it and scheduled.
**Example:** after losing a large account, an owner turns the freed capacity into a 12-week push for three mid-sized clients.

## Meaning Check
**Sources:** [P29]; mission statement [P04]; autotelic work [P21].
**What it is:** naming what the work is for: a task to finish, people served, a stance worth taking. Frankl says happiness cannot be aimed at; it follows from a cause or a person.
**When to use:** the owner is efficient but empty, or the team works hard with no sense of why.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = cannot say why; 5 = a one-line why tied to people served, reviewed quarterly.
**Example:** a burned-out clinic owner names the families she serves as the why and cuts two projects that do not serve them.

## Definite Major Purpose
**Sources:** [P30]; written goals with deadlines [P07]; vision in [P18].
**What it is:** one written statement of what you want, by when, and what you will give in return, read daily and backed by a plan.
**Quote:** "write down a clear statement of precisely what you intend to give in return for that which you desire from life" (T074)
**When to use:** the owner drifts between ideas or has no clear target.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = no written target; 3 = written, not reviewed; 5 = reviewed weekly and cascaded into a 12-week plan.
**Note:** keep the writing and planning; skip Hill's claim that belief alone attracts results.

## Mastermind and Accountability Group
**Sources:** [P30] mastermind; [P18] Weekly Accountability Meeting. Synthesis of [P18, P30].
**What it is:** two or more people meeting regularly toward a definite purpose, lending experience and holding each other to commitments, as ownership, not punishment.
**When to use:** the owner works alone and plans fade with nobody watching.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = no peers; 3 = informal check-ins; 5 = weekly meeting with scores shared.
**Example:** three owners meet 30 minutes each Monday to share scores and one problem.

## Fear-Setting
**Sources:** [P08].
**What it is:** writing the worst case, how to prevent and repair it, and the cost of not acting.
**When to use:** the owner is stuck on a hard decision such as hiring, raising prices, or stepping back.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = worst case unknown; 5 = worst case, prevention, repair and cost of inaction all written.
**Example:** an owner afraid to take two weeks off writes the worst case, sets up a manager and phone rule, and goes.

## Camcorder Method
**Sources:** [P14]; playbook thinking in [P08].
**What it is:** record a task once, then turn the recording into a written process for whoever takes it.
**When to use:** the owner says delegating takes longer than doing.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = no documentation; 5 = recorded, written, handed off, and reviewed once.
**Example:** a store owner screen-records the weekly ordering routine; a new hire runs it within two weeks.

## Effective Decisions
**Sources:** [P13].
**What it is:** few but sound decisions: classify the problem (generic or unique), define boundary conditions, decide what is right before what is acceptable, build in action, test against results.
**When to use:** a big or recurring decision.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = decided on the spot; 3 = boundary conditions written; 5 = right answer, compromise, action owner and review date all written.
**Example:** an owner facing repeat late deliveries writes one policy instead of fixing each case.
