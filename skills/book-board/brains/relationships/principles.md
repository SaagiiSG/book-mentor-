# Principles: Relationships and Leadership department

Decision rules built from the 17 Relationships and Leadership books (R01 to R17), each with its sources and a confidence note, then the places where the books disagree. Quote policy: verbatim quotes appear only from the author talks listed in talks.md, always with a T id; copyrighted book text is never quoted; everything else is paraphrase cited by book id.

Confidence: "strong" means four or more books clearly agree, "moderate" means two or three, "single source" means one book, and "synthesis" means our own reading across books.

## Decision rules

### Trust and safety
1. Trust comes first; without it, conflict turns into politics and nothing else on a team works. [R05, R14, R15, R12] Strong.
2. The leader goes first in admitting mistakes, gaps and needs; others follow only after. [R05, R15, R06, R13] Strong.
3. Leaders set the level of internal danger. Remove fear of blame and favoritism before asking people to take risks. [R14, R15, R12] Moderate.
4. Leaders give up perks and take the first hit in hard times; asking for sacrifice you do not share destroys trust. [R14, R09] Moderate.
5. Trust is built by giving time and attention, not money. [R14, R10, R15] Moderate.

### Hard conversations and feedback
6. If a team or relationship is stuck, look for the crucial conversation no one is holding, or holding badly. [R12, R13, R05] Moderate. "there are always crucial conversations we either not holding or not holding well that's the principle the diagnostic principle for us as Leaders" (T130)
7. Silence is not neutral; an unspoken problem shows up in behavior. [R12, R13] Moderate.
8. Honesty and respect are not a trade-off. Refuse the choice between telling the truth and keeping the relationship. [R12, R13, R01] Moderate.
9. Establish shared purpose and respect before content; people react to your perceived intent, not your words. [R12, R16, R01] Moderate.
10. Ask for feedback before you give it, and act on what you hear. [R13, R05, R15] Moderate.
11. Give feedback small, soon, specific and private; praise in public; never about personality. [R13, R01, R08] Moderate.
12. Separate facts from the story you tell about them before you accuse anyone. [R12, R04] Moderate.
13. Handle emotional or job-related news in person or by phone, never only by text. [R14, R16, R09, R13] Strong.

### Communication
14. Match the kind of conversation the other person is having (practical, emotional, identity) before moving on. [R16, R12] Moderate.
15. Prove you have heard someone before you argue: loop back what they said and check it. [R16, R12, R01] Moderate.
16. Never argue to win; a won argument costs goodwill. Demonstrate, ask, and let them own the idea. [R01, R03, R04] Moderate.
17. Frame requests around what the other person wants, not what you want. [R01, R03, R02] Moderate.

### Teams and decisions
18. Let everyone weigh in, then one person decides and everyone backs it; consensus is not required. [R05, R08, R06] Moderate.
19. The leader owns every result in their area; fix your part before blaming the team. [R06, R05, R09] Moderate.
20. Standards are what the leader tolerates; peer accountability needs a leader willing to confront. [R06, R05, R13] Moderate.
21. Explain intent and limits so people can decide without you; manage more closely only where they are new to the task. [R06, R08] Moderate.
22. Name and rank a few priorities and repeat them far more than feels necessary. [R15, R06] Moderate.
23. A manager's output is the team's output; spend time where it lifts many people, starting with one-on-ones and training. [R08, R13] Moderate.

### Hiring and firing
24. Hire against a written scorecard of outcomes with numbers and dates, not gut feel or a job description. [R07, R09] Moderate.
25. Judge character by patterns over time, especially how people treat juniors and peers. [R04, R11, R07] Moderate.
26. Do not hire friends for results roles without the same scorecard and checks as anyone else. [R03, R07] Moderate (R03's point as Greene narrows it in talks).
27. Act fast on a wrong person in a key seat, deliver the news yourself, and treat them with respect. [R09, R07, R12] Moderate.
28. Avoided decisions and quick people fixes pile up as debt that costs more later. [R09, R13] Moderate.

### Relationships and influence
29. Give before you ask and do not keep score; reciprocity follows real generosity. [R10, R11, R02] Moderate.
30. Give in ways that protect your own goals and energy; selfless giving burns out and feeds takers. [R11] Single source.
31. Build relationships before you need them, and do not drop people when they are down. [R10, R11] Moderate.
32. Use persuasion principles only truthfully; fake scarcity, proof or authority costs more than it wins, fastest in a small market. [R02, R11] Moderate.
33. Learn power tactics to read and defend, not to deceive. Deception, credit-taking and playing on insecurity damage trust in a small team. [R03, R04, R14, R11] Synthesis, moderate.
34. Pause before reacting to people, and take the emotion out of big people decisions. [R04, R12] Moderate.
35. People differ in how they handle closeness and distance; state needs plainly and agree contact rhythms instead of guessing. [R17, R16] Single source plus synthesis; use with care, never as a label.

## Where the books disagree

**1. Read and play power versus build safety and trust: Greene [R03, R04] versus Sinek, Coyle and Lencioni [R14, R15, R05].** R03 says the game is always on and naive people get hurt; it teaches concealment, appearance and saying less. R14, R15 and R05 say great teams run on safety, openness and leaders who go first in vulnerability. *When each is right:* inside your own small team, openness wins nearly every time, because people talk and trust is your main asset. With outside rivals, a large client's politics, or someone who has shown bad faith, Greene's lens helps you see moves and protect yourself. Use R03 to read, not to run your team.

**2. Warmth and rapport versus direct challenge: Carnegie [R01] versus Scott and Patterson et al. [R13, R12].** R01 says avoid criticism and arguments, raise mistakes indirectly and let people save face. R13 says most managers fail by being too nice, and R12 says say the hard thing clearly. *When each is right:* Carnegie's methods suit proud people, long-serving staff, partners and first conversations. When a problem is repeated, costly or the person does not know it exists, direct candor is kinder. Use Carnegie for the how (private, with appreciation, face-saving) and Scott for the whether (say it, and soon).

**3. Give freely versus guard your interests: Ferrazzi and Grant [R10, R11] versus Greene [R03].** R10 and R11 say lead with generosity and do not keep score. R03 warns against trusting too easily and says appeal to self-interest, not gratitude. *When each is right:* generosity is the better default in networks and repeat relationships, where reputation compounds. Grant's own nuance meets Greene halfway: once someone shows they are a taker, match them rather than keep giving. In one-off, high-stakes deals with unknown people, guard your interests.

**4. Leader decides versus push decisions down: Horowitz [R09] versus Willink and Babin [R06].** R09's wartime CEO narrows focus and directs closely; R09 also warns founders against over-deferring. R06 says even in combat, leaders should explain intent and let juniors decide. *When each is right:* in a true crisis, or when the owner has been polling everyone to avoid a call, decide. For routine work with capable people, push decisions down with clear intent and limits. Task-relevant maturity [R08] tells you how far.

**5. Hire top talent versus build the people you have: Smart and Street and Horowitz [R07, R09] versus Scott and Grove [R13, R08].** R07 says hire people who can hit demanding outcomes and avoid fixing weak hires; Horowitz says in a talk that CEOs cannot develop executives. R13 and R08 put heavy weight on coaching, career conversations and training. *When each is right:* for senior and key roles, hire the capability you need, because development is slow and costly. For frontline and early-career roles, training and coaching pay back well. A small business usually cannot afford top-tier pay everywhere.

**6. Act fast on people problems versus understand first: Horowitz and Smart [R09, R07] versus Patterson et al. and Duhigg [R12, R16].** R09 says avoided firings and decisions become debt. R12 and R16 say first find out what is really happening and whether the person even knows. *When each is right:* if the person has had clear, specific feedback and the pattern holds, act. If no one has ever said it plainly, hold the crucial conversation first; firing someone who was never told is the owner's failure, not theirs.

**7. Leaders sacrifice for the team versus leaders own the result: Sinek [R14] versus Willink and Babin and Grove [R06, R08].** R14 frames leadership as protecting people and putting them first. R06 and R08 frame it as owning outcomes and output, including high standards. *When each is right:* these mostly fit together, but they pull apart when protecting someone means tolerating weak work. Safety is for honest effort and admitted mistakes, not for repeated poor performance; keeping a weak performer hurts the team you are protecting.

**8. Plan your relationships versus let them be natural: Ferrazzi [R10] versus Carnegie and Duhigg [R01, R16].** R10 advises research, lists and premeditated generosity. R01 and R16 stress sincere interest and real curiosity. *When each is right:* planning who to meet and how to help is sensible for a busy owner. It turns sour when the plan replaces sincere interest. Prepare like Ferrazzi, then listen like Duhigg. "premeditated doesn't mean fake you know just because I'm planned and prepared doesn't mean I'm fake it just means that it's that important that I'm planned and prepared" (T127)

**9. Influence as technique versus influence as character: Cialdini [R02] versus Grant and Sinek [R11, R14].** R02 shows reliable triggers that move people. R11 and R14 say lasting influence comes from being a giver and a trustworthy leader. *When each is right:* Cialdini's principles help a good message land and help you defend against misuse. They do not repair a relationship where you have been a taker. Fix the character problem first, then use the techniques honestly.
