# Books: Relationships and Leadership department

Built from the 17 Relationships and Leadership books in BOOKS.md (R01 to R17), one entry each in id order, merged from the old Sales and Growth brains and the author-talk notes listed in talks.md. Quote policy: verbatim quotes appear only from author talks, always with a T id; copyrighted book text is never quoted, and everything else is paraphrase cited by book id.

## [R01] How to Win Friends and Influence People, Dale Carnegie (1936)

**Core idea.** People act from their own interests and a deep need to feel important. Criticism makes them defend themselves, not change. Show real interest, avoid arguments and let people own ideas, and they cooperate far more than when you push.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Do not criticize, condemn or complain:** criticism triggers defence and resentment, so start from understanding.
- **Become genuinely interested in other people:** listen, remember names, talk in terms of their interests, and make them feel important, sincerely.
- **Arouse an eager want:** frame any request around what the other person wants, not what you want.
- **Win people to your way of thinking:** avoid arguments, never tell someone flatly they are wrong, admit your own mistakes quickly, start friendly, and let the other person do most of the talking.
- **Let the idea be theirs:** people back what they feel they helped create.
- **Change people without resentment:** begin with honest appreciation, raise mistakes indirectly, mention your own errors first, ask questions instead of giving orders, let people save face, and praise each improvement.

**Use it when.** Building a relationship with a partner or key hire, repairing a damaged working relationship, correcting staff without a fight, or handling a proud stakeholder.

**Watch out.** Dated examples and tone. Used cynically it reads as flattery, and the book warns against insincere praise. It is weak on telling hard truths, so pair it with Crucial Conversations [R12] or Radical Candor [R13] when a problem must be named directly.

## [R02] Influence, Robert Cialdini (1984, expanded 2021)

**Core idea.** People use mental shortcuts to decide fast, and a small set of principles reliably moves them toward yes. Knowing them helps you persuade honestly and spot when they are used on you.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **The principles:** reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity, and unity (added in the 2021 edition).
- **Go first with reciprocity:** give something useful, ideally unexpected, before you ask. In a talk he says to ask "whom can I most help here" in a new setting.
- **Consistency:** people want to match what they have already said or done, especially in public, so a small spoken commitment makes the larger yes more likely.
- **Social proof** is strongest when the others are similar to us and we are uncertain.
- **Liking** grows from real similarities and real compliments.
- **Defence:** notice the automatic yes, and ask whether the trigger is real (true scarcity, real expertise) or manufactured.

**In the author's words:** "the key is to go first with reciprocity it's the opposite of the usual commercial exchange sequence" (T078). "it is the desire to be consistent with what we have already said or done especially in public" (T078)

**Use it when.** Getting buy-in from staff or partners, asking for introductions, making a change stick through small public commitments, or checking whether someone is working on you.

**Watch out.** Some studies in this tradition have faced replication questions. Fake scarcity or fake proof destroys trust fast in a small business where people talk. Sales-page uses belong to Business.

## [R03] The 48 Laws of Power, Robert Greene (1998)

**Core idea.** Power is a social game as old as royal courts, and people who pretend not to play it get played. Greene draws 48 laws from history, each with stories of people who kept or broke it and a reversal for when the opposite works. In talks he says power starts with self-control, not domination.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Never outshine the master (Law 1):** make those above you feel secure. Greene says breaking this got him fired.
- **Do not put too much trust in friends (Law 2):** in a talk he narrows this to work: hiring a friend lets emotion cloud a results decision.
- **Conceal your intentions (Law 3):** he says this depends on the audience; share the vision with your team, guard it from rivals.
- **Say less than necessary (Law 4)** and **guard your reputation (Law 5).**
- **Win through actions, not argument (Law 9).**
- **Appeal to self-interest, not mercy or gratitude (Law 13).**

**In the author's words:** "the laws are never meant to apply to every situation" (T099). "the feeling of powerlessness is actually more corrupting than the feeling of having a lot of power" (T099)

**Use it when.** Reading office politics, a board, a large client's internal rivals, or a partner who is playing games; checking whether you are naive about someone's motives.

**Watch out.** Amoral by design. Teach it as a way to read power and defend yourself. In a small business, laws built on deception (concealing intent from your own team, taking credit for others' work, using friends as tools, playing on someone's insecurity, crushing a rival totally) wreck the trust that a small team runs on. Greene himself says context is everything.

## [R04] The Laws of Human Nature, Robert Greene (2018)

**Core idea.** People are driven by deep, mostly unconscious forces they rarely see in themselves. Greene sets out 18 laws so you can first master your own reactions and then read others more calmly and accurately.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Irrationality:** emotion steers decisions. Leave a gap before reacting, and strip emotion out of big decisions, including the habit of imagining only the best case.
- **Narcissism and empathy:** everyone sits somewhere on a self-absorption spectrum; turn attention outward to read others.
- **Role-playing:** people wear masks; read tone, gesture and other nonverbal cues.
- **Compulsive behavior:** judge character by repeated patterns over time, not by words or first impressions.
- **The shadow and defensiveness:** people hide a darker side that leaks under stress, and they resist being told; confirm their good opinion of themselves before asking them to change.
- **Conformity and group culture:** groups spread emotion by contagion, so culture shapes behavior.

**In the author's words:** "we humans are deeply irrational and what I mean by that is that we are governed by our emotions more than anything else" (T100). "that space that time that 10 minutes that one hour that you don't react is actually what's going to make you a more rational person in the long run" (T100)

**Use it when.** Judging a candidate or partner's character, handling a difficult staff member, managing your own reactions in a tense meeting, or reading group mood.

**Watch out.** Long and literary, built on historical stories rather than studies. Figures such as the share of behavior that is unconscious are estimates Greene repeats, not measurements. Like R03, it can be read as a manipulation manual; use it to understand and protect.

## [R05] The Five Dysfunctions of a Team, Patrick Lencioni (2002)

**Core idea.** A fable about a new CEO fixing a broken leadership team. Teams fail through five linked dysfunctions, each built on the one below, starting with a lack of trust.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **The pyramid:** absence of trust, fear of conflict, lack of commitment, avoidance of accountability, inattention to results.
- **Vulnerability-based trust:** people can say "I don't know", "I need help" and "I'm sorry"; this differs from predicting how each other will act. The leader goes first.
- **Productive conflict:** open debate about ideas, not people.
- **Commitment without consensus:** everyone weighs in, then the leader decides (disagree and commit).
- **Peer accountability:** the best accountability is between peers, which needs a leader willing to confront.
- **Team results over individual or department goals.**

**In the author's words:** "I fundamentally believe that if people don't weigh in they don't buy in" (T020). "without vulnerability based trust conflict becomes politics I'm trying to manipulate you to win but with trust conflict is nothing but the pursuit of Truth or the best possible answer" (T020)

**Use it when.** A leadership team avoids hard topics, decisions get reopened, managers protect their departments, or meetings are polite and useless.

**Watch out.** A fable with no formal research behind it. Fixes take months and a leader willing to go first. In a five-person firm, "the leadership team" may just be the owner and one manager.

## [R06] Extreme Ownership, Jocko Willink and Leif Babin (2015)

**Core idea.** Two former Navy SEAL officers argue that leaders own everything in their world. Every failure is theirs to own and fix, and blame passed down or sideways kills a team.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Extreme Ownership:** no excuses; the leader owns the mission, the plan and the outcome.
- **No bad teams, only bad leaders:** standards are what the leader tolerates.
- **Believe in the mission:** a leader must understand the why before they can explain it.
- **Check the ego:** pride blocks honest review.
- **Cover and Move:** teams support each other toward the shared goal instead of winning their own piece.
- **Simple; Prioritize and Execute; Decentralized Command:** plans everyone understands; under pressure, work the top problem first; explain intent and limits so junior leaders can decide.
- **Lead up the chain as well as down:** if your boss does not give you what you need, explain better.

**In the author's words:** "in a leadership position you're not worried about yourself getting hurt or killed you're worried about your guys getting hurt or killed" (T023)

**Use it when.** Managers blame each other, a crisis has too many fires, or every decision funnels to the owner.

**Watch out.** Military stories make business look simpler than it is. Ownership can slide into a leader doing every task or taking blame for things truly outside their control. The available interview does not discuss the book's frameworks.

## [R07] Who, Geoff Smart and Randy Street (2008)

**Core idea.** Hiring is the biggest management problem, and most firms hire on gut feel. Smart says managers get hires wrong about half the time. The A Method gives a structured process for hiring people who can hit the outcomes the role needs.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Voodoo hiring:** common bad habits such as trusting charm, grilling candidates, or hiring and then firing.
- **The A Method:** Scorecard, Source, Select, Sell; four steps, none skipped.
- **Scorecard:** mission, outcomes and competencies, kept separate. Outcomes are numbers with dates and matter most. A scorecard is not a job description, and experience is not a competency.
- **Source:** referrals from your network and staff as the top channel.
- **Select:** a screening interview, an in-depth career history interview, focused interviews, and reference calls.
- **Sell:** address fit, family, freedom, fortune and fun.

**In the author's words:** "job descriptions are vague scorecards are specific" (T019)

**Use it when.** Hiring any key role, after a run of bad hires, or before promoting from within.

**Watch out.** Time-heavy for a small firm; scale it down rather than skip it. Smart's figure of a mistake costing 15 times salary comes from his own consulting estimates, not independent research. "A player" talk can turn elitist or biased if the scorecard is weak.

## [R08] High Output Management, Andrew S. Grove (1983)

**Core idea.** A manager's output is the output of the team they run plus the teams they influence. Grove applies production thinking to management: find the limiting step, measure with good indicators, and spend time where it gives the most leverage.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Managerial leverage:** choose activities that raise many people's output, such as training, decisions and fixing processes.
- **One-on-ones:** regular meetings driven by the report's agenda, for teaching and information.
- **Task-relevant maturity:** manage closely when someone is new to a task and loosely once they have mastered it.
- **Decision-making:** free discussion, a clear decision, then full support, including from those who argued against it.
- **Indicators:** leading indicators, and paired indicators that stop people gaming one number.
- **Training is the manager's job,** and performance reviews are among the most important things a manager does.

**Use it when.** Building a first management layer, setting up meetings, running one-on-ones, or measuring a team.

**Watch out.** 1980s Intel. Some parts (dual reporting, large meeting structures) suit only bigger firms. No verified author talk on this book is available here.

## [R09] The Hard Thing About Hard Things, Ben Horowitz (2014)

**Core idea.** There is no recipe for the hardest leadership problems: layoffs, firing friends, demoting loyal people, near-death moments. Horowitz shares how he made such calls and what he learned.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Take care of the people, the products and the profits, in that order.**
- **Peacetime versus wartime CEO:** in peace, broaden and empower; in war, focus hard and direct.
- **Lay people off and fire executives the right way:** move fast, own the decision, tell people yourself, and treat them with respect.
- **Management debt:** quick people fixes (special pay deals, two people in one job, no training) that cost more later. In talks he adds decision debt.
- **Hiring executives:** know what you want before you look; founders hire the sales head worst; take blind references.
- **Bad news must travel fast:** a culture that hides truth to spare feelings is dangerous.

**In the author's words:** "Decision debt is the worst debt, by the way, cuz it it paralyzes a company." (T010) "if you're running away from the truth to preserve feelings, that's a very dangerous thing in a tech company. And the kind of corollary to that is it's really important that like bad news travels fast." (T010)

**Use it when.** Firing or demoting a leader, cutting staff, making a 52/48 call you keep avoiding, or hiring above your own experience.

**Watch out.** Venture-backed Silicon Valley context. Much assumes boards, investors and big teams. The owner's own stress in hard times belongs to Mindset or Psychology and Mental Health.

## [R10] Never Eat Alone, Keith Ferrazzi (2005)

**Core idea.** Relationships open doors, and the way to build them is generosity before any ask. Networking done well is not collecting contacts or trading favors; it is helping people and staying in touch so that trust exists before you need it.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Do not keep score:** lead with generosity; transactionalism is the biggest mistake.
- **Build it before you need it:** relationships take time, so start long before the ask.
- **Do your homework:** know what the person cares about and plan how you can help. Planned is not fake.
- **Follow up and keep pinging:** short, regular contact keeps ties warm.
- **Conference tactics:** go with a wingman, host a dinner around one anchor guest, and reach speakers before they talk.
- **Go deep, not small talk:** ask what people hope for and struggle with, and share your own struggle first.
- **Do not drop people when they are down.**

**In the author's words:** "you're you're trying to build a relationship with me and you're leading with a bargain so transactionalism is probably one of the biggest mistakes" (T127). "premeditated doesn't mean fake you know just because I'm planned and prepared doesn't mean I'm fake it just means that it's that important that I'm planned and prepared" (T127)

**Use it when.** Building a network from scratch, finding partners or investors, getting introductions, or preparing for an event.

**Watch out.** Written for an ambitious corporate climber; the pace of dinners and events can be unrealistic for a busy owner, and the tactics can feel calculated if the generosity is not real.

## [R11] Give and Take, Adam Grant (2013)

**Core idea.** People lean toward one of three reciprocity styles: takers, givers and matchers. Givers fill both the bottom and the top of success rankings. The ones at the top give in ways that protect their own interests and avoid takers.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Three styles:** takers get as much as they can, matchers keep an even balance, givers help without counting. Most people are matchers.
- **Givers at both ends:** in his data, givers had the worst and the best results; takers and matchers sat in the middle.
- **Otherish versus selfless giving:** successful givers keep their own goals in view; selfless givers burn out.
- **Five-minute favors and chunking:** small, high-value help, and batching giving so it has visible effect (detail uncertain on exact wording).
- **Spotting fakers:** takers kiss up and kick down, so ask peers and juniors, not only bosses.
- **Dormant ties:** old contacts you have lost touch with are often valuable and easy to revive.
- **Powerless communication:** asking questions, seeking advice and showing some vulnerability can build influence.

**In the author's words:** "these reciprocity styles are not hardwired they're not fixed in fact they're choices we make in every single interaction" (T128). "the givers are not only over represented at the bottom they're also more common at the top" (T128)

**Use it when.** Building a network, designing team culture and rewards, screening hires for taker behavior, or when the owner gives until they are exhausted.

**Watch out.** Built on a mix of studies and stories; some cited effects are from single studies. Grant does not say giving always pays. The burnout of over-givers may need Psychology and Mental Health.

## [R12] Crucial Conversations, Kerry Patterson, Joseph Grenny, Ron McMillan and Al Switzler (2002)

**Core idea.** A crucial conversation has high stakes, differing opinions and strong emotions, and these moments shape relationships and teams more than any others. Most people either go silent or turn aggressive. The book teaches how to stay in honest dialogue while keeping respect.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Start with heart:** ask what you really want for yourself, the other person and the relationship, and refuse the fool's choice between honesty and kindness.
- **Learn to look:** notice when you or they slip into silence or violence.
- **Make it safe:** restore mutual purpose and mutual respect; apologize when you hurt respect; use contrasting (what I do not mean, what I do mean).
- **Master your stories:** separate facts from the story you tell about them; watch for victim, villain and helpless stories.
- **STATE your path:** share facts, tell your story, ask for theirs, talk tentatively, encourage testing.
- **Explore their path and move to action:** ask, mirror, paraphrase, prime; then agree who does what by when, and follow up.

**In the author's words:** "people never get defensive with you because of what you're saying they get defensive with you because of why why they think you're saying it it's the intent not the content that provokes that defensiveness" (T130). "talk it out or act it out are the only two options available" (T130)

**Use it when.** Any high-stakes talk: a co-founder dispute, a performance problem, a partner who missed a promise, a family member in the business.

**Watch out.** The skills take practice and feel scripted at first. Grenny says it only works if your respect and shared purpose are real. Later editions renamed some chapters (detail uncertain). Grenny's 97 percent figure is about being heard, not about agreement.

## [R13] Radical Candor, Kim Scott (2017)

**Core idea.** Good bosses care personally and challenge directly at the same time. Most managers fail by caring without challenging. Candor starts with asking for feedback and covers praise as much as criticism.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **The 2x2:** radical candor (care and challenge), obnoxious aggression (challenge without care), ruinous empathy (care without challenge), manipulative insincerity (neither).
- **Get, give and encourage guidance:** ask for criticism first, then give it, then get the team giving it to each other.
- **How to give it:** humble, helpful, immediate, in person, praise in public and criticize in private, never about personality.
- **Content of feedback:** context, what you observed, the result, and the next step.
- **Growth trajectories:** some people want steep growth and some want steady excellence; both are valuable, and both need the right role.
- **Career conversations:** three talks covering the person's past, their dreams, and an action plan.

**In the author's words:** "what happens when we remember to challenge directly but we forget to show that we care personally that is what I call obnoxious aggression" (T131). "I would say 90% of us make 90% of our mistakes in that ruinous empathy bucket" (T131)

**Use it when.** A manager avoids giving feedback, a team is polite but stuck, someone is struggling and does not know it, or the owner never hears bad news.

**Watch out.** Often misread as permission to be blunt, which Scott calls obnoxious aggression. Rooted in Google and Apple; small, close teams need extra care because relationships carry more weight. The 90 percent figure is her estimate.

## [R14] Leaders Eat Last, Simon Sinek (2014)

**Core idea.** People do their best work when they feel safe inside their group. Leaders decide how big that circle of safety is, and they earn their status by giving up perks and taking on risk for the people in their care.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **The circle of safety:** external dangers are constant; internal dangers are set by leaders. Extend safety and belonging to everyone.
- **The cost of leadership is self-interest:** leaders get perks because we expect them to run toward danger.
- **Four feel-good chemicals:** endorphins and dopamine (individual achievement), serotonin and oxytocin (status, trust and belonging).
- **Generosity of time and energy:** trust comes from giving time and attention, not money.
- **Abstraction:** as organizations grow, people become numbers, and leaders must keep them human.
- **Use the right medium:** email for information; walk over or call for anything emotional.

**In the author's words:** "inside our organizations the dangers we Face are not a constant they are a variable and they are the decisions of leadership as to how safe they make us feel when we go to work" (T132). "leaders are the ones who give us their time and give us their energy not the ones who give us their money it doesn't count" (T132)

**Use it when.** Staff do not trust the owner, layoffs or pay cuts are coming, or the owner keeps perks while asking for sacrifice.

**Watch out.** The brain-chemistry account is a heavy simplification, and some claims (about generations and dopamine) are Sinek's own. It is light on practical steps; pair with R08 or R13.

## [R15] The Culture Code, Daniel Coyle (2018)

**Core idea.** Strong group cultures are built from three skills: build safety, share vulnerability and establish purpose. They come from small, repeated signals, not values posters.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Build safety:** belonging cues (eye contact, attention, turn-taking, signals that the relationship will continue) tell people they are safe here.
- **Overcommunicate that you are listening,** say thank you often, and welcome the messenger who brings bad news.
- **Spotlight your own fallibility early:** leaders who admit gaps invite others to speak.
- **Share vulnerability:** a vulnerability loop (one person admits a weakness, another responds in kind) builds cooperation; the leader goes first; honest after-action reviews.
- **Establish purpose:** name and rank priorities, use simple catchphrases and heuristics, and tell the story of where the group is going.
- **One bad apple:** a single person who shows contempt or disengagement can drag down a group (from a study Coyle describes; detail uncertain on effect size).

**Use it when.** Building culture as a team grows, onboarding new people, fixing a team where no one speaks up, or a new manager taking over.

**Watch out.** Built on case studies of elite groups (sports teams, special forces, top studios) and selected research; it shows what strong cultures do, not proof that copying them works. No verified author talk on this book is available here, so no quotes.

## [R16] Supercommunicators, Charles Duhigg (2024)

**Core idea.** The goal of communication is to feel connected, not only to swap information. Every discussion holds three kinds of conversation, practical, emotional and social, and people only hear each other when they are in the same kind at the same moment.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Three conversations:** what is this really about (practical), how do we feel (emotional), who are we (social and identity).
- **The matching principle:** notice which conversation the other person is having and match it before moving on together.
- **Ask deep questions:** about values, beliefs and experiences, offered as invitations.
- **Looping for understanding:** ask, repeat back in your own words, then ask if you got it right. It shows understanding, not agreement.
- **Vulnerability:** share something you could be judged for; it builds closeness when the other side responds.
- **Medium matters:** bad news face to face or by phone; online, more politeness and less sarcasm.

**In the author's words:** "successful communication requires having the same kind of conversation at the same moment." (T134). "Step three is that after you summarize what they said, you ask them if you got it right." (T134)

**Use it when.** A conversation keeps going in circles, a partner or employee feels unheard, a heated meeting needs calming, or an owner tries to solve feelings with fixes.

**Watch out.** Popular-science style with vivid stories; the 10 to 80 percent figure Duhigg gives for looping is a wide range from his talk, not a precise finding. Skills look simple and take practice.

## [R17] Attached, Amir Levine and Rachel Heller (2010)

**Core idea.** Adults bond in patterns that research calls attachment styles: secure, anxious and avoidant. Knowing your style and your partner's explains a lot of conflict about closeness and availability. The book is about romantic relationships.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Three styles:** secure (comfortable with closeness), anxious (alert to signs of distance, wants reassurance), avoidant (values independence, pulls back when things get close).
- **Attachment as a safety system:** feeling safe with someone frees you to explore.
- **Activating and deactivating strategies:** anxious people turn up the alarm; avoidant people create distance.
- **Effective communication:** state your needs plainly and early rather than through protest behavior.
- **Not a pathology:** Levine says the question is whether a pattern is effective, not what is wrong with you.

**In the author's words:** "attachment is really a radar of availability of other people. It's a safety system." (T135). "the question is not about what's wrong and how we can cure it or heal it. It's more about is it effective or is it not effective." (T135)

**Use it when.** Only with care, and only as an analogy for how co-founders or partners react to distance and reassurance, or when the owner's close relationship is affecting the business.

**Watch out.** Written for romantic partners, not workplaces; never label staff or co-founders with a style. Real attachment research is more nuanced than three boxes. Anything clinical, such as anxiety, trauma or a relationship in crisis, goes to Psychology and Mental Health.
