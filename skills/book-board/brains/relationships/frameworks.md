# Frameworks: Relationships and Leadership department

Built from the 17 Relationships and Leadership books (R01 to R17), most useful first. Names are the books' names where they have one; explanations are ours. Scores are guidance for the advisor, not from the books. Quote policy: verbatim quotes appear only from the author talks listed in talks.md, always with a T id; copyrighted book text is never quoted; everything else is paraphrase cited by book id.

## 1. Crucial Conversation Prep (Start with Heart, Make It Safe)
**Sources:** [R12], with intent in [R13] and matching in [R16].
**What it is:** getting your own motive and the other person's sense of safety right before you raise a high-stakes issue.
**Steps:** 1) Write what you really want for yourself, for them and for the relationship. 2) Reject the fool's choice: find a way to be honest and respectful at once. 3) Open with mutual purpose: what you both care about. 4) Show mutual respect in the first minute. 5) If they go silent or attack, stop the content and rebuild safety (apologize, or contrast what you do and do not mean).
**When to use:** co-founder disputes, a performance problem, a missed promise, a family member in the business.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = you walk in to win or vent; 3 = clear goal, no plan for safety; 5 = written goal for all three, an opening line of shared purpose, and a contrast line ready.
**Example:** an owner about to confront a manager over late reports opens with "I want you to succeed in this role and I want the team to trust our numbers," then states the facts.
**In the author's words:** "people never get defensive with you because of what you're saying they get defensive with you because of why why they think you're saying it it's the intent not the content that provokes that defensiveness" (T130)

## 2. Master Your Story and STATE Your Path
**Sources:** [R12].
**What it is:** separating facts from the story you have told yourself, then sharing your view in a way that invites challenge.
**Steps:** 1) List the facts only (what a camera would record). 2) Name the story you are telling (victim, villain, helpless?). 3) Ask what a reasonable person might have been thinking. 4) Share facts first, then your tentative story. 5) Ask for their view and genuinely test yours. 6) End with who does what by when, and a follow-up date.
**When to use:** you feel certain the other person is acting in bad faith; emails getting heated.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = you lead with conclusions about their character; 3 = facts first but no question; 5 = facts, tentative story, real question, and a written next step.
**Example:** "The last three invoices went out after the 10th. I'm starting to wonder if month-end isn't a priority for you. Is something else going on?"

## 3. The Radical Candor 2x2
**Sources:** [R13], with directness in [R12] and kindness in [R01].
**What it is:** a compass for feedback with two axes: care personally and challenge directly.
**Parts:** radical candor (both), obnoxious aggression (challenge without care), ruinous empathy (care without challenge), manipulative insincerity (neither).
**Steps:** 1) Place your last three hard messages on the grid. 2) If you are in ruinous empathy, name the thing you have been avoiding. 3) If you are in aggression, add the care first, in words and in private. 4) Use the grid on your own behavior, never to label people.
**When to use:** feedback never happens, or it happens only as blow-ups.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = months of unspoken issues; 3 = feedback given but late or harsh; 5 = small, frequent, specific feedback both ways.
**Example:** a studio owner realizes she has said nothing for six months about a designer's missed deadlines and schedules the talk this week.
**In the author's words:** "I would say 90% of us make 90% of our mistakes in that ruinous empathy bucket" (T131)

## 4. Get, Give, Encourage Guidance
**Sources:** [R13], with weighing in from [R05] and the leader going first in [R15].
**What it is:** a sequence for building a feedback culture: ask for criticism first, then give it, then get peers giving it to each other.
**Steps:** 1) Ask a specific question in your own words, like what you could do or stop doing to make working with you easier. 2) Sit through the silence. 3) Reward the answer by acting on it visibly. 4) Then give feedback: context, what you saw, the result, the next step. 5) Praise in public, criticize in private, never about personality. 6) Teach the team to do the same with each other.
**When to use:** a new manager, a team that never raises problems, an owner who hears no bad news.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = owner never asks; 3 = asks, but people say "all good"; 5 = staff bring criticism unprompted and see changes.
**Example:** a café owner asks each shift lead "what's one thing I do that makes your shift harder?" and changes the rota-posting day the next week.
**In the author's words:** "radical caner is not all about the boss giving the employee criticism it should always start with soliciting feedback" (T131)

## 5. Looping for Understanding
**Sources:** [R16], with listening in [R01] and paraphrasing in [R12].
**What it is:** a three-step way to prove you have heard someone.
**Steps:** 1) Ask a question. 2) Repeat back what you heard, in your own words. 3) Ask if you got it right, and loop again if not.
**When to use:** someone feels unheard, a disagreement keeps circling, a customer or staff member is upset.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = you rebut before they finish; 3 = you summarize but skip the check; 5 = they say "yes, exactly" before you give your view.
**Example:** a partner says the business is taking over his life. The founder loops: "So the late calls are eating your evenings and you feel it's expected of you. Is that right?" He adds it is also about not being asked.
**In the author's words:** "Step three is that after you summarize what they said, you ask them if you got it right." (T134)

## 6. The Three Conversations and the Matching Principle
**Sources:** [R16], with emotion first in [R12].
**What it is:** sorting what kind of conversation is happening: practical (what is this really about), emotional (how do we feel) or social (who are we).
**Steps:** 1) Listen for the kind: plans and problems, feelings, or identity and status. 2) Match it before moving: do not solve a feeling. 3) Ask which they want ("Do you want ideas, or do you want to vent?"). 4) Move together to the next kind once the first is met.
**When to use:** a talk goes in circles; fixes are rejected; identity issues (title, respect, belonging) hide under practical complaints.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = you answer feelings with solutions; 3 = you notice the mismatch afterward; 5 = you name the kind and match it in the moment.
**Example:** a manager complains about a new hire's title. The owner realizes this is a "who are we" talk about status, not a practical one, and talks about the manager's role first.
**In the author's words:** "there are these practical conversations where the goal is for us to make plans or to solve problems together. But then there's emotional conversations where I tell you what I'm feeling and I don't want you to solve my feelings." (T134)

## 7. The Five Dysfunctions Pyramid
**Sources:** [R05], with safety in [R14, R15].
**What it is:** a diagnostic for teams: absence of trust, fear of conflict, lack of commitment, avoidance of accountability, inattention to results. Each causes the next.
**Steps:** 1) Find the lowest broken level. 2) Build trust: the leader admits a mistake or gap first. 3) Invite open debate on ideas, and mine for disagreement. 4) Let everyone weigh in, then decide clearly. 5) Peers hold each other to standards, not only the boss. 6) Put team results above department goals.
**When to use:** polite meetings with no change, decisions that get reopened, managers who protect their turf.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = no trust, no one admits mistakes; 3 = debate but weak follow-through; 5 = trust, debate, commitment, peer accountability and shared results all visible.
**Example:** a family firm's leaders agree to name one disagreement per meeting and log each decision so it is not reopened.
**In the author's words:** "the kind of trust that makes a team great great is what I call vulnerability based trust" (T020)

## 8. Disagree and Commit (Weigh In, Then Decide)
**Sources:** [R05, R08].
**What it is:** a decision process where everyone is heard, one person decides, and everyone backs the decision fully.
**Steps:** 1) Name who decides before the debate. 2) Ask each person for their view, quiet ones first. 3) Decide by a stated date. 4) Say the decision and reasons out loud. 5) Ask each person to commit, including those who disagreed. 6) Do not reopen without new facts.
**When to use:** decisions drag on, or are made and then quietly undermined.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = no clear decider, decisions reopened; 3 = clear decider, little real debate; 5 = full debate, dated decision, visible support.
**Example:** a three-partner agency debates a price rise for one meeting, the managing partner decides, and all three tell clients the same story.
**In the author's words:** "I fundamentally believe that if people don't weigh in they don't buy in" (T020)

## 9. The A Method for Hiring
**Sources:** [R07], with executive hiring in [R09].
**What it is:** Scorecard, Source, Select, Sell.
**Steps:** 1) Write a scorecard: mission, three to five outcomes with numbers and dates, and competencies, kept separate. 2) Source through referrals; ask your team and network "who do you know?" 3) Screen, then run a career-history interview, then focused interviews. 4) Call references, including ones the candidate did not name. 5) Sell on fit, family, freedom, fortune and fun.
**When to use:** any key hire; after a run of bad hires.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = gut hire from a job ad; 3 = structured interview, no scorecard; 5 = scorecard, history interview and references done.
**Example:** an owner hiring an operations manager writes three outcomes (on-time delivery to 95 percent in six months, two supervisors trained, weekly report live) and rejects a charming candidate who cannot show similar results.
**In the author's words:** "job descriptions are vague scorecards are specific" (T019)

## 10. The Career-History Interview and Blind References
**Sources:** [R07], with blind references in [R09] and character by pattern in [R04].
**What it is:** a long, chronological interview that reveals patterns, backed by references you find yourself.
**Steps:** 1) For each past job ask what they were hired to do, what they achieved, what went wrong, who they worked with, and why they left. 2) Ask "how?" and "tell me more" until you hear specifics. 3) Tell them you will call past bosses; watch the reaction. 4) Call named references and at least one you found yourself. 5) Look for patterns across jobs, not one story.
**When to use:** a finalist for a key role; a senior hire above your own experience.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = résumé chat; 3 = history covered, no references; 5 = full history, named and blind references, pattern noted.
**Example:** a candidate's references all praise him, but a blind reference from a peer says he took credit for the team's work; the owner probes and passes.

## 11. One-on-Ones
**Sources:** [R08], with candor in [R13] and time as trust in [R14].
**What it is:** regular private meetings, driven by the report's agenda, for coaching, information and early warning.
**Steps:** 1) Schedule them: weekly for people new to a task, every two weeks or monthly for experienced ones. 2) The report brings the agenda. 3) The manager asks questions and teaches. 4) Ask for feedback on yourself. 5) Note follow-ups and check them next time.
**When to use:** building a first management layer; staff surprised at reviews; problems found too late.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = none; 3 = irregular status updates; 5 = regular, report-driven, coaching and two-way feedback.
**Example:** a new practice manager holds 30 minutes every two weeks with each lead, and the first sign of a resignation shows up three months early.

## 12. Task-Relevant Maturity
**Sources:** [R08], with decentralized command in [R06].
**What it is:** matching how closely you manage to a person's experience with this specific task.
**Steps:** 1) Rate the person's experience on the task, not overall. 2) Low: structure, checklists, frequent check-ins. 3) Medium: two-way discussion and support. 4) High: agree goals and monitor lightly. 5) Re-rate when the task changes.
**When to use:** delegating; a strong performer failing in a new role; an owner accused of micromanaging or of abandoning people.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = same style for everyone; 3 = adjusted sometimes; 5 = style matched to each person's task maturity.
**Example:** a great technician promoted to supervisor gets a weekly checklist and check-in for three months, then a lighter monthly review.

## 13. Extreme Ownership Review
**Sources:** [R06], with vulnerability first in [R05, R15].
**What it is:** the leader owns every result in their world and opens each review by naming their own part.
**Steps:** 1) After a failure, the leader says first what they did wrong (unclear plan, wrong priority, missing training). 2) Ask what each person could have done differently, without blame. 3) Fix the process, not the person, first. 4) Check standards: what has the leader been tolerating? 5) Record one change and who owns it.
**When to use:** blame between staff or departments, slipping standards, after a missed delivery or lost client.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = blame and excuses; 3 = owner owns it, managers do not; 5 = every leader owns outcomes and reviews are blame-free.
**Example:** after a botched install, a contractor tells the crew the schedule he set was too tight, then they agree a site checklist.
**In the author's words:** "as these guys got better and started to work together and the leadership started to step up and take command and and do a better job of leading then all of a sudden they the seals would start to beat the the opposing force" (T023)

## 14. Decentralized Command (Commander's Intent)
**Sources:** [R06], with task-relevant maturity in [R08].
**What it is:** leaders explain the goal, the why and the limits, so others can decide on the spot.
**Steps:** 1) Write the intent for each area in two sentences. 2) Set limits (money, risk, customers). 3) Check people can say the intent back. 4) Let them decide inside it. 5) Step in only when limits are crossed.
**When to use:** every decision funnels to the owner; managers wait for permission.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = all calls go up; 3 = some delegated, intent unclear; 5 = staff decide within clear intent and limits.
**Example:** a restaurant owner lets each manager comp a meal up to a set amount, with the intent that every unhappy guest leaves wanting to come back.

## 15. Circle of Safety Check
**Sources:** [R14], with belonging cues in [R15] and trust in [R05].
**What it is:** asking whether people inside the business feel protected from internal dangers (blame, favoritism, sudden cuts, public humiliation) so they can face external ones together.
**Steps:** 1) List what staff fear inside the business. 2) Ask whether leaders take the first hit in hard times. 3) Give time and attention, not just money. 4) Handle emotional news in person. 5) Extend the circle to the newest and lowest-paid staff.
**When to use:** low trust in the owner, hard times ahead, a perk gap between leaders and staff.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = staff fear each other or the owner; 3 = safe for insiders only; 5 = everyone, including new staff, believes leaders have their back.
**Example:** during a slow quarter, an owner cuts her own pay before asking staff to drop overtime, and tells each team in person.
**In the author's words:** "the cost of leadership is self-interest if you're not willing to give up your perks when it matters then you probably shouldn't get promoted you might be an authority but you will not be a leader" (T132)

## 16. Belonging Cues and the Vulnerability Loop
**Sources:** [R15], with vulnerability-based trust in [R05] and vulnerability in [R16].
**What it is:** the small signals that tell people they are safe and valued here, plus the habit of leaders admitting weakness so others can.
**Steps:** 1) Give full attention in conversations: eye contact, no phone. 2) Say thank you specifically and often. 3) Leaders name a mistake or gap early in meetings. 4) Thank the person who brings bad news. 5) Run short after-action reviews: what happened, why, what next time. 6) Show new people the relationship will last (plans, mentors, future work).
**When to use:** a team where no one speaks up; onboarding; a new manager taking over.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = mistakes hidden, messengers punished; 3 = some openness at the top; 5 = staff raise problems early and admit errors freely.
**Example:** a clinic director starts each monthly meeting with one thing she got wrong, and nurses start reporting near-misses.

## 17. Name and Rank the Priorities
**Sources:** [R15], with simplicity and Prioritize and Execute in [R06].
**What it is:** making purpose concrete by naming a few priorities in order, with simple phrases and rules of thumb people can use in the moment.
**Steps:** 1) Pick three to five priorities and rank them. 2) Turn each into a short phrase. 3) Add rules of thumb for common trade-offs ("safety before speed"). 4) Repeat them far more than feels needed. 5) Tell stories of people who lived them.
**When to use:** staff make conflicting choices; culture is drifting as the team grows.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = no stated priorities; 3 = values poster nobody uses; 5 = staff quote the ranked priorities when making calls.
**Example:** a cleaning company states "1) client safety, 2) on time, 3) spotless," and a crew skips a corner rather than leave a wet floor unsigned.

## 18. Giver, Taker, Matcher Screen
**Sources:** [R11], with character by pattern in [R04].
**What it is:** reading how people handle give and take, to protect the team from takers and support givers.
**Steps:** 1) Ask candidates and partners about times they helped others with no gain. 2) Check how they treat juniors and peers, not only bosses. 3) Watch for "I" over "we" and credit-taking. 4) Notice who helps without being asked. 5) Reward helping in reviews.
**When to use:** hiring, choosing a partner, deciding who to promote, a culture of credit-grabbing.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = judged by charm with bosses; 3 = some peer input; 5 = peers and juniors consulted, helping behavior tracked.
**Example:** a candidate impresses the owner, but the receptionist says he was rude to her; the owner digs deeper and finds the same pattern in references.
**In the author's words:** "most people it turns out if you look at the data are what I call matchers and a match of somebody who's tried to keep an even balance of give and take quidd proquo tit for Tad" (T128)

## 19. Otherish Giving and Five-Minute Favors
**Sources:** [R11], with generosity first in [R10].
**What it is:** giving in ways that help others a lot at low cost to you, while keeping your own goals in view.
**Steps:** 1) Offer small, high-value help: an introduction, feedback, a shared resource. 2) Batch giving into set times so it does not eat every day. 3) Give freely to givers and matchers; be generous but guarded with known takers (start by giving, then match). 4) Track your own energy; giving that exhausts you is a warning.
**When to use:** building a network; the owner who helps everyone and has no time left.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = never gives, or gives until burnt out; 3 = gives but scattered; 5 = regular, focused giving with clear limits.
**Example:** a consultant blocks Friday afternoon for introductions and quick advice calls, and says no to free full projects.

## 20. Generosity-First Networking
**Sources:** [R10], with reciprocity in [R02] and dormant ties in [R11].
**What it is:** building relationships by helping first, preparing for each meeting, and following up steadily.
**Steps:** 1) Write the goal: what you are trying to build in the next year. 2) List the people who matter to it, including old contacts. 3) Before each meeting, learn what they care about and plan two or three ways to help. 4) Go deep: ask what they hope for and struggle with. 5) Follow up within days, then keep light, regular contact. 6) Never lead with a bargain.
**When to use:** starting a business, entering a new market, looking for partners or investors.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = only reach out when you need something; 3 = active but transactional; 5 = steady giving and contact with a clear list.
**Example:** a new accountant lists 30 local business owners and lawyers, sends each a useful tax change note, and holds a small dinner each quarter.
**In the author's words:** "I always say that if I'm showing up uh to somebody in the club that I will show up with five packets of generosity pre-planned" (T127)

## 21. Honest Influence for Buy-In
**Sources:** [R02], with letting the idea be theirs in [R01].
**What it is:** using reciprocity, commitment, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity and unity truthfully to get staff or partners behind a change.
**Steps:** 1) Go first: give help or a concession. 2) Ask for a small, spoken or written commitment. 3) Show that similar people already did it. 4) Explain why the change comes from real expertise. 5) Find genuine common ground. 6) Use "we" language for shared identity.
**When to use:** rolling out a new process, asking for introductions, getting a partner to try something.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = orders only; 3 = one or two principles used; 5 = several used truthfully. Any fake use scores zero.
**Example:** an owner rolling out a new booking system asks each lead to say at the team meeting which day they will switch, and shares how a sister site cut double-bookings.

## 22. Persuasion and Power Defence Check
**Sources:** [R02, R03], with reading people in [R04].
**What it is:** spotting when someone is using influence or power tactics on you.
**Steps:** 1) Notice an automatic yes or sudden pressure. 2) Ask: is the scarcity real? Is the authority real? Did a gift come before a big ask? 3) Watch for flattery before a request, for being made to look small, or for selective honesty that disarms you. 4) Pause before agreeing. 5) Decide on the merits as if the trigger were absent.
**When to use:** negotiating with a partner, landlord or investor; a staff member who works the owner.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = you agree under pressure often; 3 = you spot tactics afterward; 5 = you name them in the moment and decide calmly.
**Example:** a supplier offers a free upgrade, then a "today only" three-year contract; the owner thanks them and decides next week.

## 23. Change Without Resentment
**Sources:** [R01], with save-face framing in [R04].
**What it is:** correcting people in ways that keep their pride intact.
**Steps:** 1) Start with honest appreciation. 2) Raise the mistake indirectly or as a question. 3) Mention your own similar mistake. 4) Ask rather than order. 5) Let them save face. 6) Praise each improvement, and give them a reputation to live up to.
**When to use:** correcting long-serving staff, proud partners or family members; when directness has caused a fight before.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = public criticism; 3 = private but blunt; 5 = private, specific, with a face-saving path and follow-up praise.
**Example:** an owner tells a senior chef "your sauces are why people come back; I made the same mistake with portion costs early on. How could we tighten them?"

## 24. Let the Idea Be Theirs
**Sources:** [R01], with weighing in from [R05] and confirming self-opinion in [R04].
**What it is:** leading people to a conclusion through questions so they own it.
**Steps:** 1) Frame the problem around what they want. 2) Ask questions that point to the answer. 3) Let them talk most. 4) Credit them with the idea in public.
**When to use:** a manager resists a change; a partner needs to own a decision.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = you announce and argue; 3 = you ask, then tell; 5 = they propose it and you credit them.
**Example:** instead of ordering a new rota, an owner asks the shift leads how they would cut weekend gaps, and adopts their plan.

## 25. Power Map
**Sources:** [R03], with reading people in [R04].
**What it is:** a lens for the politics around a decision: who holds power, who feels threatened, and what each person stands to gain or lose.
**Steps:** 1) List the people who can help or block. 2) For each, note their interest, their fear and their insecurity. 3) Make sure your plan does not make anyone senior look outshone. 4) Frame asks around their self-interest. 5) Say less than you know until you understand the field. 6) Read and defend; do not deceive.
**When to use:** a large client's internal rivals, a board, a family business with factions, a partner joining with their own agenda.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = you know only your contact; 3 = you know who decides but not what each wants; 5 = you know each person's interest and fear and your plan lets them win.
**Example:** a supplier to a hospital lets the head nurse present the joint plan to the director, so she gains status rather than feeling bypassed.
**In the author's words:** "everything in the book is context so when you take things out of context it's a little harder to understand" (T099)

## 26. Character by Pattern
**Sources:** [R04], with fakers in [R11] and career history in [R07].
**What it is:** judging people by repeated behavior over time, not first impressions or words.
**Steps:** 1) Note how they behave under stress and when they think no one important is watching. 2) Look for patterns across jobs, partners or deals. 3) Weigh actions over promises. 4) Watch for big self-image gaps (the shadow) that leak under pressure. 5) Give trust in steps as the pattern holds.
**When to use:** choosing a co-founder or partner, promoting someone, a charming new hire.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = trust on first impression; 3 = some checking; 5 = trust extended in stages against observed patterns.
**Example:** a founder considering a partner asks three people who worked under him, not beside him, and runs a small paid project first.

## 27. The Pause and the Rosy-Scenario Check
**Sources:** [R04], with mastering your stories in [R12].
**What it is:** leaving a gap before reacting to people, and stripping hope and fear from people decisions.
**Steps:** 1) When angry or hurt, wait (minutes for an email, a day for a big decision). 2) Ask what you are feeling and why. 3) Write the worst plausible case next to the best. 4) Decide on evidence, not on how much you like or dislike the person.
**When to use:** an angry reply to a partner, hiring someone you badly want, firing in anger.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = react on the spot; 3 = pause sometimes; 5 = a set rule (sleep on it) for all heated people decisions.
**Example:** an owner drafts a furious reply to a co-founder, waits until morning, and sends a request for a call instead.
**In the author's words:** "that space that time that 10 minutes that one hour that you don't react is actually what's going to make you a more rational person in the long run" (T100)

## 28. Firing and Layoffs Done Right
**Sources:** [R09], with respect in [R12] and safety in [R14].
**What it is:** making people cuts quickly, owning the decision, and treating everyone with dignity.
**Steps:** 1) Decide, and do not let it leak. 2) Cut once, deep enough, rather than in rounds. 3) The owner or direct manager tells each person in person. 4) Give clear reasons and fair terms. 5) Tell the remaining team the truth the same day. 6) Do not blame the people leaving.
**When to use:** a wrong person in a key seat; cost cuts; demoting a loyal early employee.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = delayed for months, done by email or a deputy; 3 = done, but late or with mixed messages; 5 = quick, in person, honest, with a clear story for those who stay.
**Example:** a software founder lets two of twelve go, meets each personally, pays fair severance, and explains the numbers to the rest that afternoon.

## 29. Management Debt and Decision Debt
**Sources:** [R09].
**What it is:** short-term people fixes and avoided decisions that build long-term costs.
**Steps:** 1) List expedient people moves from the last year (special pay deals, vague roles, two people in one job, no training). 2) List decisions you are avoiding. 3) Estimate the cost of each if left. 4) Pay down the worst one this month.
**When to use:** pay disputes, unclear roles, politics, a decision stalled for weeks.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = adding new debt; 3 = holding steady; 5 = paying it down.
**Example:** a counter-offer to keep a leaving manager seems cheap until three others ask for the same; the owner sets a pay band instead.
**In the author's words:** "Decision debt is the worst debt, by the way, cuz it it paralyzes a company." (T010)

## 30. Wartime versus Peacetime Leadership
**Sources:** [R09], with Prioritize and Execute in [R06].
**What it is:** in peacetime, broaden, empower and build; in wartime, when survival is at stake, focus and direct.
**Steps:** 1) Decide honestly which mode you are in. 2) Tell the team plainly. 3) In war, narrow priorities and make fast calls. 4) Switch back when the threat passes.
**When to use:** loss of a big client, cash crisis, a new competitor.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = wrong mode for the situation; 3 = mixed signals; 5 = mode matches the threat and is explained.
**Example:** after losing its largest client, an agency freezes hiring, sets one sales priority and holds a short daily check-in for six weeks.

## 31. Career Conversations
**Sources:** [R13], with growth trajectories in [R13] and leverage in [R08].
**What it is:** structured talks about a person's past, their dreams, and a plan linking the two to their work.
**Steps:** 1) Talk one: their life story and what made them change course. 2) Talk two: what they want long term. 3) Talk three: a plan with skills to build in the next 6 to 18 months. 4) Decide if they want steep growth or steady excellence, and match the role.
**When to use:** retaining key staff, when someone seems restless, planning promotions.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = no idea what staff want; 3 = annual review only; 5 = every key person has a known goal and a plan.
**Example:** a bookkeeper who wants to run her own practice one day gets client-facing work now, and stays three more years.

## 32. The Right Medium for the Message
**Sources:** synthesis of [R14, R16, R13].
**What it is:** matching the channel to the emotional weight of the message.
**Steps:** 1) Facts and logistics: text or email. 2) Anything emotional, critical or about someone's job: in person, or by phone. 3) Online, add politeness and drop sarcasm. 4) Follow a hard talk with a short written summary of agreements.
**When to use:** any criticism, bad news, conflict or sensitive request.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = bad news by text; 3 = in person but no written follow-up; 5 = right medium plus a summary.
**Example:** an owner who was about to message a partner about missed targets calls instead, then emails the two agreed next steps.

## 33. Attachment Lens for Partners (Use With Care)
**Sources:** [R17], with matching in [R16] and safety in [R12].
**What it is:** a way to notice that people react differently to distance and reassurance: some need more contact when worried, some pull back when pressed.
**Steps:** 1) Notice your own pattern when a partner goes quiet. 2) Notice theirs when you push. 3) State needs plainly ("I need a weekly call on numbers") rather than through protest. 4) Agree on rhythms of contact that work for both. 5) Never label a person; if distress is serious, hand off to Psychology and Mental Health.
**When to use:** co-founder friction about availability and check-ins; an owner whose close relationship is straining the business.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = you read silence as betrayal and chase or punish; 3 = you notice the pattern afterward; 5 = clear, agreed contact rhythms and direct requests.
**Example:** one co-founder texts late at night when worried; the other goes silent under pressure. They agree a fixed Monday numbers call and a 24-hour reply rule.
**In the author's words:** "we have this idea that everybody loves the same, but science doesn't show that everybody loves the same." (T135)
