# Principles: Business department

Decision rules built from the 36 Business books (B01 to B36), grouped by the five spine stages, then the places where the books disagree. Quote policy: verbatim quotes appear only from author talks and public-domain texts, always with a T id; copyrighted book text is never quoted; everything else is paraphrase cited by book id.

Confidence notes: "strong" means five or more books agree, "moderate" means three or four, "single source" means one or two books, and "synthesis" means the rule is our own reading across books.

## Decision rules

### Before any stage

1. Find the stage that is the constraint (Offer, Leads, Close, Money Model or Scale) before you change anything; work on a stage that is not the constraint mostly wastes effort. Synthesis of [B01, B02, B03, B30]. Hormozi lays out the order himself: something to sell, people to sell it to, then getting them to buy, and only then does money arrive (talk T025, paraphrased). Confidence: synthesis, built on one author's sequence.
2. When several stages are weak, fix them in spine order: a better offer makes every lead and every call worth more, so it comes first. Synthesis of [B01, B02, B07]. Confidence: synthesis.
3. Get the real numbers for the weak stage, not the story about it: leads by source, close rate by stage, 30-day profit per customer, owner hours. [B29, B11, B30, B03] Confidence: moderate, four books.

### Stage 1: Offer

4. Most sales and lead problems are offer problems; when every rep and every channel struggles at once, fix the offer before the script or the ad. [B01] Single source; consistent with [B07, B18].
5. Pick the market before you polish the offer: buyers with real pain, money to spend, easy to reach, and a growing group. A good offer to the wrong crowd still fails. [B01, B22, B17] Confidence: moderate, three books.
6. Raise perceived value before you cut price: a bigger dream outcome, higher likelihood of success, less time to result, less effort for the buyer. [B01, B07] Single source, two books.
7. Make the offer hard to compare: solve every obstacle the buyer faces, stack the solutions, and name it so no rival offer lines up next to it. [B01, B36, B17] Confidence: moderate, three books.
8. Charge more and spend the margin on delivery; a low price starves the service that creates results and referrals. [B01] Single source; consistent with [B34].
9. Lower the buyer's risk with a guarantee or a small first step, but only one you can afford to honor. [B01, B12] Single source, two books.
10. Decide who it is for before what to say; your real competition is what the buyer would do without you, often nothing or do it themselves. [B17, B22, B16] Confidence: moderate, three books.
11. Own one idea in the buyer's mind; if you cannot be first in a category, define a narrower one you can lead. [B16, B17, B21] Confidence: moderate, three books.
12. You cannot create desire; find a want that already exists, match the message to how aware the buyer is, and make the claim specific. [B18, B19, B21, B15] Confidence: moderate, four books.
13. Build leverage into the offer: things you can deliver many times for little extra time beat custom work. [B01, B34, B28] Confidence: moderate, three books. "ideally you want the offer to have as much leverage as possible" (Alex Hormozi, talk T081)

### Stage 2: Leads

14. With a working offer, more engaged leads grow the business almost directly; leads are the lever most owners underuse. [B02, B10, B09] Confidence: moderate, three books.
15. Run one of the Core Four (warm outreach, free content, cold outreach, paid ads) at full volume before adding another: more, then better, then new. [B02] Single source; consistent with [B32] on focus.
16. Work a fixed daily volume of lead actions, not your mood; scale the number to your size but keep it daily. [B02, B09, B26] Confidence: moderate, three books.
17. Once your own Core Four works, recruit Lead Getters (customers, employees, agencies, affiliates) to run it for you; it is the highest-leverage source of leads. [B02, B10] Single source, two books. Hormozi calls letting others advertise for you the most efficient way (talk T025).
18. A lead magnet should solve one narrow problem fast and reveal the next problem your paid offer solves. [B02, B25] Single source, two books.
19. Win the first seconds: a headline or hook that fails means nothing after it is seen. Write several, keep the winner. [B24, B19, B02, B25] Confidence: moderate, four books.
20. Track every channel to sales, not views or likes, and stop what does not pay. [B19, B02, B11, B32] Confidence: moderate, four books.
21. Word of mouth can be designed: give people something worth telling, a trigger that reminds them, and a story that carries your name. [B20, B21, B22] Confidence: moderate, three books.
22. For outbound B2B, prospect every day and measure conversations, not dials; an empty pipeline causes most sales failures. [B09, B10, B14] Confidence: moderate, three books.

### Stage 3: Close

23. Diagnose before you prescribe: no pitch until the buyer has stated the problem, its root cause and its cost. [B04, B07, B06] Confidence: moderate, three books.
24. People buy a change in their situation; if there is no impact with a real cost, there is no deal, however friendly the buyer. [B07, B04, B05] Confidence: moderate, three books.
25. Judge each meeting by the dated next commitment it produced, on both calendars, not by how well it felt. [B04, B10] Single source, two books.
26. Qualify out early and be willing to walk; a slow no or a bad deal costs more than a fast no, and neediness repels buyers. [B10, B06, B08, B09] Confidence: moderate, four books.
27. "Let me think about it" usually means low certainty: raise certainty in the product, you and the company, or lower the step to act. [B12, B13, B01] Confidence: moderate, three books.
28. Empathy and calibrated questions beat arguing; never attack the buyer's past choice. [B06, B13, B04] Confidence: moderate, three books.
29. Discount last: when the buyer says no to price, offer a planned smaller or differently paid version before you cut the main price. [B03, B01, B06] Confidence: moderate, three books.
30. Push for the close in one-call, lower-priced sales; earn advances in multi-meeting deals, where heavy closing lowers win rates. [B04, B12, B14] Confidence: moderate, three books.
31. Teach the buyer something new about their business that leads back to your strength when you sell into committees or commodity markets. [B05, B08] Single source, two books.
32. Specialize sales roles once volume allows, hire for coachability, and coach one skill at a time from funnel data. [B10, B11] Single source, two books. Hiring process itself: hand off to Relationships and Leadership [R07].

### Stage 4: Money Model

33. Plan what every buyer is offered next: an attraction offer to start, an upsell after the yes, a downsell after the no, and continuity to keep them. The first sale is the start of the account. [B03, B25] Single source, two books.
34. Get paid more and sooner: aim for the first 30 days of gross profit per customer to cover the cost to acquire and serve them, so customers fund growth. [B03, B34] Single source, two books.
35. Scale ad spend and outreach only once those unit economics are proven; scaling a money-losing customer only loses money faster. [B03, B02, B32] Confidence: moderate, three books.
36. Every next offer must truly help the buyer; stacking offers on weak delivery raises refunds and churn and burns trust. Synthesis of [B03, B04]; Rackham says manipulative techniques have a short lifespan (talk T075, paraphrased).
37. Take profit first and watch cash timing; a growing business can run out of cash while profitable. [B33, B34, B03] Confidence: moderate, three books.

### Stage 5: Scale

38. If the business cannot run without the owner, the owner has a job, not an asset; write down how work is done so ordinary people can deliver it. [B28, B34, B30] Confidence: moderate, three books.
39. Focus on what you can be best at and what drives your economics, and say no to the rest, including side offers that need the owner. [B29, B34, B36, B35] Confidence: moderate, four books.
40. Compete where you already win; in a commodity market, change the factors you compete on rather than fight on price. [B35, B36, B31] Confidence: moderate, three books.
41. Test the riskiest assumption cheaply with real buyer behavior before building in full. [B32, B01] Single source, two books.
42. Pick a few priorities per quarter, each with one owner and a weekly number; recurring revenue and standard offers raise both cash and the price a buyer will pay. [B30, B34, B29] Confidence: moderate, three books.

## Where the books disagree

**1. Offer first versus position first: Hormozi versus Dunford and Ries.** [B01] says build an offer so strong that comparison disappears. [B17, B16] say value is judged against the buyer's alternatives and the slot you hold in their mind. *When each is right:* in services and info products where you control the bundle, build the offer [B01]. In crowded software or product markets where buyers already have categories, settle the position first, then build the offer to fit it [B17].

**2. Volume versus one strong hook: Hormozi and Vaynerchuk versus Kane.** [B02, B26] push a daily, high volume of posts and touches. [B24] says write many ideas, ship the best one, and engineer its opening seconds. *When each is right:* volume fits a founder with energy and a warm audience. Kane fits when posts reach nobody and each piece must win strangers. Most owners need both: fixed volume, with a hook written first.

**3. Narrow niche versus broad reach: Hormozi and Godin versus Sharp.** [B01, B22, B16] say choose a hungry, specific crowd. [B27] says growth comes from reaching all category buyers, mostly light ones. *When each is right:* niche fits small firms, B2B and specialized offers. Broad reach fits consumer goods and local services everyone uses. Many firms can aim the message narrowly without blocking anyone from buying.

**4. Direct response versus long-term brand: Hormozi and Brunson versus Ogilvy and Sharp.** [B02, B25, B03] want trackable sales and fast payback. [B19, B27] treat each ad as an investment in memory and recognition. *When each is right:* direct response when cash is tight and the offer is clear. Brand building once there is runway and buyers purchase on recall. Ogilvy himself tested hard, so measure either way.

**5. Fast cash from the first 30 days versus serve the long relationship: Hormozi versus Rackham and Godin.** [B03] designs upsells, downsells and continuity so each customer pays back fast. [B04, B22] warn that selling for the seller's benefit erodes trust and the long game. *When each is right:* a planned next offer serves the buyer when each add-on truly helps and delivery is strong [B03]. In advisory work and small, connected markets, earn the next sale through results and let the client ask.

**6. Close hard versus earn advances: Belfort and Cardone versus Rackham.** [B12, B14] teach steady pushing through several nos to a yes. [B04] found closing techniques hurt in large sales. *When each is right:* one-call consumer sales reward a clear, confident ask. Multi-meeting B2B deals reward earned advances and buyer-stated value.

**7. Raise the price versus bargain well: Hormozi versus Voss.** [B01] says build value and charge more, so negotiation barely happens. [B06] gives tools for hard bargaining. *When each is right:* when you set the offer, build value and hold price. When the other side has leverage, or you are the buyer, negotiate with skill.

**8. Relationship versus challenge: Carnegie versus Dixon and Adamson.** [R01] says liking and making people feel important drive results. [B05] found relationship builders trail in complex sales and that teaching with tension wins. *When each is right:* local, repeat and referral businesses run on relationships. Commoditized, committee-driven B2B rewards insight. Dixon says Challengers still build relationships, on business value.

**9. Specialize roles versus everyone sells: Ross and Roberge versus Cardone and Blount.** [B10, B11] split prospecting from closing. [B14, B09] expect every seller to prospect daily. *When each is right:* specialize with enough reps and steady demand. With a founder selling or a team of two or three, one person does both, and protected time matters more than structure.

**10. A ladder of offers versus one standard product: Hormozi and Brunson versus Warrillow.** [B03, B25] build several offers in sequence to raise what each customer pays. [B34] says specialize in one standard, teachable product to make the business run without you and be worth buying. *When each is right:* keep one core offer that is standard and teachable [B34], and build the money model around it [B03]. Add offers only when the team can deliver them without the owner.

**11. Grow fast versus stay lean: Thiel versus Michalowicz and Ries.** [B31] accepts years of losses to win a market where scale decides. [B33, B32] say stay profitable and learn cheaply. *When each is right:* B31 assumes venture money and a winner-take-most market. Most owner-funded firms die of a cash crunch first, so profit and small tests come first.

**12. Iterate versus bet on a definite plan: Ries versus Thiel.** [B32] says learn fast through small experiments. [B31] favors a definite long-term plan built on a secret and warns against tinkering. *When each is right:* iterate when tests are cheap and the market is unknown. Bet when the opportunity needs a large build that small tests cannot reveal.

**13. Systems versus people: Gerber versus Collins.** [B28] wants systems ordinary people can run. [B29] puts the right people first, then direction. *When each is right:* systems for repeatable delivery and sales steps. Great people for leadership and judgment roles that no manual covers.

**14. Purpose versus evidence: Sinek versus Sharp and Schwartz.** [B23] says people buy why you do it. [B27] finds buyers rarely think about brands, and [B18] says copy works by channeling a desire buyers already have. *When each is right:* a clear why helps hiring, culture and founder-led brands. For low-involvement purchases, lead with the benefit and keep the why for how you operate.
