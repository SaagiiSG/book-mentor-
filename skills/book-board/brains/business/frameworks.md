# Frameworks: Business department

Built from the 36 Business books (B01 to B36) and the old Sales, Marketing and Growth framework files, merged where books teach the same idea and grouped under the five spine stages, Hormozi's frame first in each. Quote policy: verbatim quotes appear only from author talks and public-domain texts, always with a T id; copyrighted book text is never quoted. Scoring guides are our guidance, not the books' own scales, and examples are illustrations we wrote, not cases from the books.

## Stage 1: Offer

### Value Equation
**Sources:** [B01].
**What it is.** A way to raise perceived value without cutting price. Value goes up with the dream outcome and the buyer's perceived likelihood of getting it, and down with time delay and effort or sacrifice. Effort is what the buyer must start doing; sacrifice is what they must give up (T136).
**Steps.** 1) Write the dream outcome in the buyer's words. 2) Rate each of the four levers from the buyer's view. 3) Fix the weakest lever first; the bottom two (time, effort) are usually cheapest to move. 4) Add proof to lift likelihood. 5) Build a quick win into the first 7 to 14 days, found by comparing what your top customers did early with the rest (T136).
**When to use.** Low close rates across reps, price objections, designing or repackaging any offer.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** Rate each lever 1 to 5. An offer scoring 3 or less on any lever has a leak; fix the lowest before touching price or ads.
**Example.** A personal trainer adds weekly check-ins and meal templates, cutting effort and lifting likelihood, and moves from hourly sessions to a higher-priced twelve-week package.
**In the author's words.** "effort and sacrifice is a nod to convenience right effort is what you what a prospect has to now begin doing that they don't want to do that they weren't doing before they started working with you" (T136). On time, the lever he pushes hardest: "the fastest way that you can provide value is look what everyone else in the market is doing and do it in half the time" (T136).

### Grand Slam Offer
**Sources:** [B01].
**What it is.** A method for building an offer so complete it cannot be compared like for like, which takes it out of price competition. Price is the symptom and value is the cause: solve every problem a specific buyer has, and the higher price you can then charge adds perceived value of its own (T136).
**Steps.** 1) Name the dream outcome. 2) List every problem the buyer faces before, during and after getting it. 3) Turn each problem into a solution. 4) Pick delivery vehicles (one to one, group, done for you, tools, templates). 5) Trim costly, low-value items; stack the high-value, low-cost ones. 6) Add enhancers.
**When to use.** Commodity pressure, a new product, any service business quoting by the hour or unit.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = same as competitors at a lower price. 3 = some unique extras. 5 = the buyer cannot find a like-for-like comparison.
**Example.** A roofer bundles an inspection report, financing help, insurance claim support and a workmanship guarantee instead of quoting per square meter.
**In the author's words.** "the price is the is this is the symptom not the cause right so the cause is that people are not providing sufficient value" (T136). "if i charge uh ten thousand dollars for a hundred thousand value they'll tell everybody" (T136).

### Starving Crowd (Pick the Market First)
**Sources:** [B01], with the smallest viable market in [B22] and the Ideal Customer Profile in [B10].
**What it is.** Market beats offer beats persuasion. Choose buyers with massive pain, purchasing power, who are easy to target, in a growing market. Under $1M in revenue, he says, run one product, one avatar and one channel (T136).
**Steps.** 1) List candidate segments. 2) Score each on the four indicators. 3) Pick one narrow, named group. 4) Write green and red flags from your best current customers [B10]. 5) Build the offer for that group only.
**When to use.** Before building an offer, when the offer works for some buyers but not others, or when marketing "to everyone."
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** One point per indicator clearly met, plus one if you can name where these buyers gather.
**Example.** A bookkeeper moves from "small businesses" to multi-site dental practices, who feel the pain, can pay, gather in one association, and are growing.
**In the author's words.** "it's one product one avatar and one channel and that is how you get from zero to a million that's it" (T136).

### Offer Enhancers and Guarantees (Risk Reversal)
**Sources:** [B01], with lowering the action threshold in [B12] and downsells in [B03].
**What it is.** Scarcity, urgency, bonuses, guarantees and naming make a good offer convert better. Guarantees reverse the buyer's risk.
**Steps.** 1) Add real scarcity (limited capacity) or urgency (a real deadline). 2) Turn next problems into bonuses, each with its own stated value. 3) Choose a guarantee type (unconditional, conditional, anti-guarantee, implied; detail uncertain) you can afford to honor. 4) Name the offer so the right buyer recognizes it (MAGIC naming, detail uncertain).
**When to use.** A sound offer with hesitant buyers, or a new firm without proof. Never fake scarcity.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = buyer carries all risk, no reason to act now. 3 = a vague guarantee or one enhancer. 5 = a specific, affordable guarantee plus a true reason to act now.
**Example.** A cleaning company offers a free reclean within 24 hours if any checklist item is missed, and caps new clients at ten a month because of crew size.

### Premium Pricing (Charge What It Is Worth)
**Sources:** [B01], with the brand premium in [B22].
**What it is.** Raise price to fund better delivery and attract committed buyers who get better results, which supports the price. A brand is the premium buyers pay over the substitute (T030).
**Steps.** 1) Raise value with the Value Equation first. 2) Test a higher price on new buyers only. 3) Put part of the extra margin into delivery. 4) Watch close rate and results together.
**When to use.** Margins are thin, the team is overloaded, or you are the cheapest by habit.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = priced below rivals with no reason. 3 = market rate. 5 = priced above the substitute with close rate and results holding.
**Example.** A web studio doubles its starter price, loses a third of inquiries, and earns more with half the projects.
**In the author's words.** "The value of a brand is how much extra am I paying above the substitute? And if I'm not paying extra, you don't have a brand." (T030)

### Positioning Components (Obviously Awesome)
**Sources:** [B17], with roots in [B16].
**What it is.** Define the context that makes your product obviously valuable to the right buyer. Positioning comes before messaging and taglines; it is an input (T035).
**Steps.** 1) Start with your best customers. 2) List competitive alternatives, including doing nothing. 3) Isolate unique attributes. 4) Map attributes to value and proof. 5) Find who cares most. 6) Pick the market category; add a trend only if it helps.
**When to use.** Prospects misunderstand you, compare you to the wrong rivals, or push on price.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = you cannot name what buyers would use instead. 3 = value clear, category fuzzy. 5 = a best-fit buyer can say what you are, why you beat their alternative, and the proof.
**Example.** A bookkeeping firm learns its real alternative is the owner's spouse with a spreadsheet, so it positions as "month-end done for you in 3 days."
**In the author's words.** "the component pieces of positioning are competitive alternatives. So, if you didn't exist, what would a customer do?" (T035)

### Positioning in the Mind (Product Ladder)
**Sources:** [B16].
**What it is.** Buyers rank a few brands per category in memory. Win by being first, or by creating a narrower category you can be first in, and own one idea there.
**Steps.** 1) Draw the ladder in your category as buyers see it. 2) If you are not first, define a subcategory you can lead. 3) Pick one word or idea to own. 4) Resist line extensions that blur it.
**When to use.** You are number three or lower, or messaging lists five benefits.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = me-too under a strong leader. 3 = a clear contrast with the leader. 5 = you own a word or subcategory customers repeat back.
**Example.** A small law firm stops being "full service" and becomes the startup equity lawyer in its city.
**In the author's words.** "the essence of positioning is to find an open hole in the prospect's mind" (T029)

### Smallest Viable Market and Two-Axis Positioning Map
**Sources:** [B22], with [B17].
**What it is.** Serve the smallest group that can sustain you, and find an open corner on two attributes they care about.
**Steps.** 1) Name the group and who it is not for. 2) Pick two attributes the group truly cares about. 3) Plot rivals. 4) Claim the open corner a real group wants.
**When to use.** The business appeals to everyone, or the market is crowded and differences are unclear.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = "anyone who needs X." 3 = a defined group. 5 = a group you can name and reach, that talks among itself, in an open corner.
**Example.** A gym plots guided versus self-directed against social versus private and finds no guided, private option for older adults.
**In the author's words.** "Find the people who care, the smallest viable group you can live with, and figure out how to give them work that matters." (T030)

### SB7 Framework and One-Liner
**Sources:** [B15].
**What it is.** A message structure with the customer as hero and you as guide.
**Steps.** 1) Character and want. 2) Problem at three levels (external, internal, philosophical). 3) Guide with empathy and competency. 4) A simple plan. 5) Direct and transitional calls to action. 6) Failure avoided, success reached. 7) Compress into a one-liner: problem, product, result.
**When to use.** Website, pitch or offer copy is unclear or self-focused.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = homepage is about the company. 3 = problem and call to action clear. 5 = a stranger can say what you offer, how it helps and how to buy within seconds.
**Example.** A roofer swaps "Family owned since 1985" for "A dry roof before winter, with a fixed quote in 24 hours," plus a "Book inspection" button.
**In the author's words.** "If you confuse, you will lose." (T026)

### The Golden Circle
**Sources:** [B23].
**What it is.** Communicate from the inside out: why you exist, how you do it, what you sell. The differentiating value proposition belongs to the how (T039).
**Steps.** 1) Find the why from the founder's story; it is positive. 2) State the how. 3) List the what. 4) Check decisions against the why and drop what does not fit.
**When to use.** The brand is interchangeable or staff cannot say why the business exists. Pair it with a specific benefit; buyers may care less about purpose than you hope [B27].
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = messaging is only what and price. 3 = a generic why. 5 = a specific why staff repeat and decisions are checked against.
**Example.** A bike shop leads with getting more kids riding to school, then its fitting service, then its bikes, and drops a cheap import line.

## Stage 2: Leads

### Core Four
**Sources:** [B02].
**What it is.** The only four ways to let people know about an offer: warm outreach, posting free content, cold outreach, paid ads. Warm or cold is whether they know you; one to one or one to many is the other axis (T025).
**Steps.** 1) List which of the four you run and at what volume. 2) Start with warm outreach, which is cheapest. 3) Add one more at fixed daily volume. 4) Track engaged leads per method.
**When to use.** Not enough leads, or a single source.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = one source, irregular. 3 = two sources run weekly and tracked. 5 = at least one source at fixed daily volume with cost per engaged lead known.
**Example.** A physio clinic that only runs ads adds messages to past patients and a referral ask at discharge.
**From the launch talk (paraphrased).** He lists the four as the only ways you can let people know about your stuff, in the order warm outreach, posting free content, paid ads, cold outreach (T025).

### Lead Getters
**Sources:** [B02].
**What it is.** Four groups who run the Core Four for you: customers (referrals), employees, agencies and affiliates. He calls this the highest-leverage route (T025).
**Steps.** 1) Ask every happy customer for a referral at the moment of result. 2) Give staff a simple reason and script to bring leads. 3) Use agencies to learn a channel, then bring it in-house if it pays. 4) Recruit affiliates who already reach your buyer, paid on results.
**When to use.** The owner is the only lead source, or the Core Four works but caps out at the owner's hours.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = no one but the owner brings leads. 3 = referrals happen but are not asked for. 5 = at least two lead getter groups produce tracked leads each month.
**Example.** A pool installer pays local landscapers a fee per completed install they refer.
**From the launch talk (paraphrased).** Only two kinds of people can tell strangers about what you sell, you and other people, so the most efficient advertising is done by others on your behalf (T025).

### Lead Magnet
**Sources:** [B02], with [B25, B15].
**What it is.** A free, complete solution to a narrow problem that reveals the bigger problem your paid offer solves.
**Steps.** 1) Pick one narrow problem your best buyer has. 2) Choose a type: reveal a problem (audit, quiz), a sample or trial, or one step of a multistep process. 3) Make it quick to consume. 4) End with a clear next step to the paid offer. 5) Track magnet to sale.
**When to use.** Cold audiences will not buy on first contact.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = generic newsletter signup. 3 = specific and useful. 5 = solves a narrow problem fast, reveals the next one, and is tracked to revenue.
**Example.** An accountant offers a five-minute deduction check that shows owners what they missed and books paid reviews.

### Rule of 100
**Sources:** [B02].
**What it is.** A fixed daily volume of lead actions, kept up long enough to work, so lead flow does not depend on mood.
**Steps.** 1) Pick the method. 2) Set the daily number (100 touches, 100 minutes of content, or a set ad spend), scaled to your size. 3) Do it before other work. 4) Track for 90 days before judging.
**When to use.** Lead flow is irregular or the owner prospects only when desperate.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = no daily target. 3 = a target hit most weeks. 5 = a set daily number tracked for 90 days or more.
**Example.** A solo designer commits to 30 warm messages a day, scaled down from 100 to fit a one-person business.

### More, Better, New
**Sources:** [B02].
**What it is.** The order for improving lead flow: do more of what works, then do it better, and only then add something new.
**Steps.** 1) Raise volume on the current channel until returns fall. 2) Improve the weakest step (hook, offer, follow-up). 3) Only then test a new channel.
**When to use.** The owner wants a new channel while the current one is under-run.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = adding channels before maxing any. 3 = one channel pushed, not improved. 5 = current channel at its limit before anything new.
**Example.** A clinic sending 10 cold emails a week raises it to 50 before trying short video.

### Hook, Retain, Reward and the Hook Point Method
**Sources:** [B02] for hook, retain, reward (T025); [B24] for the hook method (T042).
**What it is.** Every piece of content must stop the right stranger in the first seconds, keep them, and pay off. Kane engineers the hook from patterns that already work.
**Steps.** 1) Study hooks with huge reach in and outside your industry. 2) Break down their tone, pacing and questions. 3) Write at least 20 hooks; pick one per piece. 4) Pull with curiosity or a promise of value. 5) Deliver the reward. 6) Test on three-second retention and reuse winners. 7) When promoting, keep regular content and add the promotion on top (T025).
**When to use.** Posts and video ads get few views or no leads.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = no deliberate hook. 3 = hooks written, not tested. 5 = several hooks tested a month on early retention, with winners reused.
**Example.** A leather craftsman moves from leather-only videos to judging whether famous brands are worth the price, keeping his expertise under a broad theme (T042).
**In the author's words.** "the primary metric here is the audience retention in the first 3 seconds." (T042)

### Five Levels of Awareness and Market Sophistication
**Sources:** [B18].
**What it is.** Choose the opening from what the reader already knows and how many claims they have heard. Copy channels an existing desire; it cannot create one.
**Steps.** 1) Place the audience: most aware (lead with the offer), product aware (proof), solution aware (mechanism), problem aware (the problem), unaware (story or identity). 2) Place the market: simple claim, bigger claim, new mechanism, elaborated mechanism, identification. 3) Tie the opening to a want people already have.
**When to use.** Writing any ad, page or email, or claims sound like every rival's.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = product-first copy to a cold audience. 3 = opening matches awareness on most pages. 5 = each channel matched to awareness and sophistication, and hard to copy.
**Example.** Retargeting ads lead with the offer; cold ads lead with "still sore after every run?"; in a crowded weight-loss market a coach leads with a named method.

### Headline First and Specificity
**Sources:** [B19], with [B18, B21].
**What it is.** The headline carries the benefit and selects the reader, and exact claims are believed where vague ones are ignored.
**Steps.** 1) Write the benefit for a named reader. 2) Replace every superlative with a number, name or proof. 3) Write several headlines and test them. 4) Keep one big idea running for years.
**When to use.** Every ad and page, especially copy full of "best" and "quality."
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = clever, no benefit. 3 = benefit but vague. 5 = specific benefit to a named reader, tested against variants.
**Example.** "Payroll errors fixed in one day for clinics under 50 staff" beats "Payroll made easy."

### STEPPS
**Sources:** [B20].
**What it is.** Six drivers that make things shared: Social currency, Triggers, Emotion, Public, Practical value, Stories.
**Steps.** 1) Check which drivers the offer has. 2) Tie it to a frequent daily trigger. 3) Use high-arousal emotion. 4) Make use visible. 5) Wrap the brand in a story that cannot be told without it.
**When to use.** Designing referrals, a launch, or word of mouth on a small budget.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** Count drivers clearly present: one or none = 1, three = 3, five or six with a daily trigger = 5.
**Example.** A café prints a secret-menu card for regulars and ties one drink to Monday mornings.
**In the author's words.** "ideas have a habitat a set of cues or triggers that make people think about them" (T027)

### SUCCESs
**Sources:** [B21].
**What it is.** Six traits of messages people remember and act on: Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Stories. Beware the curse of knowledge.
**Steps.** 1) Find the one core idea. 2) Break a pattern to get attention. 3) Make it concrete and credible. 4) Add emotion and a story. Four traits beat two (T028).
**When to use.** A pitch, tagline or explainer is not landing.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** One point per trait clearly met, capped at 5.
**Example.** An IT firm swaps "robust cybersecurity solutions" for the fake invoice email it stopped that would have cost a client a month of payroll.
**In the author's words.** "all you have to do is to break a pattern" (T028)

### Hook, Story, Offer
**Sources:** [B25].
**What it is.** A three-part structure for any marketing piece: hook grabs the right person, story builds belief, offer asks for action.
**Steps.** 1) Write the hook for one buyer. 2) Tell a short story that breaks their main doubt. 3) Make one clear offer. 4) Test each part separately; fix the weakest.
**When to use.** Ads, videos, emails, webinars.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = only an offer. 3 = all three exist. 5 = each tested and the weakest improved first.
**Example.** A landscaper's ad opens with a before photo, tells the homeowner's story, then offers a free design call.

### Balanced Prospecting and the Law of Replacement
**Sources:** [B09], with [B02] Rule of 100 and persistence in [B14].
**What it is.** Daily multichannel prospecting so new prospects enter faster than deals close or die.
**Steps.** 1) Block prospecting first thing, even 15 minutes. 2) Mix phone, email, social, in person and referrals. 3) Track replacement rate against close and loss rate. 4) Use turnarounds for early brush-offs: anchor, disrupt, ask again.
**When to use.** Thin pipeline, feast-or-famine months, reps who wait for leads. Owner calendar problems go to Productivity.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = prospect only when desperate. 3 = weekly, one channel. 5 = daily blocks across channels with tracked replacement.
**Example.** A staffing agency owner blocks 8 to 9 each morning for calls and LinkedIn messages and ends feast-or-famine months.
**In the author's words.** "the cumulative impact of every single day prospecting is exponential and in terms of filling your pipeline up and filling your bank account up" (T084)

### Cold Calling 2.0 and the Ideal Customer Profile
**Sources:** [B10].
**What it is.** Targeted outbound to a defined customer profile, opening with short referral-seeking emails to senior people.
**Steps.** 1) Define the ICP from your best customers by profit and retention, with red and green flags. 2) Build a short target list. 3) Send short, plain emails asking who handles the topic. 4) Follow up with the referrer's name. 5) Count call conversations, not dials.
**When to use.** B2B outbound with a proven offer. Inboxes are crowded, so personalize.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = long pitch emails to anyone. 3 = short but generic. 5 = short, relevant, to ICP accounts, with referrals coming back.
**Example.** A logistics broker emails operations heads asking who manages carrier contracts and gets passed to the buyer.
**In the author's words.** "If you have a predictable way to generate qualified leads, you will generate predictable revenue." (T083)

### Mental and Physical Availability (Distinctive Brand Assets)
**Sources:** [B27], with the Big Idea in [B19].
**What it is.** Growth comes mostly from more buyers. Be easy to think of in buying situations and easy to buy, using consistent distinctive assets.
**Steps.** 1) Fix your colors, logo, sound or phrase and keep them for years. 2) Reach all category buyers, including light ones. 3) Be present wherever buyers shop.
**When to use.** Consumer, local retail, food and trades; a business tempted to target too narrowly or rebrand.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = inconsistent branding, hard to find. 3 = consistent and decent reach. 5 = recognized at a glance and present at every common point of purchase.
**Example.** A plumber keeps the same yellow van and jingle and gets listed on the two apps local homeowners use.

### Personal Brand Content Engine
**Sources:** [B26], with [B02] and [B24].
**What it is.** The founder becomes the face of the business and posts a lot, on the platforms where buyers spend attention, for years.
**Steps.** 1) Pick platforms that fit your strengths and buyers. 2) Post daily; document what you do rather than polish. 3) Give value far more often than you ask. 4) Track leads to content. 5) Expect years.
**When to use.** Founder-led firms where buyers trust people over companies. A personal brand is hard to sell with the business [B34].
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = no founder presence. 3 = weekly on one platform. 5 = daily output for 12 months or more with leads tracked.
**Example.** A roofing owner films a one-minute site update daily and gets quote requests from neighbors who follow him.

### Lead Value and Source Tracking
**Sources:** [B11], with cost per lead in [B02] and research first in [B19].
**What it is.** Know what each lead is worth and where it came from, so you buy and make more of the leads that turn into customers.
**Steps.** 1) Tag every lead with its source. 2) Lead value = conversion rate times revenue per customer [B11]. 3) Compare with cost per lead by source. 4) Move effort to the highest-value source and the highest-closing calls to action.
**When to use.** Any marketing spend, or a debate about whether marketing or sales is failing.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = no tracking. 3 = source captured. 5 = cost per customer and lead value known by source and reviewed monthly.
**Example.** A dentist gives each ad its own phone number and drops the one that brings calls but no bookings.

## Stage 3: Close

### Objections as Value Levers
**Sources:** synthesis of [B01, B12], with [B07].
**What it is.** Hormozi's view that most sales problems are offer problems, applied in the room: every objection points at one Value Equation lever, and the buyer's certainty about that lever is what is missing.
**Steps.** 1) Hear the objection. 2) Map it: "not sure it works for me" is likelihood, "too long" is time, "too much work" is effort, "too expensive" usually means the outcome is not big enough yet. 3) Answer that lever with proof, a shorter path or less effort. 4) If the same objection repeats across buyers, fix the offer, not the script.
**When to use.** Any recurring objection, and before writing new rebuttal scripts.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = argue or discount. 3 = answer the lever on the call. 5 = repeated objections feed back into the offer.
**Example.** A coaching firm hears "I don't have time" on half its calls and adds done-for-you meal plans, and the objection drops.

### Gap Analysis (Current State, Future State, Gap)
**Sources:** [B07], with the same logic in [B04] (synthesis of B04, B07).
**What it is.** A discovery map of where the buyer is, where they want to be, and what the distance costs.
**Steps.** 1) Current state: facts, problem, impact, root cause, kept separate. 2) Future state: new facts, outcomes (the opposite of today's impact), emotions. 3) The gap: size, cost and urgency. 4) Only then present.
**When to use.** Every discovery call for a considered purchase.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = you know their industry only. 3 = you know the problem and roughly its cost. 5 = the buyer has stated cost, root cause and outcome in their own words.
**Example.** A bookkeeping firm learns a client spends ten owner hours a month on books and missed a tax deadline, so its fee is weighed against that.
**In the author's words.** "impact is where the motivation to buy exists" (T085)

### SPIN Questioning
**Sources:** [B04].
**What it is.** A question sequence that grows an implied complaint into an explicit need.
**Steps.** 1) Few Situation questions. 2) Problem questions. 3) Implication questions on consequences and cost. 4) Need-payoff questions where the buyer states the value. Stay on the problem before any solution; type and order matter more than open versus closed.
**When to use.** Larger sales where buyers do not yet feel the cost.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = mostly Situation questions. 3 = problems found, no implications. 5 = buyer describes consequences and states the value of a fix.
**Example.** A commercial cleaner asks what happens when an audit finds a hygiene lapse, and the manager sees the risk to a major contract.
**In the author's words.** "top salespeople didn't talk about solutions until very late in the sales process" (T075)

### Commercial Teaching and Constructive Tension
**Sources:** [B05], with [B07].
**What it is.** Lead with an insight that reframes the buyer's business and points to your unique strength, and make staying put feel costlier than changing.
**Steps.** 1) Build the insight centrally, not rep by rep. 2) Teach the reframe. 3) Tailor it to each stakeholder. 4) Break down the status quo before building the new state. 5) Take control of money and process.
**When to use.** Commoditized markets, "we're fine" deals, buyers who think they know what they need.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = lead with features. 3 = interesting insight not tied to your strength. 5 = the insight leads only to you and the buyer re-evaluates the status quo.
**Example.** A packaging supplier shows a food brand that transit damage costs more than packaging, then shows its tested boxes.
**In the author's words.** "they don't want a sales person to come in and ask them what's keeping them up at night they want a sales person who will come in and show them what should be keeping them up at night" (T076)

### Tactical Empathy Toolkit (Mirroring, Labeling, Accusation Audit) and Calibrated Questions
**Sources:** [B06].
**What it is.** Show understanding so the other side relaxes and reveals more, then use open "how" and "what" questions so they help solve your problem.
**Steps.** 1) Mirror their last words. 2) Label the emotion. 3) Open with an accusation audit, or state your best guess of their view and let them correct it. 4) Use a calm, slow voice. 5) Ask "how am I supposed to do that?" and "what would need to be true?" Avoid "why."
**When to use.** Tense price talks, angry clients, guarded prospects, pushback on terms.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = you defend and explain. 3 = you label once but rush. 5 = they say "that's right" and volunteer constraints and options.
**Example.** Asked for 30 percent off, a print shop owner asks how she is supposed to hit the deadline at that rate, and the buyer offers a longer timeline.
**In the author's words.** "here's what I think you're thinking here's how I think you're approaching this here's what I think you're wanting out of this" (T077)

### Ackerman Bargaining
**Sources:** [B06].
**What it is.** A planned concession pattern for price negotiation.
**Steps.** 1) Set your target. 2) Open well short of it. 3) Make shrinking concessions. 4) Use empathy and calibrated questions between moves. 5) End on a precise number plus a small non-cash item.
**When to use.** Buying from suppliers or one-off contracts; less suited to long relationships.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = split the difference. 3 = planned moves, no planned end. 5 = closed near target with the relationship intact.
**Example.** A café owner buying an espresso machine lands a precise figure plus free servicing.

### Three Tens and the Action Threshold (Certainty)
**Sources:** [B12], with belief in [B14].
**What it is.** Buyers act when certain about the product, the seller and the company, or when the step to act is small enough.
**Steps.** 1) Estimate certainty on each. 2) Raise the lowest with proof. 3) Or lower the threshold: smaller first step, trial, guarantee. 4) Treat "let me think about it" as low certainty.
**When to use.** One-call closes, consumer sales, diagnosing hesitation. For diagnosis, not pressure.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** Rate each ten; any below 7 out of 10 is the blocker.
**Example.** A home security buyer doubts support, so the rep shows local service reviews and a 30-day cancel option.
**In the author's words.** "What no is it's almost a euphemism for saying what no means I am NOT certain That's all it means" (T090)

### Stalled Deal Diagnosis (No Impact or No Certainty)
**Sources:** synthesis of [B07, B12, B05].
**What it is.** A deal stalls because the buyer does not feel enough impact, or feels it but is not certain enough to act.
**Steps.** 1) Ask the buyer to state the cost of the problem. 2) If they cannot: no impact; return to the gap and challenge the status quo [B07, B05]. 3) If they can and still delay: no certainty; find the weakest ten, recommend one path, lower the threshold [B12].
**When to use.** Any stalled deal or "no decision" loss.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** Not scored; classify each stalled deal as impact or certainty.
**Example.** A clinic agrees scheduling losses cost two staff days a week but keeps asking for demos, so the seller recommends one plan and a one-site start.

### Advances and Micro-Commitments
**Sources:** [B04], with assumptive next steps in [B13].
**What it is.** Judge every call by whether the buyer committed to a specific step that moves the deal.
**Steps.** 1) Plan the best and minimum acceptable advance before the call. 2) Ask for it. 3) Put it on both calendars. 4) Define stages by buyer action, not CRM labels.
**When to use.** Long cycles, forecasting, coaching reps.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = "send me info." 3 = vague follow-up. 5 = a dated meeting with the decision maker or a signed step.
**Example.** A solar installer ends each visit by booking the roof survey with both homeowners present.

### Frame Control and the STRONG Method
**Sources:** [B08].
**What it is.** Keep the meeting on your terms and pitch in a tight sequence: Set the frame, Tell the story, Reveal the intrigue, Offer the prize, Nail the hookpoint, Get a decision.
**Steps.** 1) Set time and agenda. 2) Be new and brief. 3) Position yourself as the prize. 4) Ask for a decision. 5) Be willing to walk.
**When to use.** Investor and partner pitches, powerful buyers. Assumes real leverage.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = you chase and over-explain. 3 = you hold the agenda. 5 = the buyer qualifies themselves to you.
**Example.** A founder pitching a distributor sets a firm end time, mentions the two other regions in talks, and asks for a pilot decision.
**In the author's words.** "if you're not new and you're not dangerous you get ignored" (T080)

### Objection Handling Sequence
**Sources:** synthesis of [B04, B06, B12, B14].
**What it is.** A shared pattern for late-stage objections.
**Steps.** 1) Prevent what you can in discovery [B04]. 2) Pause. 3) Acknowledge or label [B06]. 4) Clarify the real concern; price is rarely it [B14]. 5) Address it or loop back to certainty [B12]. 6) Ask again.
**When to use.** "Too expensive," "need to think," "need to ask my partner."
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = argue or discount. 3 = clarify but forget to ask again. 5 = real concern found and a next step secured.
**Example.** "Too expensive" turns out to mean cash flow, and a landscaper offers monthly billing at the same total.
**In the author's words.** "very skilled sales people prevent objections they don't handle objections" (T075)

### Low-Pressure Language Patterns
**Sources:** [B13].
**What it is.** Short phrasing, prepared in advance, that lowers defenses and invites a decision.
**Steps.** 1) Openers that allow a no. 2) Ask how open-minded they would be. 3) Have them imagine the result. 4) Offer two clear options. 5) Ask when, not whether.
**When to use.** Calls, follow-ups, closing moments.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = pushy or vague asks. 3 = some good phrasing. 5 = every call ends with a clear, easy next step.
**Example.** A gym owner asks whether a prospect would be open to trying the program for a month instead of pushing an annual contract.
**In the author's words.** "if you say to somebody I'm not sure if it's for you their little voice kicks in" (T094)

### Qualify Out Early
**Sources:** [B10, B06], with walking away in [B08] and market choice in [B01].
**What it is.** Drop poor-fit or unwinnable deals fast; a slow no costs more than a fast one.
**Steps.** 1) Set must-have criteria from your ICP flags. 2) Test them in the first call. 3) Walk away politely and quickly.
**When to use.** Packed pipelines, long cycles, low win rates.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = chase everything. 3 = drop deals only after they die. 5 = clear exit rules used in the first two calls.
**Example.** A web agency declines projects under its minimum budget and its win rate rises.
**In the author's words.** "it's not a sin to not get the deal it's a sin to take a long time to not get the deal or it's a sin to take a long time to get a bad deal" (T077)

## Stage 4: Money Model

### Money Model (Attraction, Upsell, Downsell, Continuity)
**Sources:** [B03] (T137), with offer building in [B01] and option framing in [B13].
**What it is.** A planned sequence of offers so customers spend twice as much on you as you spend on them within 30 days (T137). Four offer types hold 15 mechanisms: attraction offers get people to buy (win your money back, giveaways, decoy offer, buy X get Y free, pay less now or pay more later); upsell offers get them to spend more (classic, menu, anchor, rollover); downsell offers get them to buy something when they say no (payment plan, trial, feature downsell); continuity offers get them to buy again and again (continuity bonus, continuity discount, waived fee) (T137). The talk caption reads dummy offer for the decoy.
**Steps.** 1) Attraction offer: something free or discounted that brings the customer in and leads to something better at a higher price. 2) Upsell right after the yes, solving the next problem the purchase creates; on a menu upsell, unsell what they do not need, prescribe what they do, ask A or B, and make paying easy (T137). 3) Downsell when the buyer says no to price. 4) Continuity offer for recurring revenue, started with a bonus worth more than the first payment, a discount for paying a year up front, or a waived fee for committing long term (T137). 5) Build one offer at a time and check 30-day gross profit against twice CAC plus COGS.
**When to use.** One-product businesses, rising acquisition costs, buyers who walk at the price. Check delivery capacity first.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = one offer, one price, nothing after. 3 = an upsell or payment plan exists but is not offered every time. 5 = every buyer hears a planned next offer, every no hears a planned downsell, and 30-day profit covers acquisition.
**Example.** A dental clinic runs a low-cost first visit, offers a cleaning plan at checkout, a payment plan to those who decline full treatment, and a yearly membership.
**In the author's words.** "A money model is a sequence of offers. A $100 million money model is a sequence of offers that make customers spend twice as much on you as you do on them in 30 days or less." (T137) "An attraction offer is something free or discounted that gets a customer in the door but makes money by getting them to buy something better at a higher price." (T137)

### 30-Day Cash and Client-Financed Acquisition
**Sources:** [B03], with lifetime gross profit to acquisition cost in [B02].
**What it is.** Grow only as fast as customers fund it. His rule: 30-day gross profit should be more than twice the cost to acquire a customer plus the cost to serve them (CAC plus COGS) (T137). Then every new customer pays for the next, and cash stops limiting growth. Simply covering CAC is break-even, not his target.
**Steps.** 1) Compute cost to acquire a customer (CAC) by channel. 2) Compute 30-day gross profit per customer and the cost of goods sold to serve them. 3) If 30-day profit is below twice CAC plus COGS, add or fix money model offers before raising spend. 4) Check lifetime gross profit against CAC; about 3 to 1 is his healthy floor (detail uncertain). 5) Scale spend while the ratios hold.
**When to use.** Before scaling ads or hiring sales, or when growth keeps draining cash.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = CAC unknown. 3 = CAC known, payback beyond 90 days. 4 = 30-day gross profit covers CAC. 5 = 30-day gross profit above twice CAC plus COGS and tracked monthly.
**Example.** A home services firm adds a paid maintenance plan at the first visit, so first-month profit is double its ad and service cost per customer and it can raise spend safely.
**In the author's words.** "30-day gross profit is greater than two times cost of acquiring customer plus cost of goods sold or CAC plus COGS." (T137)

### Downsell Instead of Discount
**Sources:** [B03], with the action threshold in [B12].
**What it is.** When a buyer says no to price, keep the price of the main offer and offer a smaller step. His three downsells are a payment plan, a trial and a feature downsell, a lighter version with fewer parts (T137).
**Steps.** 1) Prepare the downsell before the call. 2) On a price no, confirm the buyer wants the result. 3) Offer the payment plan first, then the lighter version. 4) Never cut the main price on the spot.
**When to use.** Prospects leave with nothing after the price, or reps discount to close.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = discount or lose. 3 = a downsell exists but is improvised. 5 = every price no hears a planned downsell and the main price holds.
**Example.** A course creator offers a three-payment plan, then a self-paced version without coaching, and stops giving 30 percent off.

### Value Ladder
**Sources:** [B25], with continuity in [B03] and recurring revenue in [B34].
**What it is.** A sequence of offers rising from free to premium, each leading to the next, with recurring offers at the top where they fit.
**Steps.** 1) Free bait. 2) Low-price entry. 3) Core offer. 4) High-ticket or continuity. 5) Give each rung a clear next step and measure take rates.
**When to use.** Only one product, or a big gap between free and expensive.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = single offer. 3 = an entry offer exists. 5 = every rung has a next step, a measured take rate, and recurring revenue at the top.
**Example.** A dog trainer adds a free video, a low-price group class, private sessions and a monthly plan.

### Price Anchoring and Option Framing
**Sources:** synthesis of [B01, B03, B13].
**What it is.** Order and frame options so the right one looks clearly sensible.
**Steps.** 1) Show the premium option first as an anchor. 2) Offer two or three clear choices. 3) Recommend one. 4) Show the value stack before the price.
**When to use.** Proposals, pricing pages, pricing conversations.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = one take-it-or-leave-it price, or a long menu. 3 = options, no recommendation. 5 = few options, a recommendation, value anchored.
**Example.** An event planner shows a full-service package first, then two simpler ones, and recommends the middle.

### Profit First Allocation
**Sources:** [B33].
**What it is.** Take set percentages for Profit, Owner's Comp and Tax from every deposit before paying expenses, using separate accounts.
**Steps.** 1) Open Income, Profit, Owner's Comp, Tax and Opex accounts. 2) Compare current with target percentages. 3) Start small, such as 1 percent profit. 4) Allocate on the 10th and 25th. 5) Raise percentages each quarter; take a quarterly distribution.
**When to use.** Sales come in but cash never stays, or the owner underpays themselves. Check tax with an accountant.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = no profit set aside. 3 = sometimes. 5 = fixed allocations every cycle with a rising share.
**Example.** A cleaning company moving 2 percent to profit finds it must cut an unused software plan and a van lease.
**In the author's words.** "the solution is radically simple get smaller plates don't change yourself get smaller plates" (T009)

## Stage 5: Scale

### Leverage at Every Level (Attract, Convert, Deliver)
**Sources:** Hormozi's talk on [B01] (T081), with Lead Getters in [B02] and systems in [B28].
**What it is.** A business attracts attention, converts it, and delivers. At each level, scale means more output per hour of the owner's time: media over one-to-one outreach, an automated checkout over a phone call, software or groups over one-to-one delivery (T081).
**Steps.** 1) For each level, list where output still needs owner hours. 2) Find the cheapest lever: content, lead getters, scripts and hires, productized or group delivery. 3) Move the level that is the current constraint first. 4) Keep quality checks so leverage does not break results.
**When to use.** The business works but growth is capped by the owner's hours.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = every level needs the owner. 3 = one level leveraged. 5 = all three run with leverage and results hold.
**Example.** A consultant turns her diagnostic into a recorded workshop (attract), hires a closer (convert), and moves clients into a group program (deliver).
**In the author's words.** "you have to convert attention then you have to deliver something uh for that attention" (T081)

### Technician, Manager, Entrepreneur and the Franchise Prototype
**Sources:** [B28], with productized services in [B34].
**What it is.** Every owner carries three roles; when the Technician dominates, the business is a job. Design it so ordinary staff deliver a consistent result through systems.
**Steps.** 1) Note which role takes your week. 2) Write the Primary Aim (the life you want) and Strategic Objective. 3) Draw an org chart of positions, with your name in many boxes. 4) Rank offers on teachable, valuable and repeatable [B34] and standardize the best. 5) Innovate, quantify, orchestrate: try, measure, make it standard. 6) Replace yourself box by box.
**When to use.** Skilled owner, flat business, quality depends on who does the job.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = all Technician, every job custom. 3 = core offer defined, Manager active. 5 = standard offer sold and delivered without the owner.
**Example.** A plumber who does every quote writes the quoting rules down and hands quotes to an office manager.
**In the author's words.** "Knowing how to do the work in a business has nothing to do with creating a business that works." (T002)

### Built to Sell Readiness
**Sources:** [B34], with owner independence in [B28].
**What it is.** Check that a buyer could take over: a standard offer, recurring or up-front revenue, many customers rather than a few, sales without the owner, a positive cash cycle, a management team.
**Steps.** 1) Standardize one offer and stop custom work. 2) Shift to up-front or recurring billing. 3) Hire two salespeople at once for the standard offer. 4) Give managers long-term incentives. 5) Step out for an extended period and measure.
**When to use.** Exit in 2 to 5 years, or the owner wants freedom.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = owner sells and delivers. 3 = some parts run without the owner. 5 = runs and grows for months without the owner.
**Example.** An IT support firm moves clients to monthly contracts and hires a service manager before talking to buyers.
**In the author's words.** "when a buyer buys your business they've actually got to write two checks" (T014)

### EOS Operating System (Six Components, Rocks, Scorecard, Level 10)
**Sources:** [B30].
**What it is.** Run the company on Vision, People, Data, Issues, Process and Traction, with 90-day Rocks, a weekly Scorecard and a fixed-agenda weekly meeting.
**Steps.** 1) Rate each component and fix the weakest. 2) Write the Vision/Traction Organizer. 3) Draw the Accountability Chart; one name per seat. 4) Set three to seven company Rocks per quarter, each owned. 5) Track 5 to 15 weekly numbers, leading ones first. 6) Run the weekly meeting mostly on issues: Identify, Discuss, Solve.
**When to use.** Roughly 10 to 250 people with missed priorities, blurred roles and wandering meetings. People issues beyond seats go to Relationships and Leadership.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = components absent. 3 = present but loose. 5 = clear and used weekly.
**Example.** A 40-person contractor finds Data weakest and starts a 10-number weekly Scorecard.
**In the author's words.** "You cannot build a great company on multiple operating systems. You must choose one." (T004)

### Hedgehog Concept and the Flywheel
**Sources:** [B29].
**What it is.** Focus where three circles meet (what you can be best at, what drives your economic engine, what your people care about), then push the same chain of actions for years instead of lurching between programs.
**Steps.** 1) List what you could be best at, not just good at. 2) Pick one profit per X ratio. 3) Check passion. 4) Stop work outside the overlap. 5) Map the flywheel steps that feed each other and keep pushing them. 6) Face brutal facts while keeping faith (Stockdale).
**When to use.** The owner keeps adding lines or changing direction after short tests.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = outside all circles, new direction every few months. 3 = fits one or two. 5 = fits all three, lifts the key ratio, same push for years.
**Example.** A print shop finds it earns most per press hour on short-run packaging and drops general printing.

### Build-Measure-Learn, Pivot or Persevere, and Engines of Growth
**Sources:** [B32].
**What it is.** Test the riskiest assumption with the smallest real offer, decide on a schedule whether to pivot one element or persevere, and know which engine drives growth: sticky, viral or paid.
**Steps.** 1) Write the value and growth assumptions. 2) Build the cheapest test using real behavior, such as a pre-sale. 3) Measure with cohort and conversion numbers, not totals. 4) At a set date, pivot one element (customer, problem, channel, model) or recommit. 5) Name your engine and track its metric.
**When to use.** A new product, service, market or price with unknown demand.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = full build before any test. 3 = tests with opinions only. 5 = small tests with real money and dated decisions.
**Example.** A fitness studio pre-sells a corporate class to three local firms before hiring an instructor.
**In the author's words.** "the biggest waste that product development faces today is not building things inefficiently but building things very efficiently that nobody wants" (T005)

### Monopoly Test and the Seven Questions
**Sources:** [B31].
**What it is.** Test whether a business can escape competition through proprietary technology, network effects, economies of scale or brand, and answer seven questions: engineering, timing, monopoly, people, distribution, durability, secret.
**Steps.** 1) Define the market honestly, not too broad or narrow. 2) Check the four traits. 3) Answer the seven questions. 4) Start with a small market you can dominate.
**When to use.** Choosing what to build. Venture-style ambition; use loosely for small firms.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = commodity in a crowded market. 3 = one durable advantage. 5 = several advantages in a market you can lead.
**Example.** A local software firm becomes the default tool for one trade association's members.

### Strategy Canvas and ERRC Grid
**Sources:** [B36].
**What it is.** Plot how you and rivals score on the factors the industry competes on, then decide what to Eliminate, Reduce, Raise and Create.
**Steps.** 1) List the competing factors. 2) Score you and rivals. 3) Fill the grid. 4) Check the new curve is focused and different. 5) Look at noncustomers for ideas.
**When to use.** Commoditized market, falling prices, every rival looks the same.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = same curve as rivals. 3 = small differences. 5 = a distinct curve with lower cost and more value on what buyers care about.
**Example.** A hair salon eliminates long waits and upsells, reduces its menu, raises speed, and creates booking by text.
**In the author's words.** "what I mean by Blu ocean perspective is that we say for example focus on non customer to create new market space" (T007)

### Five Factors and Seven Comparisons (Know the Rival and Yourself)
**Sources:** [B35].
**What it is.** Before a competitive move, weigh both sides on the same factors and fight only where the comparison already favors you.
**Steps.** 1) Name the rival and the ground (segment, region, channel). 2) Score both on shared purpose, timing, terrain, leadership and discipline. 3) Where you lose, avoid battle or change the ground. 4) Where you win, move fast and keep it short. 5) Prefer beating the rival's plan over a price war.
**When to use.** A rival attacks, a price war looms, or you enter a contested market.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = no comparison. 3 = compared, but fighting on their ground. 5 = chosen ground where you clearly win, short and cheap.
**Example.** A regional courier stops bidding on national contracts and owns same-day delivery in its city.
**In the author's words.** "Thus do many calculations lead to victory, and few calculations to defeat: how much more no calculation at all!" (T101)

### Sales Team Engine (Specialized Roles, Hiring Formula, Metrics-Driven Coaching)
**Sources:** [B10, B11].
**What it is.** Move from founder-led selling to a team: split prospecting, closing and account management, hire to a tested profile, coach on funnel data, and pay for customers who stay.
**Steps.** 1) Split roles once you have about four reps [B10]. 2) Score candidates on about ten traits and test coachability with feedback in a role play [B11]. 3) Certify on a documented process. 4) Find each rep's weakest funnel step and coach one skill at a time. 5) Pay part of commission after the customer stays.
**When to use.** First and later sales hires. Small teams use judgment more than statistics; general hiring and culture go to Relationships and Leadership.
**Scoring (1 to 5, guidance).** 1 = everyone does everything, hired on charm. 3 = roles split, structured interviews. 5 = each role has its own metric, hires scored, coaching targeted, pay tied to retention.
**Example.** A growing IT firm hires one appointment setter so its two closers stop skipping outreach, and pays half of commission after month four.
**In the author's words.** "the best sales manager and coaches can actually see the 90 things but focus on the one or two that are going to make the biggest impact today" (T082)
