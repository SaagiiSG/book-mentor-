# Books: Business department

Built from the 36 Business books in BOOKS.md (B01 to B36), one entry each in id order, merged from the old Sales, Marketing and Growth brains and the author-talk notes. Quote policy: verbatim quotes appear only from author talks and public-domain texts, always with a T id; copyrighted book text is never quoted, and everything else is paraphrase cited by book id. Each entry names its spine stage (Offer, Leads, Close, Money Model, Scale).

## [B01] $100M Offers, Alex Hormozi (2021)

**Stage:** Offer (the spine's first stage).

**Core idea.** Most sales and marketing problems are offer problems. In his words, a low price is the symptom and too little value is the cause (T136). Pick a market that is starving for a result, build an offer so good that people feel stupid saying no, and charge more, not less. His order of power is market first, then offer, then persuasion: a great pitch cannot save a weak offer to a market that does not want it.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Starving crowd:** choose a market with massive pain, purchasing power, that is easy to target, and that is growing. A narrow, named group gets a more valuable offer than "everyone."
- **Value Equation:** perceived value rises with the dream outcome and the perceived likelihood of getting it, and falls with time delay and effort or sacrifice. The two bottom levers are often the cheapest to move. In his talk he pushes time hardest, since speed is what buyers value most, and splits the last lever in two: effort is what the buyer must start doing, sacrifice is what they must give up (T136).
- **Customer surplus and the quick win (talk):** charge far less than the value you deliver and buyers tell everyone; charge exactly the value and they stay quiet. Engineer a quick win in a new client's first 7 to 14 days, found by comparing what your top customers did early with the rest (T136).
- **Niche and productize (talk):** under $1M in revenue, run one product, one avatar and one channel. A narrow avatar lets you standardize the offer, price differently and make loyalty attach to the product, not one provider (T136).
- **Grand Slam Offer:** name the dream outcome; list every problem the buyer will face before, during and after; turn each into a solution; choose delivery vehicles (one to one, group, done for you, tools); then trim the costly, low-value items and stack the rest. The result is hard to compare on price, so it escapes commodity pricing.
- **Charge what it is worth:** a higher price funds better delivery and draws more committed buyers, who get better results, which supports the price.
- **Enhancers:** scarcity (limited supply), urgency (limited time), bonuses (each one solving a next problem, with its own stated value), guarantees, and naming. Guarantee types include unconditional, conditional, anti-guarantees and implied or performance guarantees (detail uncertain). His naming formula is MAGIC: a magnetic reason why, the avatar, the goal, the interval, and a container word (detail uncertain).
- **Leverage and the three levels (from his talk):** a business attracts attention, converts it, and delivers; at each level you want the most output for the least time, and the offer itself should carry leverage, like software or media sold many times (T081).
- **Where ideas come from (talk):** pain, past profession, or passion. Past profession is the proven money maker; the other two are less proven with more upside (T081).

**In the author's words:** "the price is the is this is the symptom not the cause right so the cause is that people are not providing sufficient value" (T136). "the fastest way that you can provide value is look what everyone else in the market is doing and do it in half the time" (T136). "if i charge uh ten thousand dollars for a hundred thousand value they'll tell everybody" (T136). "ideally you want the offer to have as much leverage as possible" (T081).

**Use it when.** Close rate is low across all reps, buyers compare you on price, leads arrive but do not buy, or you sell services, coaching or local offers.

**Watch out.** Tilted to services and info products, and the claims rest on his own companies. Strong guarantees can sink a small firm if delivery is not reliable, so price the guarantee into your margin. The Grand Slam Offer talk (T136) covers value, price, the time and effort levers, niche and productizing, but its transcript is cut before scarcity, urgency, bonuses, guarantees and naming, so those details rest on the book alone. T081 is a general interview.

## [B02] $100M Leads, Alex Hormozi (2023)

**Stage:** Leads.

**Core idea.** Getting leads is a numbers game you control. His sequence is offer first, then people to sell to, then sales, and only then money. There are only four ways to let people know about you, most businesses underuse all of them, and the fix is usually more volume of what already works before anything new.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **The Core Four:** two axes. Warm or cold is whether they know you; one to one or one to many is the other. Warm outreach (one to one, people who know you), posting free content (one to many, people who know you), cold outreach (one to one, strangers), paid ads (one to many, strangers) (T025).
- **Lead Getters:** customers (referrals), employees, agencies and affiliates. You use the Core Four to recruit them and they run the Core Four for you; he calls this the highest-leverage route (T025).
- **What a lead is:** someone with the problem and the money to solve it (T025). The real unit is an engaged lead, a person who has shown interest, not a name on a list.
- **Lead magnets:** a free, complete solution to a narrow problem that reveals a bigger problem your paid offer solves. Types: reveal a problem, a sample or trial, or one step of a multistep process.
- **Rule of 100:** a fixed daily volume of lead actions (for example 100 outreach touches, 100 minutes of content, or a set daily ad spend) kept up long enough to work.
- **More, Better, New:** do more of the current activity, then do it better, and only then add a new channel.
- **Content:** hook, retain, reward in every post. When promoting, keep the regular content your audience came for and add the promotion on top (T025).
- **Paid ads and outreach results from the launch talk:** a three-step ad script of what, who and when; a landing page he says reached a 55 percent opt-in rate; about $275,000 spent for 137,000 leads, roughly $2 per lead. Cold outreach saved Gym Launch during COVID, from zero sales in month one to 30 in month nine and $480,000 monthly revenue; outbound is now over half of that company's sales (T025).
- **Unit economics:** ads pay when lifetime gross profit per customer is well above the cost to acquire one; he treats about 3 to 1 as a healthy floor (detail uncertain).

**From the launch talk (paraphrased, T025):** in this talk he reads from the book and delivers a scripted version of it, so no lines are quoted here. He says that, all else equal, doubling your leads doubles your business; that plenty of leads make it hard to stay poor, because advertising can make up for a weak product or weak selling; and that only two kinds of people can tell strangers about what you sell, you and other people, which is why lead getters matter (T025).

**Use it when.** The offer converts but too few people hear it, lead flow swings with the owner's mood, or the owner keeps jumping between tactics.

**Watch out.** Hype-heavy and drawn from his own companies. Volume targets assume capacity; a solo owner may need to scale the "100" down. The launch talk covers the eight methods only at overview level, and his numbers are his results, not benchmarks for you.

## [B03] $100M Money Models, Alex Hormozi (2025)

**Stage:** Money Model.

**Core idea.** A business should not rely on one offer sold once. A money model is a planned sequence of offers. His target: in the first 30 days, a customer's gross profit should be more than twice the cost to acquire them plus the cost to serve them (CAC plus COGS); in plain terms, customers spend twice as much on you as you spend on them, within 30 days (T137). Then each customer funds the next, and cash stops limiting growth. Offers answers what to sell, Leads answers who to sell to, and Money Models answers how to get paid as much as possible, as fast as possible, again and again (T137).

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Four offer types, 15 mechanisms:** attraction offers get people to buy, upsell offers get them to spend more, downsell offers get them to buy something when they say no, and continuity offers get them to buy again and again. Every mechanism sits under one of the four (T137).
- **Attraction offers (5):** something free or discounted that gets a customer in the door and makes money by leading them to buy something better at a higher price. The five: win your money back, giveaways, a decoy offer (the talk caption reads dummy offer), buy X get Y free, and pay less now or pay more later (T137).
- **Upsells (4):** offer the next thing the buyer needs right after the yes, because the purchase creates the next problem. The four: classic (reveal the next problem), menu, anchor (show the premium option first) and rollover (credit what they already paid toward the bigger offer) (T137). His menu upsell runs in four moves: unsell what they do not need, prescribe what they do, ask whether they prefer A or B, and make paying easy (T137).
- **Downsells (3):** payment plan, trial, and feature downsell (a lighter version), used instead of cutting the main price (T137).
- **Continuity (3):** continuity bonus (a free bonus worth more than the first payment, for joining), continuity discount (aimed at getting a year up front), and waived fee, where committing long term avoids a big fee so flexibility costs more (T137).
- **Compounding:** twice as valuable, twice as many customers and twice as fast gives eight times the growth (T137).
- **Same product, different model:** two businesses can sell the identical service and earn very different cash because one has a better money model, so check the model before blaming the product (T137).
- **Cash first, then scale:** grow ad spend and outreach only when 30-day gross profit per customer clears twice what it costs to get and serve them. Build the model one offer at a time rather than all at once.
- **Case numbers from the launch talk:** a gym spending $100 per free trial, three trials per member, paid $300 to win a $100 a month member and needed three months to break even; his fix was a $500 up-front win-your-money-back offer paid back as store credit, then a continuity offer. Gym Launch added a continuity bonus to a $16,000 package; he gives the lift as $380,000 to $1.76M a month in six months in one place and to $2.2M in twelve months in another (T137). These are his results, not benchmarks.

**In the author's words:** "A money model is a sequence of offers. A $100 million money model is a sequence of offers that make customers spend twice as much on you as you do on them in 30 days or less." (T137) "An attraction offer is something free or discounted that gets a customer in the door but makes money by getting them to buy something better at a higher price." (T137) "So, there might not be anything wrong with your business. You might just have something wrong with your money model." (T137)

**Use it when.** Getting customers costs too much, the business sells one product once, buyers who say no to price leave with nothing, or the team closes the first deal and stops.

**Watch out.** Drawn mainly from the author's own companies, recent and little tested. Stacked offers feel like a trap if delivery is weak, and fast-cash thinking can clash with slow, trust-based advisory selling. Check delivery capacity before adding offers.

## [B04] SPIN Selling, Neil Rackham (1988)

**Stage:** Close.

**Core idea.** Large, complex sales succeed through better questions, not better closing. Rackham, a research psychologist, led a study of 35,000 sales calls over 12 years and found small-sale tactics often hurt big sales.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **SPIN questions:** Situation, Problem, Implication (consequences and costs), Need-payoff (the buyer states the value of a fix). A broad sequence, not a rigid script.
- **Type and sequence, not open versus closed:** openness did not predict success; kind and order did.
- **Advances versus continuations:** a call succeeds only if the buyer commits to a concrete step forward.
- **Prevent objections:** top sellers got about a third as many objections because they stayed on the problem longer.
- **Closing techniques backfire in large sales.**

**In the author's words:** "top salespeople didn't talk about solutions until very late in the sales process" (T075). "very skilled sales people prevent objections they don't handle objections" (T075).

**Use it when.** Considered purchases with several stakeholders, or reps pitch too early and hear "send me something."

**Watch out.** Too many Situation questions bore senior buyers. Built for large B2B sales; transactional sales need less depth.

## [B05] The Challenger Sale, Matthew Dixon and Brent Adamson (2011)

**Stage:** Close.

**Core idea.** In complex B2B sales, stars are less often friendly relationship builders and more often reps who teach the customer something new, tailor it, and keep control of the sale.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Five rep profiles:** Challengers were overrepresented among stars; Relationship Builders finished far behind.
- **Teach, Tailor, Take Control.**
- **Commercial teaching:** the insight should lead back to a strength only you have. Building it is the company's job, not each rep's.
- **Constructive tension:** break down the status quo before building the new state.

**In the author's words:** "they don't want a sales person to come in and ask them what's keeping them up at night they want a sales person who will come in and show them what should be keeping them up at night" (T076).

**Use it when.** Buyers treat you as a commodity, deals stall at "we're fine," or reps get pushed around on price.

**Watch out.** Not permission to be aggressive; Dixon says Challengers are empathetic and build relationships on business value. Real insight needs marketing support small firms may lack. The often-cited 60 percent figure came after the book.

## [B06] Never Split the Difference, Chris Voss and Tahl Raz (2016)

**Stage:** Close.

**Core idea.** Negotiation runs on emotion, so deliberate empathy uncovers what the other side feels and needs before you shape the deal. Drawn from Voss's FBI hostage work.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Tactical empathy, mirroring and labeling.**
- **Accusation audit:** name their worst thoughts about you first, or state your best guess of their view and let them correct it.
- **Calibrated questions:** "how" and "what" questions that make them solve your problem.
- **Ackerman bargaining:** shrinking planned concessions ending in a precise number.
- **Calm voice and fast exits:** spot a bad deal early and leave. He distrusts people who open with "win-win" but does not reject mutual gain.

**In the author's words:** "it's not a sin to not get the deal it's a sin to take a long time to not get the deal or it's a sin to take a long time to get a bad deal" (T077).

**Use it when.** Price talks, contract terms, collections, partner disputes.

**Watch out.** Hostage stories do not map neatly to repeat relationships, and overused techniques feel scripted.

## [B07] Gap Selling, Keenan (2018)

**Stage:** Close.

**Core idea.** People buy change, not products. Selling means diagnosing the gap between the buyer's current and desired state and making its size and cost clear.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Current state, future state, the gap:** facts, problem, impact and root cause today; future facts, outcomes (the opposite of today's impact) and emotions.
- **Separate problem, root cause and impact:** Keenan says SPIN blends them.
- **Impact is the motivation to buy:** a big problem with small impact will not close.
- **Discovery drives everything.**

**In the author's words:** "impact is where the motivation to buy exists" (T085).

**Use it when.** Reps demo too early, deals die without a reason, or buyers say "nice, but not a priority."

**Watch out.** A sharp restatement of consultative selling. The author's disdain for rapport does not suit every market.

## [B08] Pitch Anything, Oren Klaff (2011)

**Stage:** Close.

**Core idea.** Pitches are won by controlling the frame and keeping status. The buyer's fast, survival-minded filter drops boring, complex or needy pitches, and you get about twenty minutes of real attention.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Frame control:** the stronger frame absorbs the weaker one.
- **Prizing:** you are the prize; their money is a commodity.
- **STRONG method:** Set the frame, Tell the story, Reveal the intrigue, Offer the prize, Nail the hookpoint, Get a decision.
- **Neediness kills deals:** people chase what moves away; be willing to walk early.

**In the author's words:** "if you're not new and you're not dangerous you get ignored" (T080).

**Use it when.** Pitching investors, large partners or powerful buyers.

**Watch out.** The "croc brain" is a metaphor; Klaff says he is not a biologist. The style can read as arrogant in relationship sales.

## [B09] Fanatical Prospecting, Jeb Blount (2015)

**Stage:** Leads (outbound).

**Core idea.** Empty pipelines cause most sales failure. The cure is disciplined, daily, multichannel prospecting.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Law of Replacement:** add prospects faster than deals close or die.
- **The 30-Day Rule:** this month's prospecting pays off over the next ninety days.
- **Golden Hours:** prospect first thing, before email; blocks can be 15 minutes, daily is what matters.
- **Balanced prospecting:** phone, email, social, in person, referrals.
- **Turnarounds:** sort reflex, brush-off or objection, then anchor, disrupt the pattern, and ask again.
- **Universal Law of Need:** desperation lowers your odds.

**In the author's words:** "the cumulative impact of every single day prospecting is exponential and in terms of filling your pipeline up and filling your bank account up" (T084).

**Use it when.** The pipeline is thin, revenue swings month to month, or reps "don't have time."

**Watch out.** Heavy on phone and volume. Activity without targeting burns lists and brand.

## [B10] Predictable Revenue, Aaron Ross and Marylou Tyler (2011)

**Stage:** Leads (outbound) and Scale (sales roles).

**Core idea.** Revenue becomes predictable when you specialize sales roles and build a dedicated outbound engine instead of asking closers to prospect. From Ross's work at Salesforce.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Specialization:** outbound prospectors, inbound responders, closers who carry quota, post-sales account management.
- **Cold Calling 2.0:** short emails to senior people asking who the right contact is. Success is call conversations, not dials.
- **Seeds, nets and spears:** referral, marketing-driven and targeted outbound leads.
- **Ideal Customer Profile** with red and green flags before scaling outreach.

**In the author's words:** "If you have a predictable way to generate qualified leads, you will generate predictable revenue." (T083) "people don't care what you do. They only care about what you can do for them." (T083)

**Use it when.** The offer is proven, you need more qualified meetings, and you can fund at least one prospector.

**Watch out.** Email tactics are dated and inboxes saturated. Specialization assumes a team; a solo founder plays every role.

## [B11] The Sales Acceleration Formula, Mark Roberge (2015)

**Stage:** Scale (sales team) and Leads (lead value).

**Core idea.** From HubSpot, Roberge treats a sales team as an engineering problem: hire to a data-backed profile, train to a process, coach on metrics, and supply leads predictably.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Hiring formula:** score candidates on about ten criteria and check which predicted results. At HubSpot: coachability (top), curiosity, intelligence, work ethic, prior success. Closing and objection skill in interviews correlated negatively.
- **Training formula:** certify on a documented process rather than shadowing a top rep.
- **Metrics-driven coaching:** start as high in the funnel as the problem appears; coach one or two skills.
- **Lead value:** conversion rate times revenue per customer; a marketing and sales agreement on volume and quality. Demo requests closed about three times better than content downloads.
- **Pay for retained customers.**

**In the author's words:** "the ideal sales hiring formula is different for every company" (T082). "convincing objection handling those were actually negatively correlated with success in our model" (T082).

**Use it when.** Moving from founder-led sales to first hires, or rep results vary wildly.

**Watch out.** Assumes SaaS with inbound volume. A team of two has no statistically useful data.

## [B12] Way of the Wolf, Jordan Belfort (2017)

**Stage:** Close.

**Core idea.** The Straight Line System: every sale moves from open to close, and the seller keeps the buyer on the line while building certainty.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Three Tens:** certainty about the product, you, and your company.
- **Action threshold:** a buyer acts when certainty rises or the step to act gets smaller.
- **A no means not certain:** "let me think about it" signals low certainty, not a dead sale.
- **First four seconds:** come across as sharp, enthusiastic and expert (these three are not the Three Tens).
- **Tonality and looping:** return to build certainty rather than argue.

**In the author's words:** "What no is it's almost a euphemism for saying what no means I am NOT certain That's all it means" (T090). "You have four seconds to establish three crucial things or else you're done" (T090).

**Use it when.** One-call closes and phone sales, or diagnosing why buyers hesitate.

**Watch out.** The author was convicted of securities fraud tied to high-pressure selling. Use the certainty model, not the pressure. Unsuitable for complex B2B.

## [B13] Exactly What to Say, Phil M. Jones (2017)

**Stage:** Close.

**Core idea.** Small wording changes change responses. Short phrase patterns, prepared in advance, lower pressure and invite decisions. He frames the buyer's subconscious as having only yes and no; winning removes "maybe."

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Low-pressure openers:** saying it may not be for them triggers curiosity.
- **Open-minded framing:** ask how open-minded they would be.
- **Imagination prompts, option framing (two clear choices), assumptive next steps.**

**In the author's words:** "we know the worst time to think about the thing you're gonna say is in the moment when you're saying it" (T094).

**Use it when.** The offer is fine but follow-ups and closes stall on awkward wording.

**Watch out.** Works only on top of a good offer and real discovery. Repeated mechanically, it sounds canned.

## [B14] Sell or Be Sold, Grant Cardone (2011)

**Stage:** Close.

**Core idea.** Everyone sells, and the seller who believes most, persists longest and refuses a first no usually wins.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Agree with the buyer,** then guide.
- **Price is rarely the real issue:** the buyer knew the price before calling.
- **Persistence and follow-up:** most sales come after several contacts.
- **Dominate, do not compete:** aim bigger, since rejection costs the same.

**In the author's words:** "every business person has to sell you show me one business that doesn't have to sell" (T095).

**Use it when.** A founder or rep is passive, gives up early, or fears asking.

**Watch out.** Aggressive. Persistence can become pressure that hurts reputation in small, connected markets.

## [B15] Building a StoryBrand, Donald Miller (2017)

**Stage:** Offer (message) and Leads (copy).

**Core idea.** Customers care about their own story. Make the customer the hero and the business the guide, and the message becomes clear enough to act on.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **SB7:** a character wants something, has a problem, meets a guide who gives a plan and calls them to action, helping them avoid failure and reach success.
- **Three levels of problem:** external, internal, philosophical; a villain personifies it.
- **Guide traits:** empathy plus authority, which he calls competency in his talk (T026).
- **Direct and transitional calls to action.**
- **One-liner:** problem, product, result. It is not the tagline (T026).
- **Grunt test** (detail as commonly taught): can a stranger tell in seconds what you offer, how it helps, how to buy.

**In the author's words:** "If you confuse, you will lose." (T026) "It is a massive mistake to position yourself as the hero in the story." (T026)

**Use it when.** The website or pitch is confusing or self-focused.

**Watch out.** Formulaic; sites built on it look alike. It improves clarity, not differentiation.

## [B16] Positioning, Al Ries and Jack Trout (1981)

**Stage:** Offer.

**Core idea.** Marketing is a battle for a place in the customer's mind. Minds are crowded and simplify, so the winner usually got there first or owns a clear, narrow idea.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Product ladders:** people remember only the top few brands per category.
- **Be first, or create a category you can be first in.**
- **Reposition the competition** to open a space.
- **Line extension trap:** one name on many products blurs it.
- **Focus:** a narrow idea gets into the mind more easily than a broad one (T029).

**In the author's words:** "the essence of positioning is to find an open hole in the prospect's mind" (T029)

**Use it when.** You sound like every competitor, or you are choosing what to be known for.

**Watch out.** Mass-media era examples. Being first is not always an advantage; fast followers often win.

## [B17] Obviously Awesome, April Dunford (2019)

**Stage:** Offer.

**Core idea.** Positioning is the context you set so the right buyers see why your product matters. Build it from what makes you different for customers who already love you.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Components:** competitive alternatives, unique attributes, value and proof, best-fit customer, market category, optional relevant trend.
- **Competitive alternatives:** what customers would do if you did not exist, often a spreadsheet or nothing.
- **Category strategies:** head to head, big fish in a small pond, or a new category.
- **Positioning is an input** to messaging and branding, not the same thing (T035).

**In the author's words:** "the component pieces of positioning are competitive alternatives. So, if you didn't exist, what would a customer do?" (T035) "the real question we have to answer is why pick us over the other guys." (T035)

**Use it when.** The product is good but prospects misunderstand it, cycles are long, or prices feel too low.

**Watch out.** Written for B2B tech, and needs some happy customers to work from.

## [B18] Breakthrough Advertising, Eugene Schwartz (1966)

**Stage:** Leads (copy).

**Core idea.** Copy cannot create desire; it finds a desire already in the market and channels it to your product. What you lead with depends on how aware buyers are and how many claims they have heard.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Mass desire:** point an existing, shared want at your product.
- **Five levels of awareness:** most aware, product aware, solution aware, problem aware, unaware.
- **Stages of market sophistication:** simple claim, bigger claim, new mechanism, elaborated mechanism, identification with the buyer.
- **Intensification, identification, gradualization.**

**Use it when.** Writing ads, pages or emails and deciding what to open with.

**Watch out.** Dense and written for mail-order print; examples need translating.

## [B19] Ogilvy on Advertising, David Ogilvy (1983)

**Stage:** Leads (copy).

**Core idea.** Advertising exists to sell. Research, promise a clear benefit, write strong headlines, and build a consistent brand image over years.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Research first** on product, buyer and what has worked.
- **The Big Idea** strong enough to run for years.
- **Headlines carry the benefit:** far more people read them than the body.
- **Respect the reader; long, specific copy can sell.**

**Use it when.** Creative is clever but not selling, or writing any brief.

**Watch out.** Print and TV era; some research figures are dated.

## [B20] Contagious, Jonah Berger (2013)

**Stage:** Leads (word of mouth).

**Core idea.** Things catch on for reasons you can design. Most word of mouth is offline (he puts online at about 7 percent), so design for the psychology, not the platform (T027).

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **STEPPS:** Social currency, Triggers, Emotion, Public, Practical value, Stories.
- **High-arousal emotions** drive sharing; some emotions lower it (T027).
- **Trojan horse stories:** the story cannot be told without the brand (T027).
- **Message over messenger:** he calls influencers overrated (T027).

**In the author's words:** "ideas have a habitat a set of cues or triggers that make people think about them" (T027)

**Use it when.** You want referrals and shares without a big ad budget.

**Watch out.** Explains spread after the fact better than it predicts it.

## [B21] Made to Stick, Chip Heath and Dan Heath (2007)

**Stage:** Offer (message) and Leads (copy).

**Core idea.** Ideas that are remembered and acted on share six traits. The enemy is the curse of knowledge: experts cannot imagine not knowing.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **SUCCESs:** Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Stories. Four traits beat two; all six are not required (T028).
- **Simple is not short:** it is the core people can act on in your absence (T028).
- **Curiosity gap, Sinatra test, human-scale statistics.**

**In the author's words:** "the test of a good simple idea is can you make decisions based on that idea can you take the right action" (T028)

**Use it when.** A pitch, message or training is forgotten or misunderstood.

**Watch out.** About memorability, not conversion.

## [B22] This Is Marketing, Seth Godin (2018)

**Stage:** Offer (market choice).

**Core idea.** Marketing helps people make a change they want, starting with the smallest group you can truly serve. Build trust and tension, speak to status and belonging, and let customers spread the word.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Smallest viable market.**
- **People like us do things like this:** culture drives behavior.
- **Tension and status** (affiliation as well as dominance).
- **Two-axis positioning map:** find an empty corner buyers care about.
- **This is for, this is not for.**
- **Brand versus direct:** the split is measurability, not channel (T030).

**In the author's words:** "The value of a brand is how much extra am I paying above the substitute? And if I'm not paying extra, you don't have a brand." (T030)

**Use it when.** The business appeals to everyone and so competes on price.

**Watch out.** Light on measurement and tactics; can become an excuse not to test or sell.

## [B23] Start With Why, Simon Sinek (2009)

**Stage:** Offer (message).

**Core idea.** Inspiring firms lead with why they exist, then how, then what. People are drawn to those who believe what they believe, and that bond outlasts price promotions.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **The Golden Circle:** why, how, what, communicated inside out.
- **Manipulation versus inspiration:** discounts win sales, not loyalty.
- **Celery test:** drop choices that do not prove the why.
- **A why is found, not invented,** and is always positive (T039).
- **The how is not the why:** your differentiating value proposition belongs to the how (T039).

**In the author's words:** "a why is always positive. It's always striving for something." (T039)

**Use it when.** The brand feels interchangeable or staff cannot say why the business exists.

**Watch out.** Famous cases told after the fact, a simplified brain story, and Sharp's data says most buyers think little about brands [B27]. A why does not replace a clear offer.

## [B24] Hook Point, Brendan Kane (2020)

**Stage:** Leads (content).

**Core idea.** In a feed where people decide in about three seconds, the hook decides whether anything else is seen. Engineer hooks from patterns that already work and test them.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Three-second world** and **two pulls:** curiosity and a promise of value (T042).
- **Five-step hook method** (his 2025 talk): study what works, break down tone and pacing, brainstorm, write at least 20 and pick one, test on three-second retention (T042).
- **One hook, one piece; promise, progress, pay off** (T042).
- **Generalist principle:** layer niche expertise under a broad theme (T042).

**In the author's words:** "make niche content universal. You don't lose your expertise or niche appeal. You layer it underneath a broader theme." (T042) "the primary metric here is the audience retention in the first 3 seconds." (T042)

**Use it when.** Content or video ads get no views.

**Watch out.** Attention is not buying. The five steps come from the 2025 talk and may go beyond the book. Hooks can tip into clickbait.

## [B25] DotCom Secrets, Russell Brunson (2015)

**Stage:** Money Model (value ladder) and Leads (funnels).

**Core idea.** Sell through a sequence of offers, not one page. Map a value ladder from free to premium and build a funnel for each step.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Value Ladder:** offers rising in price and value, each leading to the next.
- **Secret Formula:** who is the dream customer, where they gather, what bait attracts them, what result you give.
- **Hook, Story, Offer.**
- **Attractive Character:** a consistent persona whose stories build connection.
- **Soap opera sequence and daily story emails.**
- **Traffic you earn, control and own:** turn rented traffic into an owned list.

**Use it when.** Selling online, especially info, coaching or services with an upsell path.

**Watch out.** Promotes the author's software and fits info marketing more than local or B2B firms.

## [B26] Crushing It!, Gary Vaynerchuk (2018)

**Stage:** Leads (content).

**Core idea.** Anyone can build a personal brand that becomes a business by putting out lots of content where attention is now, playing to strengths, and being patient for years.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Personal brand as the asset** and main lead source.
- **The essentials** (detail uncertain): intent, authenticity, passion, patience, speed, work, attention.
- **Volume and patience; document, don't create** (detail uncertain).
- **Self-awareness:** build on strengths; hard work is the cost of entry (T041).

**In the author's words:** "i implore you to triple down on your strengths and punt your weaknesses" (T041). Note: T041 is a general keynote, not about the book.

**Use it when.** The founder is the face of the business and buyers trust people over companies.

**Watch out.** Survivor stories; platform chapters date fast. Volume can burn out a small team, and a personal brand is hard to sell with the business [B34].

## [B27] How Brands Grow, Byron Sharp (2010)

**Stage:** Leads (reach).

**Core idea.** From sales data across categories, brands grow mainly by winning more buyers, many of them light buyers, not by deepening loyalty. Be easy to think of and easy to buy.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Double Jeopardy:** smaller brands have fewer and slightly less loyal buyers.
- **Penetration drives growth;** reach all category buyers.
- **Mental and physical availability.**
- **Distinctive brand assets** over claimed differentiation.
- **Polygamous loyalty:** buyers rotate among a few brands (T033).

**In the author's words:** "the evidence is that people do not see Brands as people" (T033)

**Use it when.** A broad-appeal business targets too narrowly or overinvests in loyalty schemes.

**Watch out.** Evidence leans on consumer goods and larger brands. A tiny B2B firm may still need focus.

## [B28] The E-Myth Revisited, Michael E. Gerber (1995)

**Stage:** Scale.

**Core idea.** Most small businesses are started by technicians who think knowing the work means knowing how to run a business. Build it like a franchise prototype on documented systems so it does not depend on the owner. Gerber blames the owner's technician mindset, not the market.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Entrepreneurial seizure;** the Entrepreneur, Manager and Technician inside every owner.
- **Work on the business, not in it.**
- **Franchise prototype:** ordinary people deliver a consistent result by following systems.
- **Innovate, quantify, orchestrate.**
- **Business development program:** starts with the owner's Primary Aim (the life wanted), then Strategic Objective, an org chart of positions, then the other strategies.

**In the author's words:** "Knowing how to do the work in a business has nothing to do with creating a business that works." (T002) "I'm going to suggest you go to work on your business as though you were going to replicate it 5,000 times." (T002)

**Use it when.** The owner is the bottleneck or quality depends on who does the job.

**Watch out.** A repetitive parable. Full systemization can feel wrong where the owner's judgment is the product. His failure statistics come from talks and may differ from the book.

## [B29] Good to Great, Jim Collins (2001)

**Stage:** Scale.

**Core idea.** Companies that jumped from average to strong results got there through disciplined people, thought and action compounding over years, not one launch or a celebrity CEO.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Level 5 Leadership:** humility plus fierce will.
- **First Who, Then What.**
- **Stockdale Paradox:** faith you will win plus facing brutal facts.
- **Hedgehog Concept:** what you can be best at, what drives your economic engine (one profit per X ratio), what your people care about.
- **Flywheel versus doom loop.**

**Use it when.** The business is decent but stuck, or the owner keeps chasing new ideas.

**Watch out.** Picks winners in hindsight, and several later struggled. Large public companies; translate for a 20-person firm.

## [B30] Traction, Gino Wickman (2007)

**Stage:** Scale.

**Core idea.** The Entrepreneurial Operating System (EOS): a simple toolset for running a small or mid-size company on six components and a weekly and quarterly rhythm.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Six components:** Vision, People, Data, Issues, Process, Traction (Rocks plus meetings).
- **Vision/Traction Organizer:** a core (core values, core focus) plus six sections for the plan.
- **Accountability Chart; right people, right seats** (get it, want it, capacity).
- **Rocks, Scorecard, Level 10 Meeting with Identify, Discuss, Solve.**
- **Visionary and Integrator.**

**In the author's words:** "You cannot build a great company on multiple operating systems. You must choose one." (T004) "I don't know if you knew this, but core values plus core focus equals soul." (T004)

**Use it when.** Roughly 10 to 250 people with missed priorities, unclear roles and wandering meetings.

**Watch out.** A branded system with paid implementers; light on strategy and market choice.

## [B31] Zero to One, Peter Thiel and Blake Masters (2014)

**Stage:** Scale (strategy).

**Core idea.** Progress comes from creating something new, not copying. Great businesses escape competition by building a kind of monopoly in a market they can dominate.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Competition is for losers.**
- **Monopoly traits:** proprietary technology, network effects, economies of scale, branding.
- **Start small and monopolize,** then expand.
- **Secrets and last-mover advantage; the seven questions.**

**In the author's words:** "there isn't that much progress in science or Tech or not as much as Silicon Valley likes to claim" (T006)

**Use it when.** Choosing what to build or which market to attack.

**Watch out.** Venture-backed tech. Most small firms will not be monopolies, but "dominate a small niche" still applies. The talk does not cover the book's frameworks.

## [B32] The Lean Startup, Eric Ries (2011)

**Stage:** Scale (new products) and Offer (testing demand).

**Core idea.** Under high uncertainty, learn what customers will pay for as fast and cheap as possible. A startup is defined by uncertainty, not size. Plans are hypotheses with predictions, tested on real behavior.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Build-Measure-Learn** and the **MVP.**
- **Leap-of-faith assumptions:** value and growth.
- **Validated learning and innovation accounting** over vanity metrics; the failure mode is building the wrong thing well.
- **Pivot or persevere:** a pivot changes one element at a time; it is not a synonym for changing direction.
- **Engines of growth:** sticky, viral, paid.

**In the author's words:** "the biggest waste that product development faces today is not building things inefficiently but building things very efficiently that nobody wants" (T005)

**Use it when.** Launching a product, service or market with unknown demand.

**Watch out.** Less useful where the answer is execution. A poor MVP can hurt a brand in a small market.

## [B33] Profit First, Mike Michalowicz (2014)

**Stage:** Money Model (cash).

**Core idea.** Owners spend whatever is in the bank. Flip the formula so profit comes off every deposit first: same math, swapped variables; the point is behavior.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Sales minus Profit equals Expenses;** profit is a habit, not an event.
- **Small plates:** spending grows to fit the money available.
- **Separate accounts:** Income, Profit, Owner's Comp, Tax, Operating Expenses.
- **Target allocation percentages,** reached in small steps (exact percentages not taken from the talk).
- **Allocate on the 10th and 25th;** quarterly profit distributions.

**In the author's words:** "that sales - profit equals expenses and what you'll notice right away is it's the same variable" (T009) "the solution is radically simple get smaller plates don't change yourself get smaller plates" (T009)

**Use it when.** Sales come in but money never stays, or the owner underpays themselves.

**Watch out.** A behavioral cash system, not accounting or tax advice. Rigid percentages can starve a business that needs investment.

## [B34] Built to Sell, John Warrillow (2010)

**Stage:** Scale (exit).

**Core idea.** An owner-dependent business is hard to sell. A fable on turning a custom service firm into a standardized, sellable company.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Teachable, valuable, repeatable:** rank offers; repeatable means customers rebuy often, not that you can repeat the process.
- **Stop custom work.**
- **Recurring revenue and up-front payment;** contract revenue ranks above consumables, and a positive cash cycle raises the price.
- **Avoid customer concentration.**
- **Hire two salespeople at once** for the standard offer; managers on long-term incentives.

**In the author's words:** "when a buyer buys your business they've actually got to write two checks" (T014)

**Use it when.** Planning an exit, or wanting a business that runs without you.

**Watch out.** Service-firm fable; valuation multiples vary widely.

## [B35] The Art of War, Sun Tzu (c. 5th century BC)

**Stage:** Scale (competition).

**Core idea.** Win conflicts at least cost: weigh your position against the rival before acting, win by position rather than open battle, keep campaigns short, adapt to the ground. Public domain; Giles translation (T101).

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Five constant factors:** the Moral Law, Heaven (timing), Earth (terrain), the Commander, Method and discipline.
- **Seven comparisons** before battle.
- **Win without fighting:** defeat plans first, alliances next, armies last.
- **Avoid strength, strike weakness; avoid long campaigns.**

**In the author's words:** "Thus do many calculations lead to victory, and few calculations to defeat: how much more no calculation at all!" (T101) "supreme excellence consists in breaking the enemy's resistance without fighting." (T101)

**Use it when.** Facing a direct rival, a price war or a new entrant.

**Watch out.** Customers are not enemies, and the line on deception is about feints, not misleading anyone. The popular "subdue the enemy without fighting" is a paraphrase.

## [B36] Blue Ocean Strategy, W. Chan Kim and Renee Mauborgne (2005)

**Stage:** Scale (strategy) and Offer (differentiation).

**Core idea.** Instead of fighting in crowded red oceans, create uncontested space by raising buyer value while lowering cost. Kim stresses this need not disrupt an incumbent.

**Key frameworks and ideas**
- **Strategy Canvas** and the **ERRC grid:** Eliminate, Reduce, Raise, Create.
- **Six Paths** to look across industries, groups and time.
- **Three tiers of noncustomers.**

**In the author's words:** "what I mean by Blu ocean perspective is that we say for example focus on non customer to create new market space" (T007)

**Use it when.** The industry is commoditized, prices fall, and every rival looks the same.

**Watch out.** Cases picked in hindsight; blue oceans attract copycats fast.
