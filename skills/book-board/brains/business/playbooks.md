# Playbooks: Business department

Common owner situations, each tagged with its spine stage, built from the 36 Business books (B01 to B36) and the frameworks in frameworks.md. Quote policy: verbatim quotes appear only from author talks and public-domain texts, always with a T id; copyrighted book text is never quoted; everything else is paraphrase cited by book id.

Every playbook starts the same way: confirm the stage tag is really the constraint. If the "Ask first" answers point to an earlier stage, go to that playbook instead (see spine.md).

## 1. "I don't get enough leads"
**Stage:** Leads
**Ask first**
- How many engaged leads came last month, and from which source?
- Which of the Core Four (warm outreach, free content, cold outreach, paid ads) do you run, and how often?
- When leads do come, what share buy? (If qualified leads rarely buy, check Offer and Close first.)
- What does a customer pay you in the first 30 days and over a year?
**Plan**
1. List every source against the Core Four and find the one already working best [B02].
2. Apply More, Better, New: raise volume on that source before adding any other [B02].
3. Set a fixed daily number of lead actions, scaled to your size, and track it for 90 days (Rule of 100) [B02, B09].
4. Add one lead magnet that solves a narrow problem for your best buyer and points to the paid offer [B02, B25].
5. Ask every happy customer for a referral at the moment of delivered value; this is your first Lead Getter [B02, B10].
6. Track each lead from source to sale [B19, B11].
**Metric:** engaged leads per week by source, and cost per engaged lead.
**Common mistake:** adding a new channel before the current one runs at full volume.

## 2. "My ads cost too much and don't work"
**Stage:** Leads
**Ask first**
- What are cost per lead and cost per customer, against 30-day gross profit per customer?
- How aware of the problem is the audience you show the ad to?
- What does the first line of the ad and the landing page say?
- Do people who reach the page want the offer, or is the ad doing all the work?
**Plan**
1. Check tracking first: can you see which ad produced which sale [B19]?
2. Match the opening to awareness: problem-first for cold audiences, proof and offer for warm ones [B18].
3. Rewrite the headline around one specific benefit for a named reader [B19, B21].
4. Run the landing page through the SB7 clarity test [B15].
5. Test three hooks at a time with the offer held constant [B24, B25].
6. If no hook wins, the constraint is the Offer: rebuild it with the Value Equation [B01].
7. If ads cost more than 30-day profit, fix the Money Model before spending more [B03].
**Metric:** cost per acquired customer against 30-day gross profit per customer.
**Common mistake:** changing audience, creative and offer at once, so nothing is learned.

## 3. "We're treated as a commodity and compete on price"
**Stage:** Offer
**Ask first**
- What would your best customers do if you did not exist?
- What do customers who pay more say they get from you?
- Do you sell hours and units, or an outcome?
- Who do buyers line you up against?
**Plan**
1. Interview five to ten best customers and work through the Positioning Components [B17].
2. List every obstacle the buyer faces and turn each into part of a Grand Slam Offer [B01].
3. Score the Value Equation levers from the buyer's view and fix the weakest [B01].
4. Pick a narrower category or segment you can lead [B16, B17].
5. If the whole industry looks the same, draw a Strategy Canvas and use the ERRC grid [B36].
6. Raise price with the new offer, not with an apology [B01].
**Metric:** average price per sale and share of sales won without discount.
**Common mistake:** cutting price, which makes the commodity problem worse.

## 4. "Buyers keep saying it's too expensive"
**Stage:** Close (check Offer)
**Ask first**
- Do they say it before or after they understand the value?
- Is it price, budget or cash flow?
- Who are they comparing you to?
- What do your best customers pay, and why did they say yes?
**Plan**
1. Ask a calibrated question to find the real concern under the objection [B06].
2. Go back to discovery and quantify the impact, so price is weighed against the cost of the problem [B07, B04].
3. If many buyers say it, the constraint is the Offer: raise value on the Value Equation [B01].
4. Present a small number of options with a clear recommendation [B13].
5. For those who still say no, offer a planned downsell such as a payment plan or lighter version, not a discount [B03].
6. Add risk reversal you can afford [B01, B12].
**Metric:** win rate on proposals and average sale value.
**Common mistake:** discounting at once, which teaches buyers to always ask.

## 5. "I want to raise prices"
**Stage:** Offer
**Ask first**
- What results do customers get, and can you prove them?
- What is your close rate today?
- Which customers would leave, and would that hurt?
**Plan**
1. Quantify the outcomes clients get [B07, B01].
2. Improve the Value Equation levers before announcing the new price [B01].
3. Stack low-cost, high-value additions and name the offer [B01].
4. Test the new price on new prospects first (synthesis of [B01, B11]).
5. Keep a planned downsell for those who balk [B03].
6. Give existing clients notice and explain the change with empathy [B06].
7. Watch close rate: a very high close rate often means the price is too low [B01].
**Metric:** revenue per customer and close rate after the change.
**Common mistake:** raising price without changing the offer or the story.

## 6. "We sound like everyone else"
**Stage:** Offer
**Ask first**
- How do prospects describe you today?
- What do you have that the alternatives lack?
- Which customers love you most, and what do they share?
**Plan**
1. Work the Positioning Components: alternatives, unique attributes, value, best-fit buyer, category [B17].
2. Choose one idea to own in the buyer's mind [B16].
3. Write a one-liner of problem, product and result, with the customer as hero [B15].
4. Make claims specific and concrete [B21, B19].
5. Decide what you will stop saying or selling to keep the focus [B16, B22].
**Metric:** share of sales calls where the prospect repeats your positioning back.
**Common mistake:** picking a difference buyers do not care about.

## 7. "My website gets traffic but no inquiries"
**Stage:** Leads
**Ask first**
- Can a stranger tell what you offer within seconds?
- Is there one obvious next step?
- How aware are the visitors, and where do they come from?
**Plan**
1. Run the SB7 grunt test on the homepage [B15].
2. Put one direct call to action and one transitional offer, such as a lead magnet, above the fold [B15, B02].
3. Lead with the buyer's problem and result, not your history [B15, B18].
4. Add specific proof: numbers, named results, reviews [B19, B21].
5. Answer price and common objections on the page (synthesis of [B15, B01]).
**Metric:** visitor to inquiry rate on key pages.
**Common mistake:** redesigning visuals while the words stay vague.

## 8. "Nobody refers us"
**Stage:** Leads
**Ask first**
- Do you ask for referrals, and when?
- Is there anything about the experience people would mention?
- Can others see a customer using you?
**Plan**
1. Check delivery first: people refer results, not promises [B01].
2. Score the experience on STEPPS and add one thing worth telling [B20].
3. Make use visible or shareable [B20].
4. Ask at the moment of peak satisfaction with a ready-made message [B02].
5. Build customers into a Lead Getter program with a simple reward [B02].
**Metric:** share of new customers who came by referral.
**Common mistake:** paying cash bounties for an experience nobody finds worth talking about.

## 9. "I don't know what to post, and nobody knows who I am"
**Stage:** Leads
**Ask first**
- What questions do prospects ask before buying?
- Which one platform do your buyers use most?
- Are you better talking, writing or showing?
- How many hours a week can you give this for a year?
**Plan**
1. Pick one platform and one format that fit your strengths [B26, B02].
2. Build topics from buyer questions and your real work (synthesis of [B26, B02]).
3. Use hook, retain, reward in every post, and write the hook first [B02, B24].
4. Frame niche expertise under a broad theme strangers care about [B24].
5. Give mostly value, with a clear ask on a set rhythm [B26, B02].
6. Track which posts bring inquiries, not only views [B19].
**Metric:** inquiries per month that mention your content.
**Common mistake:** posting on five platforms irregularly, then quitting in month two.

## 10. "My pipeline is empty or lumpy" (B2B)
**Stage:** Leads
**Ask first**
- How many hours a week does anyone prospect?
- Where did your last ten customers come from?
- Do you have a written ideal customer profile?
**Plan**
1. Work back from your close rate to the number of new conversations you need each month (Law of Replacement) [B09].
2. Write an ideal customer profile with red and green flags, and build a target list [B10].
3. Block daily prospecting time first thing; 15-minute bursts count [B09].
4. Run outreach across phone, email and social; short, referral-style emails work best (Cold Calling 2.0) [B10, B09].
5. Prepare short answers for the three most common brush-offs [B09].
**Metric:** new qualified conversations per week, not dials [B10].
**Common mistake:** prospecting only when revenue drops, so the gap repeats each quarter.

## 11. "My close rate dropped"
**Stage:** Close
**Ask first**
- Did it drop at every step, or at one step?
- Did lead sources, price, offer or reps change in the last 90 days?
- Are deals lost to competitors or to no decision?
**Plan**
1. Break the funnel into steps and find the one that fell (Metrics-Driven Coaching) [B11].
2. If the lead mix changed, the constraint may be Leads: check fit against your ideal customer profile [B10].
3. Review five lost deals for thin discovery: did the buyer state impact and root cause [B07, B04]?
4. Sort stalls into no impact versus low certainty (Stalled Deal Diagnosis) [B07, B12].
5. If every rep dropped at once, look at the Offer before the people [B01].
6. Coach one skill per rep for 30 days, then remeasure [B11].
**Metric:** conversion at the step that fell.
**Common mistake:** adding discounts or more leads before finding which step broke.

## 12. "Deals stall at 'let me think about it' or ghost after the proposal"
**Stage:** Close
**Ask first**
- Did the buyer state the problem and its cost?
- Was a review meeting booked before the proposal went out?
- Has the person who signs seen it?
**Plan**
1. Diagnose no impact versus low certainty [B07, B12].
2. For low certainty, find the weakest of the Three Tens (product, you, company) and address it [B12].
3. Label the hesitation calmly and ask what would need to be true to move [B06].
4. Recommend one option and lower the action threshold: a small start, pilot or guarantee [B12, B13, B01].
5. Never send a proposal without a dated review meeting on both calendars [B04].
6. Follow up with a short message that restates their gap in their words [B07, B06].
**Metric:** share of late-stage deals lost to no decision.
**Common mistake:** sending more case studies and options, or "just checking in".

## 13. "Leads come in but don't buy"
**Stage:** Close (check Leads)
**Ask first**
- Are the leads your best-fit buyer?
- How fast do you respond?
- What happens between first contact and the sales call?
**Plan**
1. Check targeting and the lead magnet: are they drawing the right person [B02, B17]?
2. Respond fast and have a set number of follow-up attempts (detail of counts varies by deal size) [B11].
3. Send content before the call that answers common questions (synthesis of [B15, B25]).
4. Run discovery with SPIN or Gap Analysis instead of pitching early [B04, B07].
5. If qualified buyers still say no, the constraint is the Offer [B01].
**Metric:** lead to customer rate by source.
**Common mistake:** blaming the sales call when marketing attracts the wrong people.

## 14. "I hate selling and avoid it"
**Stage:** Close
**Ask first**
- What feels worst: calling, asking for money, or rejection?
- Do you believe the product helps the buyer?
- How many clear asks did you make last week?
**Plan**
1. Reframe selling as problem solving for the buyer [B07, B04].
2. Lead with discovery questions, which feels less pushy [B04, B07].
3. Use low-pressure phrasing that lets the buyer say no [B13].
4. Make the offer strong enough that asking feels like helping [B01].
5. Count asks, not wins, as a small daily habit. For the fear itself, hand off to Mindset; for the calendar, to Productivity.
**Metric:** clear asks per week.
**Common mistake:** hiring a salesperson before the sale is repeatable.

## 15. "Negotiating a big contract"
**Stage:** Close
**Ask first**
- What is your walk-away point and best alternative?
- Who on their side can agree, and who might feel threatened?
- What do they value besides price?
**Plan**
1. Prepare an accusation audit and the likely objections [B06].
2. Use labels, mirrors and calibrated questions to surface real needs [B06].
3. Trade, do not concede: every give gets a get (synthesis of [B06, B01]).
4. On price, plan concessions in shrinking steps (Ackerman Bargaining) [B06].
5. Hold your frame and stay willing to walk [B08].
**Metric:** final margin against target, and time to signature.
**Common mistake:** splitting the difference to end the discomfort.

## 16. "Building a sales team or hiring my first salesperson"
**Stage:** Close
**Ask first**
- Can you close deals yourself, repeatably, with a known process?
- Will the hire get leads, or must they find them?
- Can you afford a ramp period?
**Plan**
1. Write down your own sales process and buyer journey first [B11].
2. Define the traits that predict success in your sale, coachability first in Roberge's data (Hiring Formula) [B11].
3. Decide whether the role prospects, closes or manages accounts (Specialized Sales Roles) [B10].
4. Pay for customers who stay, not only for signatures [B11].
5. Coach weekly on one skill chosen from funnel data [B11].
6. For interviewing and the wider hiring process, hand off to Relationships and Leadership [R07].
**Metric:** time to first deal and month-three conversion against yours.
**Common mistake:** hiring a big-company rep who relied on brand and inbound leads.

## 17. "Customers buy once and don't come back"
**Stage:** Money Model
**Ask first**
- Did they get the result they bought?
- What do you offer after the first sale, and when?
- Who owns the relationship after delivery?
**Plan**
1. Check delivery against the promise; no next offer fixes a broken first one [B01, B03].
2. Map the Money Model: what is the upsell right after the yes, and what continuity keeps them [B03]?
3. Build a value ladder so each customer has a clear next step [B25].
4. Separate account management from new business if team size allows [B10].
5. Ask for referrals once results are visible [B02].
**Metric:** repeat purchase rate and revenue per customer in the first 90 days.
**Common mistake:** treating retention as a marketing problem when the promise was not kept.

## 18. "Getting a customer costs more than they pay us at first"
**Stage:** Money Model
**Ask first**
- What does it cost to acquire one customer, all in?
- What gross profit does a customer bring in the first 30 days, and in the first year?
- What happens to buyers who say no to price?
**Plan**
1. Calculate 30-day gross profit per customer against acquisition cost [B03].
2. Add an upsell at the point of sale that helps the buyer get the result faster [B03].
3. Add a downsell, such as a payment plan or lighter version, for those who say no [B03].
4. Consider an attraction offer that brings buyers in and leads to the core offer [B03, B25].
5. Collect cash earlier: deposits, up-front billing or recurring payment [B34, B03].
6. Only then raise spend on the Core Four [B02].
**Metric:** 30-day gross profit per customer divided by acquisition cost.
**Common mistake:** spending more on ads to fix a money model that loses on each customer.

## 19. "We're profitable but always out of cash"
**Stage:** Money Model
**Ask first**
- How many days from paying for inputs to being paid by customers?
- Do customers pay deposits or up front?
- How much profit goes to debt, owner draws or tax surprises?
**Plan**
1. Get paid sooner: deposits, up-front or recurring billing [B34, B03].
2. Set up Profit First accounts, including one for tax, and allocate on a fixed rhythm (exact percentages vary by business) [B33].
3. Review operating costs against the smaller amount left for them [B33].
4. Build a cash reserve of a set number of months of costs [B33].
5. Check tax or debt changes with an accountant.
**Metric:** month-end cash balance and days from paying to being paid.
**Common mistake:** chasing more sales, which makes a long cash cycle worse.

## 20. "Revenue has plateaued"
**Stage:** Scale (find the real constraint first)
**Ask first**
- If leads doubled tomorrow, could you close and deliver them profitably?
- Which stage looks weakest in the numbers: offer, leads, close, money model, or capacity to deliver?
- Which customers and offers make the most profit?
**Plan**
1. Get the real numbers by stage, offer and customer [B29, B30].
2. Name the constraint stage and go to its playbook; most plateaus are Leads or Offer, not systems (synthesis of [B01, B02]).
3. If the limit is delivery capacity or the owner, standardize and systemize the core offer [B28, B34].
4. Check focus with the Hedgehog Concept and cut low-profit lines [B29].
5. Set one 90-day Rock aimed at the constraint and track it weekly [B30].
**Metric:** profitable customers served per month.
**Common mistake:** buying more leads when the business cannot deliver more work.

## 21. "The business can't run without me"
**Stage:** Scale
**Ask first**
- What breaks if you disappear for two weeks?
- Which tasks only you can do, and which only you do?
- Is the main offer standard or custom each time?
**Plan**
1. Draw an org chart of positions and mark every box with your name [B28, B30].
2. Write down how the most repeated work is done, step by step (Franchise Prototype) [B28].
3. Standardize the core offer so others can deliver it (the teachable, valuable, repeatable test) [B34].
4. Hand off one box at a time with clear outcomes; for managing people, hand off to Relationships and Leadership [R08].
5. Block weekly time to work on the business; for the calendar itself, hand off to Productivity.
**Metric:** share of revenue delivered without the owner.
**Common mistake:** delegating tasks without the outcome or system, then taking them back.

## 22. "I want to sell the business in 3 to 5 years"
**Stage:** Scale
**Ask first**
- Could the business run for three months without you?
- How much revenue is recurring or contracted?
- Who sells, and who manages delivery?
**Plan**
1. Score Built to Sell Readiness [B34].
2. Standardize the main offer and stop owner-dependent custom work [B34, B28].
3. Move clients to recurring or up-front billing [B34, B03].
4. Fill management seats on an Accountability Chart [B30].
5. Keep clean weekly numbers a buyer can trust [B30].
6. Prove it with an extended owner absence [B34]. Confirm valuation and deal terms with a professional.
**Metric:** share of revenue that arrives without the owner selling or delivering.
**Common mistake:** starting to reduce owner dependence a year before the sale.

## 23. "Should we launch a new product or enter a new market?"
**Stage:** Scale (test the Offer first)
**Ask first**
- Does it fit what you can be best at and what drives your economics?
- Who already holds that ground, and where are they weak?
- What is the cheapest real test with paying buyers?
**Plan**
1. Check Hedgehog fit [B29].
2. Compare your position with incumbents and enter only where you win (Five Factors) [B35].
3. Pick one narrow segment you can dominate first [B31].
4. Build a Grand Slam Offer for that segment and test it as an MVP for real money or commitments [B01, B32].
5. Set a pivot or persevere date [B32].
**Metric:** paying customers or pre-orders from the test segment.
**Common mistake:** launching in full because the idea is exciting, starving the core.

## 24. "A bigger or cheaper competitor is attacking us"
**Stage:** Scale
**Ask first**
- Which customers are leaving, and are they your most or least profitable?
- Is the rival good enough for them, or just cheaper?
- Where do you already win?
**Plan**
1. Map who is leaving and why [B35].
2. Draw a Strategy Canvas for you and the rival [B36].
3. Fight only on ground where you are stronger; do not answer on their strong ground [B35].
4. Strengthen the offer for the customers you keep with the ERRC grid and the Value Equation [B36, B01].
5. Keep any fight short; long price wars drain cash [B35, B33].
**Metric:** gross margin and retention in your core segment.
**Common mistake:** cutting prices across the board.
