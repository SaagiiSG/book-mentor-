# Frameworks: Psychology and Mental Health department

Built from the 15 Psychology and Mental Health books (H01 to H15), most useful first. Names follow the books where they have one; explanations are ours. Scores are guidance for the advisor, not from the books. Quote policy: verbatim quotes appear only from the author talks in talks.md, always with a T id; copyrighted book text is never quoted; everything else is paraphrase with book ids.

These are self-help tools for ordinary stress and struggle. None of them diagnoses anything or replaces a doctor or licensed therapist. If a situation involves risk to life, severe symptoms or substances, follow the Safety section in _hot.md first.

## 1. Complete the Stress Cycle
**Sources:** [H08]; body-first regulation [H02].
**What it is:** treating the stress response in the body as separate from the stressor, and finishing it with something physical or social even before the problem is solved.
**Steps:** 1) Name the stressor (what is out there). 2) Name the stress (what your body is doing: tight chest, racing thoughts, clenched jaw). 3) Pick one cycle completer: 20 to 60 minutes of movement, slow breathing, a warm chat, a real laugh, a long hug with someone you trust, a cry, or making something. 4) Notice the shift. 5) Then plan the stressor separately.
**When to use:** daily, and any time an owner says they will relax when the deal closes or the hire starts.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = no cycle completers most days; 3 = some, irregular; 5 = at least one every day, chosen on purpose.
**Example:** a restaurant owner who lies awake replaying service starts a 25-minute walk after close and a 10-minute call with her sister on Sundays. The supplier problem is still there, but she falls asleep faster.

## 2. Thought Record (Triple Column and Daily Mood Log)
**Sources:** [H03]; defusion as a complement [H07].
**What it is:** writing an upsetting moment down, catching the automatic thoughts, naming their distortions and writing more accurate responses.
**Steps:** 1) Describe one specific upsetting moment. 2) Rate each emotion 0 to 100. 3) List the automatic thoughts and how much you believe each. 4) Name the distortions (see framework 3). 5) Write a response you actually believe. 6) Rerate belief and emotions.
**When to use:** spiraling thoughts after a setback, catastrophizing about cash or a client, perfectionism.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = belief in the thought unchanged; 3 = belief down by a third; 5 = belief and emotion down by half or more.
**Example:** after losing a bid, a founder writes the thought that the company is finished. He tags it fortune-telling and all-or-nothing, notes two bids won this quarter, and writes that this loss is real but one of many tries.

## 3. Ten Distortions Check
**Sources:** [H03]; substitution and story bias [H01].
**What it is:** a checklist of common thinking traps used to test a thought: all-or-nothing, overgeneralization, mental filter, discounting the positive, mind reading, fortune-telling, magnifying or minimizing, emotional reasoning, should statements, labeling, personalization and blame.
**Steps:** 1) Write the thought in one sentence. 2) Go down the list and mark every trap present. 3) For each mark, ask what evidence a fair friend would point to. 4) Rewrite.
**When to use:** as the fast version of framework 2, or before sending an angry email.
**Score (guidance):** count the traps; 0 to 1 = probably a fair read; 2 to 3 = rewrite before acting; 4 or more = do not act on it today.
**Example:** a shop owner thinks a bad review means everyone hates her. She marks overgeneralization, mental filter and labeling, and checks that 94 of her last 100 reviews were positive.

## 4. Defusion: Notice the Thought
**Sources:** [H07]; observing thoughts [H14]; System 1 stories [H01].
**What it is:** stepping back from a thought so it becomes words in your head rather than a command or a fact.
**Steps:** 1) Catch the sticky thought. 2) Say it with a prefix: I'm having the thought that. 3) Optionally name the story (the not good enough story). 4) Ask whether following this thought helps you act on your values right now. 5) Let it stay and act anyway, or act on it if it is useful.
**When to use:** rumination, worry that cannot be solved by evidence, intrusive self-criticism.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = fully fused, the thought decides; 3 = can notice it but it still drives; 5 = notices, names and chooses the action.
**Example:** before a pitch a founder notices the thought that investors will see through him. He names it the fraud story and walks in with it still there.

## 5. Values Compass
**Sources:** [H07]; junk values [H12]; community feeling [H10].
**What it is:** picking two or three values, defined as qualities of action, and using them to choose behavior, especially when feelings are hard.
**Steps:** 1) List values in one or two words each (fair, curious, steady, generous). 2) Choose two or three for this week. 3) For each, write one concrete action. 4) At day's end note when you lived them. 5) Check that they are not disguised goals or rigid rules (T117).
**When to use:** loss of meaning, anxious avoidance, decisions where every option is uncomfortable.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = no named values; 3 = named but not used in decisions; 5 = used weekly to choose actions.
**Example:** an agency owner picks honest and generous. He tells a client the campaign underperformed before they ask, and mentors a junior for 30 minutes.

## 6. Self-Compassion Break
**Sources:** [H14]; common humanity and shame resilience [H09].
**What it is:** a short practice with three parts: acknowledge this is a moment of struggle, remember struggle is part of being human, and offer yourself a kind phrase.
**Steps:** 1) Pick a mildly distressing issue first (T138). 2) Say: this is hard. 3) Say: other people in my position feel this too. 4) Offer a kind line you would say to a friend. 5) Optionally a hand on the chest. 6) Then ask what you need.
**When to use:** harsh self-talk after a mistake, before a hard conversation, at the end of a bad day.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = self-attack only; 3 = can do it when calm; 5 = uses it in the moment of failure.
**Example:** a founder who missed payroll by a day does the break in the car before telling staff, then explains the fix calmly.

## 7. Tender and Fierce Self-Compassion
**Sources:** [H14]; boundaries [H15].
**What it is:** the soothing side (comfort, acceptance) and the protective side (saying no, setting limits, making needed changes).
**Steps:** 1) Ask what you need right now: comfort, protection, provision or motivation. 2) If comfort, use framework 6. 3) If protection, name the limit and what you will do if crossed. 4) If change, set one step framed as care, not punishment.
**When to use:** when kindness to self feels like letting yourself off the hook.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = only harshness or only soothing; 3 = both but rarely matched to need; 5 = picks the right side for the moment.
**Example:** a consultant who keeps taking 10 pm client calls decides, as an act of care, to stop answering after 7 and says so to clients.

## 8. When to Trust Your Gut
**Sources:** [H01] (T110).
**What it is:** a test for whether intuition is skilled recognition or just a confident feeling.
**Steps:** 1) Is the environment regular, with patterns that repeat? 2) Have you had long practice in it? 3) Did you get fast, clear feedback? 4) If all three are yes, intuition is a useful input. 5) If any is no, use data, a formula or an outside view.
**When to use:** hiring, pricing, market bets, choosing partners.
**Score (guidance):** count yes answers: 0 to 1 = do not trust the gut; 2 = treat it as a hypothesis; 3 = trust it, with a check.
**Example:** a 20-year carpenter can trust his estimate for a deck, but not his gut about a new franchise market he has never sold in.

## 9. Premortem
**Sources:** [H01], crediting Gary Klein.
**What it is:** imagining that the plan has failed a year from now and writing why, before committing.
**Steps:** 1) Gather the people involved. 2) Say: it is a year from now and this failed. 3) Each writes reasons alone for five minutes. 4) Share and group them. 5) Change the plan for the top two or three.
**When to use:** big launches, hires, leases, any decision where the group is excited and confident.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = no risks named; 3 = risks named but plan unchanged; 5 = plan changed for the top risks.
**Example:** before signing a second-location lease, a cafe owner and her manager list staffing and parking; she adds a clause and delays the opening by two months.

## 10. Outside View
**Sources:** [H01].
**What it is:** estimating from how similar projects actually went (a reference class) before adjusting for your own specifics, to correct the planning fallacy.
**Steps:** 1) Name the reference class. 2) Find the typical result for it (time, cost, success rate). 3) Start from that number. 4) Adjust modestly for real differences. 5) Write the estimate down and compare later.
**When to use:** timelines, budgets, sales forecasts, "this time is different" thinking.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = inside view only; 3 = outside view checked but ignored; 5 = estimate anchored on the reference class.
**Example:** a founder plans to build an app in three months; past projects in her network took eight to ten, so she budgets nine.

## 11. Substitution Check
**Sources:** [H01] (T110).
**What it is:** spotting when your mind answered an easier question than the one you face.
**Steps:** 1) Write the real question. 2) Write the question you actually answered (often: do I like this, does this feel familiar, how do I feel today). 3) If they differ, answer the real one with evidence.
**When to use:** hiring by likability, investing because a story is vivid, judging a strategy by mood.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = answered the easy question; 3 = noticed but did not redo; 5 = redid the real question.
**Example:** an owner realizes she rated a candidate highly because the interview was pleasant, then scores the work sample, which is weak.

## 12. Loss Frame Check
**Sources:** [H01].
**What it is:** checking whether a choice feels different only because it is framed as a loss or a gain, and whether sunk costs are driving it.
**Steps:** 1) Restate the choice as a gain and as a loss. 2) If your preference flips, the frame is doing the work. 3) Ignore money already spent. 4) Decide on future costs and benefits only.
**When to use:** keeping a failing product, refusing to cut a price, holding a bad hire.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = frame and sunk cost decide; 3 = noticed; 5 = decision made on future value only.
**Example:** a retailer keeps a slow line because of stock already bought; reframed, the shelf space would earn more with a better seller.

## 13. The 30-Day Abstinence Experiment
**Sources:** [H04] (T114); habit design hands off to Productivity.
**What it is:** stepping away from a compulsive behavior for about a month to let the pleasure-pain balance reset and see how life feels without it.
**Steps:** 1) Pick one behavior (social media, news, late-night work, online trading, gaming). 2) Write what you hope to learn. 3) Expect the first one to two weeks to feel worse. 4) Use self-binding (framework 14). 5) At day 30, decide whether to return, moderate with rules, or keep going.
**When to use:** compulsive digital habits and behaviors. For alcohol, drugs or prescribed medication, never stop suddenly without medical advice: withdrawal can be dangerous. Talk to a doctor first.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = no break taken; 3 = break with lapses; 5 = full 30 days and a written decision at the end.
**Example:** a founder deletes social apps for 30 days; by week three he notices he reads more and his morning anxiety is lower, and he returns with one 20-minute window a day.

## 14. Self-Binding
**Sources:** [H04]; environment design overlaps with Productivity.
**What it is:** putting barriers between you and the trigger, so you rely less on willpower in the moment.
**Steps:** 1) Physical: remove the app, keep the phone in another room, no alcohol in the house. 2) Time: only after 6 pm, only weekends. 3) Category: no news sites at all, rather than less news. 4) Tell one person the rule.
**When to use:** whenever you notice craving beating intentions.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = relying on willpower; 3 = one barrier; 5 = physical, time and category barriers plus someone who knows.
**Example:** an owner who checks sales dashboards 40 times a day removes the app from his phone and checks at 9 am and 4 pm on the laptop.

## 15. Four C's Reflection
**Sources:** [H04] (T114).
**What it is:** a private reflection on whether a habit has become compulsive: out of control, compulsive, craving, continued despite consequences. It is not a diagnosis.
**Steps:** 1) Pick the behavior. 2) For each C, write one honest example from the last month. 3) If several C's apply, or harm is growing, talk to a doctor or addiction professional. 4) If not, use frameworks 13 and 14.
**When to use:** when an owner jokes about being addicted to work, the phone, drinking or betting.
**Score (guidance):** count C's with a clear example: 0 to 1 = habit to adjust; 2 = take seriously and run an experiment; 3 to 4 = recommend a professional.
**Example:** a founder notices craving and continued use despite his partner's complaints about evening drinking. He books a GP appointment to talk it through before changing anything.

## 16. Sleep Opportunity Floor
**Sources:** [H05]; recovery [H08].
**What it is:** protecting a fixed window of about eight hours in bed with a regular wake time, as the base for mood and judgment.
**Steps:** 1) Fix the wake time, including weekends. 2) Count back eight hours for lights out. 3) Wind down for 30 to 60 minutes without work. 4) Keep the room cool and dark. 5) If awake for a long time, get up and do something quiet, then return. 6) Track for two weeks.
**When to use:** owners on five to six hours, late-week decision errors, irritability.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = under six hours most nights; 3 = seven hours some nights; 5 = regular seven to nine hours most nights.
**Example:** a founder sets a 6:30 wake time and a 10:15 wind-down alarm; after two weeks his afternoon slump shortens.

## 17. Caffeine and Alcohol Audit
**Sources:** [H05]; pleasure-pain balance [H04].
**What it is:** checking how caffeine and alcohol timing affect sleep, since caffeine lingers for hours and alcohol fragments sleep.
**Steps:** 1) Log the time and amount of each for a week. 2) Note sleep quality each morning. 3) Try a caffeine cutoff around midday or early afternoon. 4) Notice whether evening drinking is linked to waking at night. 5) Adjust one thing at a time. If you drink heavily every day, talk to a doctor before cutting down.
**When to use:** poor sleep with no obvious cause, or using a nightcap to fall asleep.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = no awareness; 3 = log kept; 5 = one change tested and its effect noted.
**Example:** a bakery owner moves her last coffee from 4 pm to 12 pm and sleeps through more nights.

## 18. Chronotype Fit
**Sources:** [H05] (T115).
**What it is:** arranging the hardest thinking and key meetings around whether you are a morning or evening type, where the business allows.
**Steps:** 1) Note your natural sleep and peak hours on a free week. 2) Put the hardest work in your peak. 3) Move routine tasks to your low hours. 4) Negotiate meeting times with your team.
**When to use:** owners forcing a 5 am routine that leaves them foggy, or couples who fight over bedtimes.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = schedule fights type; 3 = some alignment; 5 = peak hours protected for key work.
**Example:** an evening-type founder moves investor calls to the afternoon and stops booking 7 am meetings.

## 19. Pause the Hijack
**Sources:** [H06]; body-first [H02, H08].
**What it is:** noticing a strong emotion taking over and buying time before you speak or act.
**Steps:** 1) Learn your early signs (heat, tight jaw, faster speech). 2) Name the emotion to yourself. 3) Pause: slow breaths, a short walk, or say you will come back in 20 minutes. 4) Return and decide what you want the outcome to be.
**When to use:** snapping at staff, angry emails, tense negotiations. The words to use with the other person hand off to Relationships and Leadership.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = reacts instantly; 3 = pauses sometimes, after the damage; 5 = pauses reliably before acting.
**Example:** a construction owner who used to shout on site now walks to his truck for five minutes when a delivery is wrong, then calls the supplier.

## 20. Five Steps to Grow One EI Skill
**Sources:** [H06] (T116).
**What it is:** a plan to improve one emotional or social competency at a time.
**Steps:** 1) Decide whether you really care about improving. 2) Get an honest assessment from people who see you (a simple 360). 3) Pick one competency. 4) Write a learning plan with specific moments to practice. 5) Practice at every natural opportunity for three to six months.
**When to use:** feedback that you are volatile, cold or a poor listener.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = no feedback sought; 3 = feedback gathered, no plan; 5 = plan practiced weekly for three months.
**Example:** a founder told she interrupts picks listening, asks two staff for monthly feedback, and practices summarizing before replying in every one-on-one.

## 21. Three Kinds of Empathy
**Sources:** [H06] (T116).
**What it is:** separating understanding how someone sees it (cognitive), feeling what they feel (emotional) and caring about their good (empathic concern).
**Steps:** 1) Before a hard talk, write how they see the situation. 2) Note what they likely feel. 3) Decide what you want for them, not only for you. 4) Watch for emotional overload; you can care without absorbing.
**When to use:** leading through layoffs, client complaints, caring for a team member in distress.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = only your view; 3 = one or two kinds present; 5 = all three, without being flooded.
**Example:** a clinic owner cutting hours explains the cash position, acknowledges staff fear, and offers flexible shifts and references.

## 22. Shame Resilience
**Sources:** [H09]; self-compassion [H14].
**What it is:** recognizing shame, checking its message, reaching out and speaking about it so it loses power.
**Steps:** 1) Notice the body signs of shame (heat, wanting to hide). 2) Name it: this is shame. 3) Check the message against reality: is this about what I did or who I am? 4) Tell one trusted person. 5) Separate the behavior to fix from your worth.
**When to use:** after a public failure, layoff, lawsuit, bad press or a mistake that hurt someone.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = hides and attacks self; 3 = names it privately; 5 = names it and shares with a trusted person.
**Example:** a founder whose product recall hit the news tells his co-founder how ashamed he feels, then they work on the customer response.

## 23. The Arena and Your Short List
**Sources:** [H09] (T121).
**What it is:** deciding in advance whose feedback counts: people who are also taking risks and who care about you, plus your own inner critic, not the cheap seats.
**Steps:** 1) Write the few names whose opinion you will weigh. 2) Note the seats that are always taken: shame, scarcity, comparison. 3) Before showing up, name your value for doing it. 4) After criticism, check: is this from my list? 5) Take useful points and leave the rest.
**When to use:** fear of posting, launching, public speaking, online reviews.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = all criticism counts equally; 3 = list exists but not used; 5 = feedback filtered through the list each time.
**Example:** a coach launching a podcast writes five names; a stranger's sneer goes unread, a mentor's note on pacing gets acted on.

## 24. Separation of Tasks
**Sources:** [H10]; boundaries [H15]; acceptance [H07].
**What it is:** asking whose task this is, meaning who ultimately bears the result of the choice, then doing your own task and not carrying others'.
**Steps:** 1) Name the situation. 2) Split it: what is my task, what is theirs. 3) Do your task fully. 4) Let them have theirs, including their feelings about you. 5) Offer help when asked, without taking over.
**When to use:** people-pleasing, fear of being disliked, worrying about how others will react, taking over staff work.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = carrying others' tasks and feelings; 3 = can see the split but still intrudes; 5 = acts on own task and lets go.
**Example:** an owner dreads raising prices because clients may be upset. Her task is a fair price and a clear notice; how they feel is theirs.

## 25. Encouragement over Praise
**Sources:** [H10]; caring leadership [H06].
**What it is:** treating staff and family as equals by thanking contribution and encouraging effort, rather than ranking them with praise and rebuke.
**Steps:** 1) Notice when you judge from above (good job, that was bad). 2) Replace with thanks for a specific contribution. 3) Ask what they think. 4) Let them own their task.
**When to use:** building a team that does not depend on your approval. Detailed management practice hands off to Relationships and Leadership.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = mostly praise and rebuke; 3 = mixed; 5 = mostly specific thanks and questions.
**Example:** instead of praising a manager's report, an owner thanks her for flagging the stock issue early and asks what she would do next.

## 26. Stimulation Budget and Restorative Niches
**Sources:** [H11]; free trait theory as reported in [H11].
**What it is:** planning high-stimulation work (pitching, events, sales days) with recovery time and places to restore, especially for introverts.
**Steps:** 1) List the week's high-stimulation events. 2) Book a recovery block after each. 3) Find a restorative niche (a quiet room, a walk, a closed door). 4) Treat events as a few one-on-one conversations (T122). 5) Count one real connection as success.
**When to use:** introverted owners drained by networking, sales or managing.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = back-to-back stimulation; 3 = some recovery; 5 = recovery planned after every big event.
**Example:** an introverted founder at a trade show books a 30-minute walk after each half-day and aims for three good conversations.

## 27. Shy or Introvert Check
**Sources:** [H11] (T122); story rewrite [H15].
**What it is:** separating a preference for quiet (introversion) from fear of judgment (shyness or social anxiety), because they need different responses.
**Steps:** 1) After social time, do you feel depleted or energized? 2) Before it, is there fear of being judged? 3) If depleted but not afraid, plan recovery (framework 26). 4) If afraid, use small exposure steps and story testing (framework 35). 5) If fear blocks daily life, see a professional.
**When to use:** an owner who says they are just an introvert to explain avoiding all sales calls.
**Score (guidance):** for fear of judgment: 1 = none; 3 = moderate, manageable; 5 = blocks key tasks, consider professional help.
**Example:** a designer realizes she loves one-on-one client calls but fears group critique, so she practices presenting to two trusted peers first.

## 28. Connection Audit
**Sources:** [H12]; mutual care [H08]; belonging [H09].
**What it is:** checking which basic needs are being met: belonging, meaning, being seen and valued, and a future that makes sense (T124).
**Steps:** 1) Rate each need 1 to 5. 2) For the lowest, list one person or group who could help. 3) Book one recurring contact (weekly dinner, peer group, sport, volunteering). 4) Review in a month.
**When to use:** founder loneliness, flatness despite success, isolation after a move or exit.
**Score (guidance):** use the four ratings; any need at 1 or 2 is the starting point.
**Example:** a solo e-commerce founder rates belonging at 1, joins a local founders breakfast and a weekly running club.

## 29. Intrinsic versus Junk Values Check
**Sources:** [H12]; values [H07].
**What it is:** sorting goals by whether they serve intrinsic needs (connection, growth, contribution) or extrinsic markers (money for status, image, display).
**Steps:** 1) List current big goals. 2) Mark each intrinsic or extrinsic. 3) Ask what the extrinsic ones are really for. 4) Rebalance one goal toward an intrinsic aim.
**When to use:** hitting revenue targets and feeling empty, comparing yourself to other founders online.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = almost all goals extrinsic; 3 = mixed; 5 = goals tied to clear intrinsic aims.
**Example:** a founder chasing a bigger office realizes he wants respect from peers; he puts the money into a mentoring program instead.

## 30. Phone Boundaries
**Sources:** [H13], [H04]; adult use is a synthesis of [H04, H13].
**What it is:** applying the collective-norms idea to your own and your family's devices: delay, limit and make phone-free spaces together.
**Steps:** 1) Pick phone-free zones (bedroom, meals). 2) Set hours. 3) Remove the most compulsive apps. 4) For kids, consider the book's norms (no smartphone before high school, no social media before 16, phone-free school, more free play) and agree them with other parents. 5) Review in a month.
**When to use:** doomscrolling, kids' screen fights, a team culture of constant messaging.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = no limits; 3 = rules that break often; 5 = agreed rules kept most days.
**Example:** a parent-founder charges all phones in the kitchen overnight and agrees a no-social-media-until-16 pact with three other families.

## 31. Body First, Then Words
**Sources:** [H02]; stress cycle [H08].
**What it is:** when you are flooded or triggered, calming the body before trying to reason or problem-solve.
**Steps:** 1) Notice you are flooded (heart racing, cannot think straight). 2) Use rhythm or breath: slow exhale, walking, humming, feet on the floor. 3) Look around and name where you are. 4) Then talk or decide.
**When to use:** panic-like surges, overreactions, before a hard call.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = tries to think through flooding; 3 = calms sometimes; 5 = body first as a habit.
**Example:** a founder who freezes before board meetings walks the stairs and does slow breaths for five minutes first.

## 32. Then and Now
**Sources:** [H02] (T111).
**What it is:** helping a triggered reaction catch up with the present: that was then, this is now, and today I am safe.
**Steps:** 1) Notice a reaction that feels bigger than the situation. 2) Ask what it reminds you of. 3) Name differences between then and now. 4) Ground in the present (framework 31). 5) If this happens often or involves past trauma, work with a trauma-trained professional rather than alone.
**When to use:** strong reactions to a type of person, tone or situation.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = reaction runs the show; 3 = can name the link after; 5 = can name it in the moment and ground.
**Example:** an owner notices she panics when a client raises his voice, links it to an old boss, and reminds herself she can end the call.

## 33. Acceptance of Feelings (Expansion)
**Sources:** [H07]; self-compassion [H14]; numbing [H09].
**What it is:** making room for an uncomfortable feeling instead of fighting or numbing it, so energy goes into action.
**Steps:** 1) Locate the feeling in the body. 2) Breathe into it. 3) Allow it to be there without liking it. 4) Name what you will do next anyway.
**When to use:** anxiety before a pitch, grief after a loss, dread before hard tasks.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = fights or numbs; 3 = allows briefly; 5 = allows and acts.
**Example:** a founder lets the dread sit while making the call to a customer she let down.

## 34. When to Get Help Ladder
**Sources:** [H15]; limits stated in [H03, H04, H05].
**What it is:** a simple ladder for matching support to severity.
**Steps:** 1) Emergency (thoughts of suicide or self-harm, harming others, abuse, medical emergency, psychosis-like experiences, dangerous withdrawal): emergency number or crisis line now (see Safety in _hot.md). 2) Persistent (low mood or anxiety most days for two weeks or more, trauma symptoms, addiction, insomnia for weeks): see a doctor or licensed therapist. 3) Ordinary stress: self-help from this brain, plus support from people around you. 4) Recheck weekly.
**When to use:** at the start of every conversation in this department, silently.
**Score (guidance):** not scored; it sorts the situation.
**Example:** an owner says he has felt flat and unable to enjoy anything for a month. The advisor names this as a reason to see a doctor or therapist, alongside a sleep and connection plan.

## 35. Story Rewrite and Small Tests
**Sources:** [H15] (T126); thought records [H03].
**What it is:** finding the old story behind a fear (often about not being liked), checking it, and testing a new story in small steps.
**Steps:** 1) What are you afraid people will think? 2) Where did that story come from? 3) Is it true now? 4) Write a new, fair story. 5) Test it in a small step, then a slightly bigger one.
**When to use:** social anxiety at events, fear of selling, old labels from childhood.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = story unexamined; 3 = new story written; 5 = new story tested twice.
**Example:** a founder told as a child he was the dull one tests speaking up in a team meeting, then at a peer group.

## 36. Presenting Problem Check
**Sources:** [H15]; substitution [H01].
**What it is:** asking whether the problem you came with is the real one.
**Steps:** 1) State the problem. 2) Ask: if this were solved, what would still bother me? 3) Look for repeating patterns across years. 4) Address the pattern, with professional help if it is deep.
**When to use:** the same crisis repeating with different staff, partners or businesses.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = only the surface; 3 = pattern named; 5 = pattern worked on.
**Example:** a founder who keeps falling out with co-founders sees he avoids disagreement until it explodes, and starts therapy.

## 37. Mutual Care Circle
**Sources:** [H08] (T118); connection [H12].
**What it is:** building a small group of people who take care of each other, since burnout recovery is not a solo project.
**Steps:** 1) Name two or three people. 2) Agree a regular check-in. 3) Ask each other one honest question. 4) Share practical help (cover, meals, rest).
**When to use:** founders who say they have to do it all alone.
**Score (guidance):** 1 = nobody; 3 = one person, irregular; 5 = a group with a regular rhythm.
**Example:** three local owners start a fortnightly breakfast and cover each other's shops for one long weekend each per year.
