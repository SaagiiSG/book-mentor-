# Frameworks: Marketing department

Built from the 25 Marketing books (M01 to M19, M21, M24, M26 to M29), most useful first. Scores are guidance for judgment, not measurements from the books.

Quote policy: verbatim quotes appear ONLY from the author talks and public-domain texts listed in talks.md, always with a T id. Text of copyrighted books is never quoted. Everything else is paraphrase cited by book id.

## Positioning Components (Obviously Awesome)
**Source** [M18], with roots in [M05, M06]
**What it is.** A way to define the context that makes your product obviously valuable to the right buyer.
**Parts.** Competitive alternatives; unique attributes; value those attributes create (with proof); best-fit customer characteristics; market category; optional relevant trend. Positioning comes before messaging and taglines, not after: "positioning is an input." (T035)
**When to use.** Prospects misunderstand you, compare you to the wrong rivals, or push on price.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = you cannot name what buyers would use instead. 3 = attributes and value are clear, but the category is fuzzy. 5 = a best-fit customer can say in one sentence what you are, why you beat their alternative, and the proof.
**Example.** A bookkeeping firm learns its real alternative is "the owner's spouse doing it in a spreadsheet", so it positions as "month-end done for you in 3 days" instead of competing with large accounting firms.

## The Core Four
**Source** [M01]
**What it is.** The four ways anyone can make others aware of an offer.
**Parts.** Warm outreach, posting free content, cold outreach, paid ads. Plus lead getters: referrals, employees, agencies, affiliates. "only two people can let strangers know about the stuff you sell. You and other people." (T025)
**When to use.** Not enough leads, or the owner is relying on a single source.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = one source, done irregularly. 3 = two sources run weekly with tracked results. 5 = at least one source run daily at fixed volume, with cost per engaged lead known.
**Example.** A physio clinic that only runs ads adds warm outreach to past patients and a referral ask at discharge, then measures leads per channel.

## Five Levels of Awareness
**Source** [M12]
**What it is.** A rule for choosing what your copy opens with based on what the reader already knows.
**Parts.** Most aware (lead with the offer), product aware (lead with proof and why yours), solution aware (lead with your mechanism), problem aware (lead with the problem), unaware (lead with a story or identity).
**When to use.** Writing any ad, landing page, email, or sales page.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = copy opens with the product for a cold audience. 3 = opening matches the audience on most pages. 5 = each channel and stage has an opening matched to its awareness level.
**Example.** Retargeting ads to past visitors lead with the discount; cold social ads lead with the problem ("still sore after every run?").

## Stages of Market Sophistication
**Source** [M12]
**What it is.** How claims must change as buyers hear more of them.
**Parts.** Stage 1 make a simple claim; stage 2 enlarge it; stage 3 introduce a new mechanism; stage 4 elaborate the mechanism; stage 5 shift to identification with the buyer.
**When to use.** Your claims sound like everyone else's in a crowded market.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = repeating the same claim as rivals. 3 = adding a mechanism. 5 = message matches the market's stage and is hard to copy.
**Example.** In a market full of "lose weight fast" claims, a coach leads with a named method and why it works, rather than a bigger promise. Hand off to Sales [S07] if the offer itself needs rebuilding.

## STEPPS
**Source** [M03]
**What it is.** Six drivers that make things shared.
**Parts.** Social currency, Triggers, Emotion (high arousal; some emotions lower sharing), Public, Practical value, Stories. A frequent trigger can beat an exciting product.
**When to use.** Designing a referral push, launch, or word-of-mouth campaign.
**Score it (guidance).** Count drivers clearly present. 1 = none or one. 3 = three. 5 = five or six, with a trigger tied to daily life.
**Example.** A café prints a "secret menu" card for regulars (social currency, public) and ties a drink to Monday mornings (trigger).

## SUCCESs
**Source** [M04]
**What it is.** Six traits of messages people remember and act on.
**Parts.** Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Stories. Beware the curse of knowledge.
**When to use.** A pitch, tagline, or explainer that is not landing.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 point per trait clearly met, capped at 5. Heath's rule of thumb: four traits beat two; all six are not required (T028). For unexpected: "all you have to do is to break a pattern" (T028)
**Example.** An IT firm swaps "robust cybersecurity solutions" for "we stopped a fake invoice email that would have cost a client a month of payroll".

## SB7 Framework
**Source** [M02]
**What it is.** A story structure for brand messaging with the customer as hero.
**Parts.** Character with a want; problem (external, internal, philosophical); guide with empathy and competence; plan; call to action (direct and transitional); avoid failure; success. "If you confuse, you will lose." (T026)
**When to use.** Website, pitch deck, or one-liner is unclear or self-focused.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = homepage is about the company. 3 = problem and CTA clear. 5 = a stranger can say what you offer, how it helps, and how to buy within seconds.
**Example.** A roofer's homepage headline changes from "Family owned since 1985" to "A dry roof before winter, with a fixed quote in 24 hours", with a "Book inspection" button.

## Channel Discovery Tests
**Source** [M24], with [M01]
**What it is.** Find your best channel with cheap tests to discover first, then tests to optimize the winner. Channels have short shelf lives, so revisit often (T038).
**Steps.** List candidate channels across the Core Four [M01]; pick two or three that reach your best buyer; run each small, with a fixed end and cost per customer tracked; focus on the winner; apply More, Better, New before adding the next.
**When to use.** Choosing channels or a channel stops growing.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = channels chosen by habit. 3 = two or three tested with numbers. 5 = one channel chosen on cost per customer and capacity to scale.
**Example.** A B2B software firm tests LinkedIn ads, a trade show, and a partnership with an accountant network for a month each, then focuses on the partnership.

## Value Ladder
**Source** [M10]
**What it is.** A sequence of offers from free to premium.
**Parts.** Free bait, low price entry, core offer, high ticket or continuity; each step leads to the next.
**When to use.** Only one product, or a big gap between "free" and "expensive".
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = single offer. 3 = an entry offer exists. 5 = every rung has a clear next step and a measured take rate.
**Example.** A dog trainer adds a free video, a low price group class, then private sessions and a monthly plan. Hand off pricing details to Sales [S07].

## Hook, Story, Offer
**Source** [M10, M11]
**What it is.** A three-part structure for any piece of marketing.
**Parts.** Hook grabs the right person; story builds belief; offer asks for action.
**When to use.** Ads, videos, emails, webinars.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = only an offer. 3 = all three exist. 5 = each is tested separately and the weakest gets fixed first.
**Example.** A landscaper's ad opens with a before photo (hook), tells the homeowner's story (story), then offers a free design call (offer).

## Lead Magnet
**Source** [M01], with [M10, M19, M02]
**What it is.** A free, useful thing that attracts the right prospect and leads toward your paid offer.
**Types.** Reveal a problem (an audit or quiz), a sample or trial, or one step of a multistep process.
**When to use.** Cold traffic will not buy on first contact.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = generic newsletter signup. 3 = specific, useful to the right buyer. 5 = solves a narrow problem fast and reveals the next problem your offer solves; tracked from magnet to sale.
**Example.** An accountant offers a "five minute tax deduction check" that shows owners what they missed, leading to a paid review.

## More, Better, New
**Source** [M01]
**What it is.** The order for improving lead flow.
**Steps.** Do more of what works; then improve it; only then add something new.
**When to use.** The owner wants a new channel while the current one is under-run.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = adding channels before maxing any. 5 = current channel is at its volume limit before something new is added.
**Example.** A clinic running 10 cold emails a week raises it to 50 before trying TikTok.

## Rule of 100
**Source** [M01]
**What it is.** A fixed daily volume of lead-getting actions.
**When to use.** Lead flow is irregular because outreach depends on mood.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = no daily target. 5 = a set daily number tracked for 90 days or more.
**Example.** A solo designer commits to 30 warm messages a day (scaled from 100 to fit a one-person business).

## The Nine Square Plan
**Source** [M19]
**What it is.** A one-page marketing plan across the customer journey.
**Parts.** Before: target market, message, media. During: lead capture, lead nurture, sales conversion. After: experience, lifetime value, referrals.
**When to use.** A small firm needs one plan instead of random tactics.
**Score it (guidance).** One point per phase fully filled with a named action and owner, then scale to 1 to 5.
**Example.** A gym realizes it has ads (media) but no follow-up for leads who do not join on day one, and adds an email and text sequence.

## Smallest Viable Market
**Source** [M07], with [M19]
**What it is.** Pick the smallest audience you can serve brilliantly and that can sustain you. "Find the people who care, the smallest viable group you can live with, and figure out how to give them work that matters." (T030)
**When to use.** The business tries to appeal to everyone.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = "anyone who needs X". 3 = a defined group. 5 = a group you can name, reach, and that talks to each other.
**Example.** A web designer focuses on independent veterinary clinics and gets referred within that network.
**Note.** See the tensions with [M15] and [M29] in principles.md.

## Mental and Physical Availability
**Source** [M15]
**What it is.** Growth comes from being easy to think of and easy to buy.
**Parts.** Mental: reach all category buyers, build memory with distinctive assets, link to buying situations. Physical: be present and easy to buy where buyers shop. Small brands can apply the same principle in their own channels (T033).
**When to use.** Consumer or broad-market businesses, local retail, food, trades.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = inconsistent branding, hard to find. 3 = consistent assets and decent reach. 5 = recognized at a glance and present at every common point of purchase.
**Example.** A regional bakery uses the same color and logo on vans, bags, and ads, and gets onto two delivery apps.

## Distinctive Brand Assets
**Source** [M15], with [M13]
**What it is.** Consistent cues (colors, logo, character, sound, phrasing) that make the brand recognizable.
**When to use.** Brand looks different in every ad, or the owner wants a rebrand.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = changes every campaign. 5 = the same assets used for years and recognized without the name.
**Example.** A plumber keeps the same yellow van and jingle rather than "refreshing" each year.

## Positioning in the Mind (Product Ladder)
**Source** [M05, M06]
**What it is.** Buyers rank a few brands per category in memory; win by being first or creating a category where you are first.
**When to use.** You are number three or lower in a category.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = me-too in a category with a strong leader. 3 = a clear contrast with the leader. 5 = you own a word or subcategory.
**Example.** A small law firm stops being "full service" and becomes "the startup equity lawyer" in its city.

## Law of Focus (Own a Word)
**Source** [M06], with [M05]
**What it is.** Own one word or idea in the customer's mind.
**When to use.** Messaging lists many benefits.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = a list of five benefits. 5 = one word customers repeat back.
**Example.** A courier owns "same hour" and builds every ad around it.

## Law of Sacrifice
**Source** [M06], with [M05, M07]
**What it is.** To stand for something you must give up products, audiences, or constant change.
**When to use.** The owner wants to add a product line or market.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = adding without removing. 5 = each addition paired with something dropped.
**Example.** A catering firm drops weddings to own corporate lunches.

## Hook Model
**Source** [M09]
**What it is.** A loop that builds habitual use.
**Parts.** Trigger (external then internal), Action (easy), Variable reward (tribe, hunt, self), Investment. "the action phase is defined as the simplest Behavior done in anticipation of a reward" (T032)
**When to use.** Apps, subscriptions, communities, any repeat-use product.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = no reason to return. 3 = reminders and some reward. 5 = users return unprompted and invest data or content. Run the Manipulation Matrix check.
**Example.** A meal-plan app sends a Sunday prompt, varies recipes, and saves preferences that improve next week's plan.

## Must-Have Test and North Star Metric
**Source** [M24]
**What it is.** Confirm product fit before growth spend, then align on one value metric.
**Steps.** Survey active users on how disappointed they would be without you; Ellis uses about 40 percent "very disappointed" as the bar. Then pick a metric that tracks value delivered.
**When to use.** Before scaling paid acquisition.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = no survey, vanity metric. 3 = survey done, below bar. 5 = above bar with a clear North Star tracked weekly.
**Example.** A SaaS firm finds 25 percent "very disappointed", pauses ads, and fixes onboarding first. Hand off to Growth [G05] for product fit work.

## Growth Hacking Process and ICE
**Source** [M24]
**What it is.** A fast experiment cycle.
**Steps.** Analyze data; ideate; prioritize with ICE (impact, confidence, ease, each 1 to 10); test; learn; repeat. Set a weekly test number. "for us ice is about impact confidence and ease" (T038)
**When to use.** Enough traffic to read results.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = no tests. 3 = monthly tests. 5 = weekly tests with a log of results.
**Example.** An online shop tests free shipping threshold vs. a bundle; the bundle wins on margin.
**Note.** Assumes volume. Small firms should run fewer, bolder tests.

## Test with Keyed Responses
**Source** [M14], with [M13, M24]
**What it is.** Track every ad to the sales it produced, and let results decide.
**When to use.** Any spending on marketing.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = no tracking. 3 = lead source captured. 5 = cost per customer known by ad and channel.
**Example.** A dentist uses a separate phone number for each ad and drops the one that brings calls but no bookings.

## The Big 5
**Source** [M17]
**What it is.** The five topics buyers search most and companies avoid.
**Parts.** Cost and price, problems, comparisons, reviews, best-of lists.
**When to use.** Planning content for a business with a researched purchase.
**Score it (guidance).** One point per topic covered honestly on your site.
**Example.** A pool installer publishes "what a fiberglass pool really costs" and gets its best leads from it.

## Assignment Selling
**Source** [M17]
**What it is.** Send specific content to prospects before sales conversations.
**When to use.** Sales calls repeat the same education.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = no pre-call content. 5 = a standard pack sent and tracked before every call.
**Example.** A kitchen remodeler sends a pricing article and a project video before the first visit. Hand off call technique to Sales.

## Show Your Work (Share the Process)
**Source** [M27], with [M17]
**What it is.** Build an audience by sharing your process, what you are learning, and work you admire, a little every day, instead of only promoting finished products.
**Parts.** Process not product; something small every day; share influences; teach what you know; do not be human spam; stick around.
**When to use.** Owner has expertise and patience but no audience and dislikes selling.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = posts only finished work or promotions, irregularly. 3 = regular process posts. 5 = daily small shares, readers reply and send work, and some become leads.
**Example.** A woodworker posts a short clip of each day's bench work and the mistakes, builds an email list, then sells plans and classes.

## Personal Brand Content Engine
**Source** [M28], with [M16, M27]
**What it is.** The founder becomes the face of the business and posts a high volume of content on the platforms where buyers spend attention.
**Steps.** Pick the platforms that fit your strengths and buyers; post daily; document what you do rather than polish; give value before asks; expect years, not weeks.
**When to use.** Founder-led firms where buyers trust people more than companies.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = no founder presence. 3 = weekly posts on one platform. 5 = daily output for 12 months or more, with leads tracked to it.
**Example.** A roofing owner films a one-minute site update each day and gets quote requests from neighbors who follow him.
**Note.** A personal brand is hard to hand over or sell with the business.

## Jab, Jab, Jab, Right Hook
**Source** [M16], with [M01, M28]
**What it is.** Give value repeatedly before asking.
**When to use.** Social feeds that are only promotions.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = every post is a sales ask. 5 = mostly value with clear, periodic asks that convert.
**Example.** A hairdresser posts styling tips and client stories, then one booking offer per week.

## Epiphany Bridge and False Beliefs
**Source** [M11]
**What it is.** Use your discovery story to break the beliefs stopping a purchase.
**Parts.** Vehicle belief (does the method work), internal (can I do it), external (will something outside stop me).
**When to use.** Selling a method, course, or expertise.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = features only. 5 = each of the three beliefs addressed by a story.
**Example.** A fitness coach tells how she failed with diets before discovering strength training, then shows a client with a busy schedule succeeding.

## Purple Cow
**Source** [M08], with [M03, M07]
**What it is.** Build remarkability into the product and target the people who spread ideas. "the only option is to be the only one" (T031)
**When to use.** Product is fine but invisible.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = no one would mention it. 5 = customers describe it to friends unprompted.
**Example.** A dentist adds noise-cancelling headphones and a "no wait or it's free" promise, which patients talk about.

## Psycho-logic and Reframing
**Source** [M21]
**What it is.** Change perception when changing reality is costly. Often the lever is uncertainty, not time: "what bothers us about waiting for a taxi isn't actually the duration it's the degree of uncertainty" (T037)
**When to use.** A rational fix is expensive, or customers act in "irrational" ways.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = only logical fixes considered. 5 = cheap perception changes tested alongside.
**Example.** A repair shop sends progress photos, which cuts complaints about time more than adding staff would.

## Costly Signalling
**Source** [M21], with [M13]
**What it is.** Visible effort or expense signals confidence and quality.
**When to use.** Trust is low, or the brand looks cheap.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = no signal. 5 = signals buyers notice and cannot fake easily.
**Example.** A consultant sends a handwritten summary after first meetings.

## The Golden Circle
**Source** [M26]
**What it is.** Communicate from the inside out: why you exist, then how you do it, then what you sell.
**Parts.** Why (a positive cause, found rather than invented); how (the way you do it, including your differentiating value proposition); what (products and services) (T039).
**When to use.** The brand is interchangeable, or staff cannot say why the business exists.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = messaging is only what and price. 3 = a stated why that is generic. 5 = a specific why that staff repeat and that decisions are checked against.
**Example.** A bike shop leads with "we want more kids riding to school", then its fitting service, then its bikes, and drops a cheap-import line that does not fit.
**Note.** Sharp's data questions how much buyers care about brand purpose [M15]. Pair a why with a specific benefit.

## Two-Axis Positioning Map
**Source** [M07], with [M18]
**What it is.** Plot your market on two attributes your audience cares about and find an open corner.
**When to use.** Crowded market, unclear difference.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = axes are things buyers do not care about. 5 = an open corner that a real group wants.
**Example.** A gym plots "guided vs. self-directed" against "social vs. private" and finds no guided, private option for older adults.

## Headline First
**Source** [M13, M14, M12]
**What it is.** The headline carries the benefit and selects the reader.
**When to use.** Every ad and page.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = clever, no benefit. 5 = specific benefit to a named reader, tested against variants.
**Example.** "Payroll errors fixed in one day for clinics under 50 staff" beats "Payroll made easy".

## Specificity
**Source** [M14], with [M04, M13]
**What it is.** Exact claims are believed; vague ones are ignored.
**When to use.** Copy with "best", "quality", "leading".
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = superlatives only. 5 = numbers, names, and proof in each claim.
**Example.** "Fitted in 4 hours, cleaned up before you're home" beats "fast, professional service".

## Hook Point Method
**Source** [M29], with [M10]
**What it is.** Engineer the first one to three seconds of content so the right strangers stop scrolling.
**Steps.** Study hooks that already have huge reach in and outside your industry; break down their tone, pacing, and questions; brainstorm your own; write at least 20, pick one, one hook per piece; test on retention in the first three seconds (T042). Pull with curiosity or a promise of value.
**When to use.** Posts or video ads get few views, or openings are weak.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = no deliberate hook. 3 = hooks written, not tested. 5 = several hooks tested per month on early retention, winners reused.
**Example.** A leather craftsman moves from leather-only videos to judging whether famous brands are worth the price, keeping his expertise under a broad theme (T042).

## Brand Image and the Big Idea
**Source** [M13], with [M15]
**What it is.** One durable campaign idea, and consistent brand personality.
**When to use.** Campaigns change every quarter.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = new idea every campaign. 5 = one idea run for years with variations.
**Example.** A furniture shop keeps "built to be handed down" as its idea for every ad.

## Mass Desire
**Source** [M12]
**What it is.** You cannot create desire; find an existing one and channel it.
**When to use.** Trying to convince people to want something new.
**Score it (guidance).** 1 = copy argues people should want it. 5 = copy taps a want people already search for.
**Example.** A meal kit sells "dinner decided in 30 seconds", tapping a known want, not "healthy eating".
