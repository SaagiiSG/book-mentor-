# Principles: Marketing department

Built from the Marketing books (M01 to M25). Decision rules first, then where the books disagree. Confidence notes count how many of our books support a rule.

## Decision rules

1. Decide who it is for before you decide what to say or where to say it. [M07, M18, M19, M25] Confidence: high, four books, and implied by most others.
2. Define your competition as what the buyer would do without you, which is often doing nothing or doing it themselves. [M18] Confidence: medium, one book states it directly; consistent with [M12].
3. You cannot create desire; find a want that already exists and point it at your offer. [M12, M22] Confidence: medium, two books.
4. Match the opening of your message to how aware the reader already is. [M12], echoed in [M02, M11] Confidence: medium.
5. The customer is the hero of the story; the business is the guide. [M02], consistent with [M07, M17] Confidence: medium.
6. Specific claims beat vague ones every time. [M14, M04, M22, M23, M13] Confidence: high, five books.
7. The headline does most of the selling, so write and test it first. [M13, M14, M12] Confidence: high, three books.
8. Measure each campaign to the sales it produced and stop what does not pay. [M14, M13, M24, M20, M01] Confidence: high, five books.
9. Do more of the channel that works before you add a new one. [M01, M20] Confidence: medium, two books.
10. Test channels cheaply and in parallel, then focus on one. [M20, M24] Confidence: medium, two books.
11. Fix product fit before you spend to grow; marketing amplifies a weak product's weakness. [M24, M08] Confidence: medium; also matches Growth [G05].
12. Remarkability is built into the product, not bolted on with ads. [M08, M03] Confidence: medium.
13. Own one idea or word in the buyer's mind rather than a list of benefits. [M05, M06, M04] Confidence: high, three books.
14. If you cannot be first in a category, define a narrower one you can lead. [M05, M06, M18] Confidence: high, three books.
15. Focus requires giving something up: a product, an audience, or a feature. [M06, M07, M05] Confidence: high, three books.
16. Be consistent with brand assets for years; boredom inside the company is not fatigue in the market. [M15, M13] Confidence: medium, two books.
17. Make yourself easy to think of and easy to buy wherever buyers are. [M15] Confidence: medium, one book with large data; partly echoed by [M03] on triggers.
18. Give value repeatedly before asking for the sale. [M16, M01, M17, M25] Confidence: high, four books.
19. Answer the questions buyers actually ask, including price and comparisons, in public. [M17], consistent with [M23] Confidence: medium.
20. Most prospects are not ready to buy today, so capture them and follow up. [M19, M10, M01] Confidence: high, three books.
21. Use a free offer that solves a narrow problem and reveals the next one your paid offer solves. [M01, M10] Confidence: medium, two books.
22. Build a path of offers so each customer has a clear next step. [M10, M19] Confidence: medium, two books. Hand off offer design to Sales [S07].
23. Word of mouth comes from social currency, triggers, emotion, visibility, usefulness, and stories, and you can design for each. [M03] Confidence: medium, one book; consistent with [M08].
24. Ask for referrals as a system, not a hope. [M01, M19] Confidence: medium, two books.
25. Stories carry ideas further than facts alone. [M04, M02, M11, M03] Confidence: high, four books.
26. Translate abstract claims into concrete images; beware the curse of knowledge. [M04, M23] Confidence: medium, two books.
27. Perception changes are often cheaper than reality changes, so test them. [M21] Confidence: low to medium, one book, largely anecdotal.
28. Do not cut something just because its value is unmeasured. [M21] Confidence: low to medium, one book.
29. Sell a new opportunity rather than an improvement when buyers have tried and failed before. [M11, M12] Confidence: medium; [M12] frames it as a new mechanism in sophisticated markets.
30. Respect the reader's intelligence; hype erodes trust. [M13, M23, M17] Confidence: high, three books.
31. Publish natively for each platform; the same asset pasted everywhere underperforms. [M16] Confidence: low to medium, one book, platform specifics dated.
32. Prefer an owned audience (email, subscribers) over rented followers. [M25, M10] Confidence: medium, two books.
33. Pick one content platform and be consistent there before spreading. [M25, M20] Confidence: medium. Synthesis of [M25, M20].
34. Habit products need an easy action and a reason to come back; most other businesses should not chase habit. [M09] Confidence: medium, one book; the second half is our synthesis.
35. Draft fast, edit hard, and write for one reader. [M23] Confidence: medium, one book; consistent with [M04].
36. Run a fixed daily volume of lead actions instead of working by mood. [M01] Confidence: low to medium, one book.

## Where the books disagree

**1. Narrow niche vs. broad reach: Godin and Ries/Trout versus Sharp.** [M07, M05, M06, M19] say pick a small audience and own it. [M15] says growth comes from reaching all category buyers, and that tight targeting starves growth. Niche is right when you are small, the buyer group talks to each other, and the offer is specialized or B2B. Broad reach is right for consumer goods, local services everyone uses (food, trades, retail), and established brands trying to grow share. Many firms should target narrow messaging but avoid excluding buyers from distribution.

**2. Differentiation vs. distinctiveness: Dunford and Ries/Trout versus Sharp.** [M18, M05, M06] say win with a clear difference. [M15] argues buyers rarely perceive brand differences and that being recognizable matters more. Differentiation matters for considered purchases where buyers compare. Distinctiveness matters for frequent, low-involvement purchases.

**3. Loyalty and habit vs. penetration: Eyal versus Sharp.** [M09] treats repeat use as the engine. [M15] says loyalty mostly follows size and that chasing loyal heavy users is a weak growth lever. Habit wins for software and subscriptions where retention is the business model. Penetration wins for products bought occasionally by many people.

**4. Measure everything vs. value the unmeasurable: Hopkins and Ellis versus Sutherland.** [M14, M24] say test and let numbers decide. [M21] warns that over-reliance on metrics kills ideas whose value is hard to measure. Testing is right for response channels with enough volume. Sutherland is right for brand, experience, and small samples where tests cannot detect the effect.

**5. Long-term brand vs. direct response: Ogilvy and Sharp versus Hopkins and Brunson.** [M13, M15] treat each ad as an investment in brand memory. [M14, M10, M01] focus on immediate, trackable sales. Direct response is right when cash is tight and the offer is clear. Brand building is right once there is runway and buyers purchase on recall.

**6. Patience vs. volume in content: Pulizzi versus Hormozi and Vaynerchuk.** [M25] says one platform, one niche, consistency for a long time. [M01, M16] push high volume and many touches. Pulizzi fits owners with deep expertise and a long horizon. Volume fits when you need leads this quarter and have the energy to produce.

**7. Clarity formula vs. being different: Miller versus Godin.** [M02] gives a repeatable clarity template. [M07, M08] warn that playing it safe makes you invisible. Use SB7 when your message is confusing. Use Godin when your message is clear but forgettable.

**8. Logic of advertising vs. psychology of persuasion: Hopkins and Ogilvy versus Whitman and Brunson.** [M14, M13] lean on reasons, facts, and respect for the reader. [M22, M11] lean on emotional drives and belief-shifting stories, which can tip into manipulation. Use emotion to get attention and facts to justify the purchase; drop any tactic you would not explain to the customer.

**9. First mover vs. better positioning: Ries/Trout versus Dunford.** [M05, M06] stress being first in the mind. [M18] shows late entrants can win by reframing the category. Being first matters in a new category with few rivals. Reframing matters when you enter a crowded one.
