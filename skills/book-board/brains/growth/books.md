# Books: Business Growth department

Built from the 25 Business Growth books (G01 to G25), one entry each, in id order, paraphrased in our own words.

## [G01] Good to Great, Jim Collins (2001)

**Core idea:** Collins's team compared companies that jumped from average to strong results with similar companies that did not. The jump came from disciplined people, thought and action compounding over years, not one big launch or a celebrity CEO.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **Level 5 Leadership:** the leaders of the companies that made the jump mixed personal humility with fierce will for the company, rather than charisma.
- **First Who, Then What:** get the right people on the bus (and the wrong ones off) before you pick the direction.
- **Confront the Brutal Facts (the Stockdale Paradox):** stay sure you will win in the end while facing the worst facts of the present honestly.
- **The Hedgehog Concept:** do only what sits where three circles meet: what you can be best at, what drives your economic engine (one key ratio, "profit per X"), and what your people care deeply about.
- **The Flywheel and the Doom Loop:** results come from steady pushes that compound; lurching between new programs resets the wheel.

**Use it when:** the business is decent but stuck, the owner keeps chasing new ideas, or the team needs to agree what the company is best at.

**Watch out:** the method picks winners in hindsight (survivorship bias), and several "great" companies later struggled or failed. Based on large public companies; translate for a 20-person firm.

## [G02] The E-Myth Revisited, Michael E. Gerber (1995)

**Core idea:** Most small businesses are started by skilled technicians who think knowing the work means knowing how to run a business. The fix is to build the business like a franchise prototype that runs on documented systems, so it does not depend on the owner doing the work.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **The Entrepreneurial Seizure:** the moment a technician quits a job to work for themselves, mostly to escape a boss, and ends up with a harder job.
- **The Entrepreneur, the Manager, the Technician:** three personalities inside every owner; the Technician usually wins and the business stalls.
- **Work On your business, not In it:** treat the business as a product you design.
- **The Franchise Prototype:** design the business so ordinary people can deliver a consistent result by following systems.
- **The Business Development Process:** innovation, quantification, orchestration: try a better way, measure it, then make it the standard.
- **The Business Development Program:** a sequence starting from your Primary Aim (what you want your life to be), then Strategic Objective, Organizational Strategy (org chart of positions, not people), and Management, People, Marketing and Systems strategies.

**Use it when:** the owner is the bottleneck, quality depends on who does the job, or the owner wants a life outside the business.

**Watch out:** written as a parable with lots of repetition and few specifics. Full systemization can feel wrong for craft or relationship businesses where the owner's judgment is the product.

## [G03] Traction, Gino Wickman (2007)

**Core idea:** Traction presents the Entrepreneurial Operating System (EOS), a simple toolset for running a small or mid-size company around six components and a weekly and quarterly rhythm.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **The Six Key Components:** Vision, People, Data, Issues, Process, Traction.
- **The Vision/Traction Organizer (V/TO):** a two-page plan covering core values, core focus, 10-year target, marketing strategy, 3-year picture, 1-year plan, quarterly Rocks and the issues list.
- **Accountability Chart and Right People, Right Seats:** structure first, then people; a seat fits when the person "Gets it, Wants it, and has the Capacity to do it" (GWC), and the right person shares the core values (People Analyzer).
- **Rocks:** a few 90-day priorities per person and per company.
- **Scorecard:** a short weekly set of numbers that shows whether the business is on track.
- **Level 10 Meeting and IDS:** a fixed-agenda weekly meeting where issues are worked by Identify, Discuss, Solve.

**Use it when:** the company is roughly 10 to 250 people and has growing pains: missed priorities, unclear roles, meetings that wander.

**Watch out:** it is a branded system with paid implementers; some teams apply it mechanically. Light on strategy and market choice.

## [G04] Scaling Up, Verne Harnish (2014)

**Core idea:** Growing companies must get four decisions right: People, Strategy, Execution and Cash. Harnish updates his earlier Rockefeller Habits with one-page tools for each.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **The Four Decisions:** People, Strategy, Execution, Cash.
- **One-Page Strategic Plan (OPSP):** core values, purpose, BHAG, targets, priorities and metrics on one page.
- **Rockefeller Habits:** a short list of execution habits, centered on a few priorities, good data and a meeting rhythm from the daily huddle up to the annual plan.
- **Function Accountability Chart:** who owns each function and its numbers, separate from the org chart.
- **Cash Conversion Cycle:** how many days cash is tied up from paying for inputs to getting paid by customers.
- **The Power of One:** small changes in a few levers (price, volume, cost of goods, overhead, receivable days, payable days, inventory days) and their effect on cash and profit.

**Use it when:** a firm of roughly 10 to several hundred people is growing and feels chaotic or runs short of cash despite sales.

**Watch out:** dense, with many tools; small firms should pick two or three. Some advice leans toward ambitious growth companies.

## [G05] The Lean Startup, Eric Ries (2011)

**Core idea:** Under high uncertainty, a startup's job is to learn what customers will actually pay for, as fast and cheap as possible. Treat plans as hypotheses and test them with real behavior.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **Build-Measure-Learn:** a loop to run quickly; plan it backward from what you need to learn.
- **Minimum Viable Product (MVP):** the smallest thing that tests a key assumption with real customers.
- **Leap-of-faith assumptions:** the value hypothesis (do they want it) and the growth hypothesis (how will it spread).
- **Validated Learning and Innovation Accounting:** progress is measured by learning, using actionable metrics rather than vanity metrics.
- **Pivot or Persevere:** a regular, deliberate decision to change one part of the strategy or stay the course.
- **Engines of Growth:** sticky (retention), viral (referral), paid (acquisition spending). Five Whys finds root causes.

**Use it when:** launching a new product, service line or market where you do not know what will work.

**Watch out:** less useful for proven businesses where the answer is execution. A poor MVP can damage a brand in a small market where everyone talks.

## [G06] Zero to One, Peter Thiel and Blake Masters (2014)

**Core idea:** Real progress comes from creating something new (zero to one), not copying what works (one to n). Great businesses escape competition by building a kind of monopoly in a market they can dominate.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **Zero to One versus One to n:** invention versus copying.
- **Competition is for losers:** fierce competition destroys profit; aim for a market you can own.
- **Characteristics of a monopoly:** proprietary technology, network effects, economies of scale, and branding.
- **Start small and monopolize:** dominate a small market, then expand.
- **Last mover advantage and secrets:** what matters is who holds the market for the long run, often built on an important truth few people see.
- **The seven questions:** engineering, timing, monopoly, people, distribution, durability, secret.

**Use it when:** choosing what to build or which market to attack, especially to avoid a crowded commodity fight.

**Watch out:** written for venture-backed tech. Most small businesses will not be monopolies, but "dominate a small niche" still applies. Contrarian tone; treat claims as provocations.

## [G07] Built to Last, Jim Collins and Jerry Porras (1994)

**Core idea:** Companies that thrived for many decades were built around a lasting core ideology while constantly pushing for progress. Their founders focused on building the organization (clock building) more than on any single product or idea.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **Clock Building, Not Time Telling:** build a company that works without its founder.
- **The Genius of the AND:** reject false either/or choices, such as profit or purpose, stability or change.
- **Preserve the Core / Stimulate Progress:** keep core values and purpose fixed; change everything else.
- **Core Ideology:** core values plus core purpose.
- **Big Hairy Audacious Goals (BHAGs):** bold, clear long-term goals that energize people.

**Use it when:** defining values and purpose, planning succession, or deciding what must never change during growth.

**Watch out:** same hindsight bias critique as G01; many of the companies studied later underperformed. Long horizon, big-company examples.

## [G08] Blue Ocean Strategy, W. Chan Kim and Renee Mauborgne (2005)

**Core idea:** Instead of fighting rivals in crowded red oceans, create uncontested blue oceans by raising buyer value while lowering cost.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **Red Oceans versus Blue Oceans.**
- **The Strategy Canvas:** chart how you and rivals score on the factors the industry competes on.
- **The Four Actions Framework and the ERRC Grid:** which factors to Eliminate, Reduce, Raise and Create.
- **The Six Paths Framework:** look across alternative industries, strategic groups, buyer groups, complementary offerings, functional versus emotional appeal, and time.
- **Three Tiers of Noncustomers:** people who barely use you, refuse the industry, or were never considered.

**Use it when:** your industry is commoditized, prices are falling, and every competitor looks the same.

**Watch out:** cases are selected in hindsight; successful blue oceans attract copycats quickly. Execution is harder than the canvas suggests.

## [G09] The Innovator's Dilemma, Clayton M. Christensen (1997)

**Core idea:** Well-run leading firms often fail because they do the right things: listen to their best customers and invest in higher-margin improvements. Meanwhile, cheaper, simpler disruptive products start at the low end or in new markets and improve until they take over.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **Sustaining versus Disruptive Technologies:** improvements for current customers versus simpler, cheaper offers that first serve overlooked customers.
- **Resource dependence:** customers and investors shape where a firm spends, which steers it away from small new markets.
- **Performance oversupply:** products improve faster than customers need, opening the door to "good enough" rivals.
- **Separate organization:** give a disruptive effort its own unit with its own cost structure.

**Use it when:** a cheaper competitor is taking your low end, or you are deciding whether to launch a simpler version of your offer.

**Watch out:** the theory has been challenged on case selection and predictive power, and the term "disruption" is overused. Mainly about technology industries.

## [G10] Profit First, Mike Michalowicz (2014)

**Core idea:** Owners spend whatever is in the bank, so profit ends up as leftovers. Flip the formula: take profit first from every deposit, then run the business on what remains.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **Sales minus Profit equals Expenses:** profit is set aside before spending.
- **Parkinson's Law applied to cash:** spending grows to fit the money available.
- **Separate bank accounts:** Income, Profit, Owner's Comp, Tax and Operating Expenses.
- **Target Allocation Percentages:** goal percentages by revenue size; start with small steps from current percentages.
- **The 10/25 rhythm and quarterly distributions:** allocate twice a month; pay out part of the profit account each quarter.

**Use it when:** the business makes sales but never seems to keep money, or the owner underpays themselves.

**Watch out:** it is a behavioral cash system, not accounting or tax advice. Rigid percentages can starve a business that needs investment; consult an accountant.

## [G11] The Hard Thing About Hard Things, Ben Horowitz (2014)

**Core idea:** There is no recipe for the hardest CEO problems: layoffs, firing friends, near-death moments. Horowitz shares how he made such calls.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **The Struggle:** expect stretches when nothing works; keep going and tell the truth.
- **Peacetime CEO versus Wartime CEO:** in peace, expand and empower; in war, focus hard and direct.
- **Take care of the people, the products and the profits, in that order.**
- **Lay people off the right way:** move fast, own it, and treat people with respect.
- **Management Debt:** quick fixes in people decisions (like overpaying to keep someone) that cost more later.

**Use it when:** in crisis, cutting costs, firing or demoting a leader, or deciding how to lead through uncertainty.

**Watch out:** venture-backed Silicon Valley context. Some advice assumes boards, investors and big teams.

## [G12] Measure What Matters, John Doerr (2018)

**Core idea:** Objectives and Key Results (OKRs) align a team on a few ambitious goals and make progress measurable. Doerr shows how Intel and later Google and others used them.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **OKRs:** an objective is what you want to achieve; key results are measurable outcomes showing you got there.
- **Four superpowers:** focus and commit, align and connect, track for accountability, stretch for amazing.
- **Committed versus Aspirational OKRs:** must-hit goals versus stretch goals where partial success is fine.
- **CFRs:** Conversations, Feedback and Recognition, the ongoing coaching that makes OKRs work.

**Use it when:** a team needs to agree what matters this quarter and how to measure it.

**Watch out:** heavy for a team of five. Linking OKRs to pay invites sandbagging. Examples are mostly large tech companies.

## [G13] Crossing the Chasm, Geoffrey A. Moore (1991)

**Core idea:** New technology products are adopted by groups in sequence. There is a deep gap between early adopters, who buy vision, and the early majority, who buy proven solutions, and many products die there.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **Technology Adoption Life Cycle:** innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, laggards.
- **The Chasm:** pragmatists want references from people like them, which early adopters cannot provide.
- **The D-Day strategy (beachhead):** focus everything on one narrow segment and dominate it.
- **The Whole Product:** everything the customer needs to get the result, not just the core product.
- **Bowling Pin strategy:** win one segment, then use it to knock over adjacent ones.

**Use it when:** you have enthusiastic early customers but growth stalls with mainstream buyers.

**Watch out:** written for B2B technology. Less relevant to simple consumer products or local services.

## [G14] Good Strategy Bad Strategy, Richard Rumelt (2011)

**Core idea:** Most "strategy" is fluff or wish lists. Real strategy diagnoses the key challenge and focuses effort on a coherent response.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **The Kernel:** diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent actions.
- **Hallmarks of Bad Strategy:** fluff, failure to face the challenge, mistaking goals for strategy, and bad strategic objectives.
- **Sources of power:** leverage, proximate objectives, chain-link systems, design, focus, using advantage, and riding dynamics.

**Use it when:** the plan is a list of targets with no clear story of how to beat the obstacle.

**Watch out:** more a way of thinking than a step-by-step method. Examples are large firms and military history.

## [G15] The Personal MBA, Josh Kaufman (2010)

**Core idea:** A broad primer on the core business ideas, built on the view that every business does five things: create value, market it, sell it, deliver it, and manage the money.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **The Five Parts of Every Business:** value creation, marketing, sales, value delivery, finance.
- **Iron Law of the Market:** no business beats a market that does not want what it sells.
- **Profit margin, value capture and sufficiency:** know your numbers and what "enough" means.

**Use it when:** diagnosing which of the five parts is weak, or onboarding an owner who lacks business training.

**Watch out:** broad, not deep; each idea needs other sources to apply well.

## [G16] Built to Sell, John Warrillow (2010)

**Core idea:** A business that depends on its owner is hard to sell. Told as a story of an agency owner, it shows how to turn a custom service firm into a standardized, sellable company.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **Productize a service:** pick one thing you do well and standardize it.
- **Stop custom work:** say no to one-off projects that need you.
- **Recurring revenue and up-front payment** raise value and fund growth.
- **Build a sales team** that sells the standard offer without the owner (the book suggests more than one rep).
- **Management team with long-term incentives,** and step back to prove the business runs without you.

**Use it when:** planning an exit in a few years or wanting a business that runs without the owner.

**Watch out:** fable format; built around service firms. Valuation multiples vary widely by industry and buyer.

## [G17] Clockwork, Mike Michalowicz (2018)

**Core idea:** A business should run like a clock without the owner doing everything. Free the owner by identifying the most important role, protecting it, and shifting work into systems and other people.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **The 4 Ds:** Doing, Deciding, Delegating, Designing; owners must move time toward Designing (the book suggests target ratios, detail uncertain).
- **Queen Bee Role (QBR):** the one function that most drives the company's success; protect it.
- **Capture systems:** record how work is done now instead of writing manuals from scratch.
- **Balance the team:** match people to work and remove bottlenecks.
- **Commit to the clients you serve best** and to your big promise.
- **The Four-Week Vacation:** a forcing test that the business can run without you.

**Use it when:** the owner is overloaded and the business stalls whenever they step away.

**Watch out:** written for owner-run small firms. The vacation goal can be unrealistic early on.

## [G18] Rework, Jason Fried and David Heinemeier Hansson (2010)

**Core idea:** A short, contrarian guide to running a small, profitable, calm company. Keep things simple, small and focused, and ignore much of conventional startup wisdom.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **Planning is guessing:** decide based on now, not long-range plans.
- **Underdo the competition:** do less, but better.
- **Embrace constraints:** limited time and money force good decisions.
- **Hire when it hurts:** do the job yourself first, hire only when necessary.
- **Meetings and interruptions are toxic** to productive work.

**Use it when:** the owner wants a profitable, lean business without outside money or rapid headcount growth.

**Watch out:** anecdotal, from a small software company. Some lines are slogans that do not suit every industry.

## [G19] High Output Management, Andrew S. Grove (1983)

**Core idea:** A manager's output is the output of the team they run and the teams they influence. Grove applies production thinking to management: find the limiting step, measure with good indicators, and spend time where it gives the most leverage.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **Managerial output and leverage:** pick activities that raise many people's output.
- **Production principles:** find the limiting step, use the breakfast-factory analogy, catch problems at the lowest-cost stage.
- **Indicators:** leading indicators, and paired indicators that guard against gaming one number.
- **One-on-ones:** regular meetings driven by the report, for teaching and information.
- **Task-relevant maturity:** adjust how closely you manage based on someone's experience with that task.
- **Decision-making:** open discussion, a clear decision, then full support.

**Use it when:** building a management layer, setting up meetings, or measuring a team.

**Watch out:** 1980s Intel; some parts (dual reporting, review formats) suit large organizations.

## [G20] Buy Then Build, Walker Deibel (2018)

**Core idea:** Instead of starting from zero, buy an existing profitable small business and grow it. You skip startup risk and start with customers, cash flow and staff.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **Acquisition entrepreneurship:** buy a business as an alternative to a startup.
- **Startup risk versus acquisition risk:** buying removes product and market risk but adds deal and integration risk.
- **Search:** define criteria, use brokers and direct outreach.
- **Seller's Discretionary Earnings (SDE)** and valuing small firms.
- **Financing:** bank loans (in the US, SBA lending), seller financing, and investors.

**Use it when:** an owner or would-be owner weighs starting versus buying, or wants to add a business.

**Watch out:** US-focused on financing. Leverage magnifies losses. You may buy a job, not a business.

## [G21] Blitzscaling, Reid Hoffman and Chris Yeh (2018)

**Core idea:** In winner-take-most markets, companies may need to put speed ahead of efficiency, accepting waste and chaos to grab the market first. It is a deliberate, risky choice.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **Speed over efficiency under uncertainty.**
- **Stages of scale:** Family, Tribe, Village, City, Nation, each by employee count, each needing different management.
- **Growth factors and limiters:** market size, distribution, margins, network effects versus lack of product/market fit and operational scalability.
- **Counterintuitive rules:** embrace chaos, launch before you are comfortable, let some fires burn.

**Use it when:** a winner-take-most market, capital on hand, and a rival racing for it.

**Watch out:** needs lots of capital and suits few markets. Criticized for encouraging reckless growth. Most small businesses should not blitzscale.

## [G22] Playing to Win, A.G. Lafley and Roger L. Martin (2013)

**Core idea:** Strategy is a set of linked choices that position a company to win. Drawn from Procter and Gamble's experience under Lafley.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **The Strategy Choice Cascade:** winning aspiration, where to play, how to win, core capabilities, management systems.
- **Where to Play and How to Win** are the heart of strategy.
- **Two ways to win:** cost leadership or differentiation.
- **What would have to be true:** test options by listing conditions and checking the weakest.

**Use it when:** choosing markets, customers and channels, or aligning a leadership team on strategic choices.

**Watch out:** big consumer goods context. Small firms should apply it in a lightweight form.

## [G23] The Goal, Eliyahu M. Goldratt and Jeff Cox (1984)

**Core idea:** A novel about a plant manager who saves his factory by learning the Theory of Constraints: the output of any system is limited by its bottleneck. Improving anything else is mostly wasted.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **The goal is to make money:** measured by throughput, inventory and operating expense.
- **Bottlenecks and non-bottlenecks:** an hour lost at the bottleneck is lost for the whole system.
- **The Five Focusing Steps:** identify the constraint, exploit it, subordinate everything to it, elevate it, then repeat.
- **Dependent events and statistical fluctuations:** variation accumulates along a chain.
- **Local optimization fools you:** keeping everyone busy can lower total output.

**Use it when:** throughput is stuck, work piles up, or teams optimize their own part.

**Watch out:** set in manufacturing; services need translation. The novel simplifies real constraints.

## [G24] Who, Geoff Smart and Randy Street (2008)

**Core idea:** Hiring is the biggest management problem, and most firms hire on gut feel. The A Method gives a structured process to hire high performers.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **Voodoo hiring:** common bad habits like trusting charm or gut.
- **The A Method:** Scorecard, Source, Select, Sell.
- **Scorecard:** mission, outcomes and competencies for the role.
- **Source:** referrals from your network as the top channel.
- **Select through four interviews:** screening, the in-depth "who" interview on career history, focused interviews, and reference interviews.
- **Sell:** address fit, family, freedom, fortune and fun.

**Use it when:** hiring a key role, or after a string of bad hires.

**Watch out:** time-heavy for small firms. "A player" thinking can turn elitist or biased if the scorecard is weak.

## [G25] The Five Dysfunctions of a Team, Patrick Lencioni (2002)

**Core idea:** A fable about a new CEO fixing a broken leadership team. Teams fail through five linked dysfunctions, each building on the one below.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **The five dysfunctions:** absence of trust, fear of conflict, lack of commitment, avoidance of accountability, inattention to results.
- **Vulnerability-based trust:** people admit mistakes and weaknesses.
- **Productive conflict:** open debate on ideas.
- **Commitment through clarity and buy-in,** even without consensus.

**Use it when:** a leadership team avoids hard topics, decisions get revisited, or departments fight.

**Watch out:** a fable with no formal research behind it. Fixes take time and a leader willing to go first.
