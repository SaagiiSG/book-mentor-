# Books: Business Growth department

Built from the 25 Business Growth books (G01 to G06, G08 to G14, G16 to G19, G23 to G30), one entry each, in id order. Quote policy: verbatim quotes appear only from the author talks and public-domain texts listed in talks.md, always with a T id. Book text of copyrighted books is never quoted. Everything else is paraphrased in our own words with book ids.

## [G01] Good to Great, Jim Collins (2001)

**Core idea:** Collins's team compared companies that jumped from average to strong results with similar companies that did not. The jump came from disciplined people, thought and action compounding over years, not one big launch or a celebrity CEO.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **Level 5 Leadership:** the leaders of the companies that made the jump mixed personal humility with fierce will for the company, rather than charisma.
- **First Who, Then What:** get the right people on the bus (and the wrong ones off) before you pick the direction.
- **Confront the Brutal Facts (the Stockdale Paradox):** stay sure you will win in the end while facing the worst facts of the present honestly.
- **The Hedgehog Concept:** do only what sits where three circles meet: what you can be best at, what drives your economic engine (one key ratio, profit per X), and what your people care deeply about.
- **The Flywheel and the Doom Loop:** results come from steady pushes that compound; lurching between new programs resets the wheel.

**Use it when:** the business is decent but stuck, the owner keeps chasing new ideas, or the team needs to agree what the company is best at.

**Watch out:** the method picks winners in hindsight (survivorship bias), and several "great" companies later struggled or failed. Based on large public companies; translate for a 20-person firm.

## [G02] The E-Myth Revisited, Michael E. Gerber (1995)

**Core idea:** Most small businesses are started by skilled technicians who think knowing the work means knowing how to run a business. The fix is to build the business like a franchise prototype that runs on documented systems, so it does not depend on the owner doing the work. Gerber blames the owner's mindset, not the economy or competition.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **The Entrepreneurial Seizure:** a technician quits to escape a boss and ends up with a harder job.
- **The Entrepreneur, the Manager, the Technician:** three personalities inside every owner; the Technician usually wins and the business stalls.
- **Work On your business, not In it:** treat the business as a product you design.
- **The Franchise Prototype:** design the business so ordinary people can deliver a consistent result by following systems. The system is the solution, not the people or the product.
- **The Business Development Process:** innovation, quantification, orchestration: try a better way, measure it, then make it the standard.
- **The Business Development Program:** a seven-step sequence starting from your Primary Aim (what you want your life to be), then Strategic Objective, Organizational Strategy (org chart of functions, not people), and Management, People, Marketing and Systems strategies.

**In the author's words:** "Knowing how to do the work in a business has nothing to do with creating a business that works." (T002) "I'm going to suggest you go to work on your business as though you were going to replicate it 5,000 times." (T002)

**Use it when:** the owner is the bottleneck, quality depends on who does the job, or the owner wants a life outside the business.

**Watch out:** written as a parable with lots of repetition and few specifics. Full systemization can feel wrong for craft or relationship businesses where the owner's judgment is the product.

## [G03] Traction, Gino Wickman (2007)

**Core idea:** Traction presents the Entrepreneurial Operating System (EOS), a simple toolset for running a small or mid-size company around six components and a weekly and quarterly rhythm.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **The Six Key Components:** Vision, People, Data, Issues, Process, Traction (Traction being Rocks plus the meeting pulse).
- **The Vision/Traction Organizer (V/TO):** a two-page plan. Its core is core values and core focus (together, the company's soul); the other six sections carry the plan: 10-year target, marketing strategy, 3-year picture, 1-year plan, quarterly Rocks and the issues list.
- **Accountability Chart and Right People, Right Seats:** structure first, then people; a seat fits when the person understands the role, wants it and has the capacity for it (GWC), and the right person shares the core values (People Analyzer).
- **Rocks:** a few 90-day priorities per person and per company.
- **Scorecard:** a short weekly set of numbers that shows whether the business is on track; ideally every person has a number.
- **Level 10 Meeting and IDS:** a fixed-agenda weekly meeting where issues are worked by Identify, Discuss, Solve.
- **Visionary and Integrator:** an ideas owner paired with an operator who breaks ties.

**In the author's words:** "You cannot build a great company on multiple operating systems. You must choose one." (T004) "I don't know if you knew this, but core values plus core focus equals soul." (T004)

**Use it when:** the company is roughly 10 to 250 people and has growing pains: missed priorities, unclear roles, meetings that wander.

**Watch out:** it is a branded system with paid implementers; some teams apply it mechanically. Light on strategy and market choice.

## [G04] Scaling Up, Verne Harnish (2014)

**Core idea:** Growing companies must get four decisions right: People, Strategy, Execution and Cash. Harnish updates his earlier Rockefeller Habits with one-page tools for each.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **The Four Decisions:** People, Strategy, Execution, Cash.
- **One-Page Strategic Plan (OPSP):** core values, purpose, BHAG, targets, priorities and metrics on one page.
- **Rockefeller Habits:** a short list of execution habits, centered on a few priorities, good data and a meeting rhythm from the daily huddle up to the annual plan.
- **Function Accountability Chart:** who owns each function and its numbers, separate from the org chart.
- **Cash Conversion Cycle:** how many days cash is tied up from paying for inputs to getting paid by customers.
- **The Power of One:** small changes in a few levers (price, volume, cost of goods, overhead, receivable days, payable days, inventory days) and their effect on cash and profit.

**Use it when:** a growing firm of 10 to several hundred people feels chaotic or runs short of cash.

**Watch out:** dense, with many tools; small firms should pick two or three. Some advice leans toward ambitious growth companies.

## [G05] The Lean Startup, Eric Ries (2011)

**Core idea:** Under high uncertainty, a startup's job is to learn what customers will actually pay for, as fast and cheap as possible. A startup is defined by uncertainty, not size. Treat plans as hypotheses with predictions and test them with real behavior.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **Build-Measure-Learn:** a loop to run quickly; plan it backward from what you need to learn.
- **Minimum Viable Product (MVP):** the smallest thing that tests a key assumption with real customers.
- **Leap-of-faith assumptions:** the value hypothesis (do they want it) and the growth hypothesis (how will it spread).
- **Validated Learning and Innovation Accounting:** progress is measured by learning, using actionable metrics rather than vanity metrics or features shipped.
- **Pivot or Persevere:** a regular, deliberate decision. A pivot keeps one foot in what you learned and changes one element at a time. Runway is the number of pivots left.
- **Engines of Growth:** sticky (retention), viral (referral), paid (acquisition spending). Five Whys finds root causes.

**In the author's words:** "the biggest waste that product development faces today is not building things inefficiently but building things very efficiently that nobody wants" (T005)

**Use it when:** launching a new product, service or market with unknown demand.

**Watch out:** less useful for proven businesses where the answer is execution. A poor MVP can damage a brand in a small market where everyone talks.

## [G06] Zero to One, Peter Thiel and Blake Masters (2014)

**Core idea:** Real progress comes from creating something new (zero to one), not copying what works (one to n). Great businesses escape competition by building a kind of monopoly in a market they can dominate.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **Zero to One versus One to n:** invention versus copying.
- **Competition is for losers:** fierce competition destroys profit; aim for a market you can own.
- **Characteristics of a monopoly:** proprietary technology, network effects, economies of scale, and branding.
- **Start small and monopolize:** dominate a small market, then expand.
- **Last mover advantage and secrets:** what matters is who holds the market for the long run, often built on an important truth few people see.
- **The seven questions:** engineering, timing, monopoly, people, distribution, durability, secret.

**In the author's words:** "there isn't that much progress in science or Tech or not as much as Silicon Valley likes to claim" (T006)

**Use it when:** choosing what to build or which market to attack.

**Watch out:** written for venture-backed tech. Most small businesses will not be monopolies, but "dominate a small niche" still applies. Contrarian tone; treat claims as provocations.

## [G08] Blue Ocean Strategy, W. Chan Kim and Renee Mauborgne (2005)

**Core idea:** Instead of fighting rivals in crowded red oceans, create uncontested blue oceans by raising buyer value while lowering cost. Kim stresses that creating a new market does not have to disrupt an incumbent.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **Red Oceans versus Blue Oceans.**
- **The Strategy Canvas:** chart how you and rivals score on the factors the industry competes on.
- **The Four Actions Framework and the ERRC Grid:** which factors to Eliminate, Reduce, Raise and Create.
- **The Six Paths Framework:** look across alternative industries, strategic groups, buyer groups, complementary offerings, functional versus emotional appeal, and time.
- **Three Tiers of Noncustomers:** people who barely use you, refuse the industry, or were never considered.

**In the author's words:** "what I mean by Blu ocean perspective is that we say for example focus on non customer to create new market space" (T007)

**Use it when:** your industry is commoditized, prices are falling, and every competitor looks the same.

**Watch out:** cases are selected in hindsight; successful blue oceans attract copycats quickly. Execution is harder than the canvas suggests.

## [G09] The Innovator's Dilemma, Clayton M. Christensen (1997)

**Core idea:** Well-run leading firms often fail because they do the right things: listen to their best customers and invest in higher-margin improvements. Meanwhile, cheaper, simpler disruptive products start at the low end or in new markets and improve until they take over. Christensen says the cause is the rational pursuit of profit, not bad managers: the leader gladly exits each low tier.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **Sustaining versus Disruptive Technologies:** improvements for current customers versus simpler, cheaper offers that first serve overlooked customers.
- **Resource dependence:** customers and investors shape where a firm spends, which steers it away from small new markets.
- **Performance oversupply:** products improve faster than customers need, opening the door to "good enough" rivals.
- **Separate organization:** give a disruptive effort its own unit with its own cost structure.
- **Enter at the bottom:** attack the leader's best customers and it fights; start at the bottom and it flees.

**In the author's words:** "if you define a business or an approach where you come in at the bottom of them, then in pursuit of profit, the giant is motivated to flee rather than fight you." (T008)

**Use it when:** a cheaper competitor is taking your low end, or you are deciding whether to launch a simpler version of your offer.

**Watch out:** challenged on case selection and predictive power; "disruption" is overused.

## [G10] Profit First, Mike Michalowicz (2014)

**Core idea:** Owners spend whatever is in the bank, so profit ends up as leftovers. Flip the formula: take profit first from every deposit, then run the business on what remains. Same math, variables swapped; the point is behavior.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **Sales minus Profit equals Expenses:** profit is set aside before spending, as a habit on every deposit, not an event at year end.
- **Parkinson's Law applied to cash (small plates):** spending grows to fit the money available, so shrink the plate rather than rely on willpower.
- **Separate bank accounts:** Income, Profit, Owner's Comp, Tax and Operating Expenses.
- **Target Allocation Percentages:** goal percentages by revenue size; start with small steps from current percentages.
- **The 10/25 rhythm and quarterly distributions:** allocate twice a month; pay out part of the profit account each quarter as the owner's reward for risk.

**In the author's words:** "that sales - profit equals expenses and what you'll notice right away is it's the same variable" (T009) "the solution is radically simple get smaller plates don't change yourself get smaller plates" (T009)

**Use it when:** sales come in but money never stays, or the owner underpays themselves.

**Watch out:** it is a behavioral cash system, not accounting or tax advice. Rigid percentages can starve a business that needs investment; consult an accountant.

## [G11] The Hard Thing About Hard Things, Ben Horowitz (2014)

**Core idea:** There is no recipe for the hardest CEO problems: layoffs, firing friends, near-death moments. Horowitz shares how he made such calls.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **The Struggle:** expect stretches when nothing works; keep going and tell the truth.
- **Peacetime CEO versus Wartime CEO:** in peace, expand and empower; in war, focus hard and direct.
- **Take care of the people, the products and the profits, in that order.**
- **Lay people off the right way:** move fast, own it, and treat people with respect.
- **Management Debt:** quick fixes in people decisions (like overpaying to keep someone) that cost more later. In talks he adds decision debt: avoided calls that paralyze a company.
- **Hiring executives:** founders hire senior roles badly, the sales head most of all; learn the job well enough to judge candidates and take blind references.

**In the author's words:** "Decision debt is the worst debt, by the way, cuz it it paralyzes a company." (T010) "CEOs can't really develop executives. They either can do it or they can't." (T010)

**Use it when:** in crisis, cutting costs, firing or demoting a leader, or deciding how to lead through uncertainty.

**Watch out:** venture-backed Silicon Valley context. Some advice assumes boards, investors and big teams.

## [G12] Measure What Matters, John Doerr (2018)

**Core idea:** Objectives and Key Results (OKRs), invented by Andy Grove at Intel in the 1970s, align a team on a few ambitious goals and make progress measurable.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **OKRs:** an objective is what you want to achieve; key results are measurable outcomes showing you got there. They are the few things that deserve special attention, not the sum of all tasks.
- **Four superpowers:** focus and commit, align and connect, track for accountability, stretch for amazing.
- **Committed versus Aspirational OKRs:** must-hit goals versus stretch goals where partial success is fine.
- **CFRs:** Conversations, Feedback and Recognition, the ongoing coaching that makes OKRs work.
- **Leader commitment:** OKRs fail most when the CEO does not use them personally.

**In the author's words:** "I think of those in the football analogy as the objective is the goal posts and the key results are the 10 yard markers as we march our way down the field" (T011)

**Use it when:** a team needs to agree what matters this quarter and how to measure it.

**Watch out:** heavy for a team of five. Tying key goals to bonuses makes teams risk-averse.

## [G13] Crossing the Chasm, Geoffrey A. Moore (1991)

**Core idea:** New technology products are adopted by groups in sequence. There is a deep gap between early adopters, who buy into a shared vision, and the early majority, pragmatists who buy proven solutions their peers use, and many products die there.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **Technology Adoption Life Cycle:** innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, laggards.
- **The Chasm:** pragmatists want references from people like them, which visionaries cannot provide.
- **The D-Day strategy (beachhead):** focus everything on one narrow segment and dominate it. A huge group such as the Fortune 500 is not a beachhead.
- **The Whole Product:** everything the customer needs to get the result, not just the core product.
- **Bowling Pin strategy:** win one segment, then use it to knock over adjacent ones.

**In the author's words:** "you want to have a Target segment that is big enough to matter small enough to lead and a good fit with your crown jewels that's the formula" (T012) "pragmatic people buy what they see their peers are buying" (T012)

**Use it when:** you have enthusiastic early customers but growth stalls with mainstream buyers.

**Watch out:** written for B2B technology; less relevant to local services.

## [G14] Good Strategy Bad Strategy, Richard Rumelt (2011)

**Core idea:** Most "strategy" is fluff or wish lists. Real strategy diagnoses the key challenge and focuses effort on a coherent response. Bad strategy is an active set of mistaken beliefs, not just a missing plan.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **The Kernel:** diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent action. A useful diagnosis points to something you can act on.
- **Hallmarks of Bad Strategy:** fluff, failure to face the challenge, mistaking goals for strategy, and bad strategic objectives (in talks he includes the "dog's dinner", a long committee list of items).
- **Sources of power:** leverage, proximate objectives, chain-link systems, design, focus, using advantage, and riding dynamics.

**In the author's words:** "a strategy is not what you hope your performance will be strategy is about how you're going to get there" (T013)

**Use it when:** the plan is a list of targets with no clear story of how to beat the obstacle.

**Watch out:** more a way of thinking than a step-by-step method. Examples are large firms and military history.

## [G16] Built to Sell, John Warrillow (2010)

**Core idea:** A business that depends on its owner is hard to sell. Told as a fable, it shows how to turn a custom service firm into a standardized, sellable company.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **Productize a service with the trifecta of scale:** rank every offer on teachable (to staff), valuable (hard to commoditize) and repeatable (customers must rebuy often, not that you can repeat the process); keep the best, drop the worst.
- **Stop custom work:** say no to one-off projects that need you.
- **Recurring revenue and up-front payment** raise value and fund growth; buyers rank contract revenue above consumables. Buyers also fund working capital, so a positive cash cycle raises the price.
- **Sell a few things to many customers:** a concentrated customer base scares buyers.
- **Build a sales team** that sells the standard offer without the owner: hire two reps at once so one cannot hold you hostage.
- **Management team with long-term incentives** (a deferred bonus paid over three years), and step back to prove the business runs without you.

**In the author's words:** "when a buyer buys your business they've actually got to write two checks" (T014)

**Use it when:** planning an exit in a few years or wanting a business that runs without the owner.

**Watch out:** fable built around service firms; valuation multiples vary widely.

## [G17] Clockwork, Mike Michalowicz (2018)

**Core idea:** A business should run like a clock without the owner doing everything. Free the owner by identifying the most important role, protecting it, and shifting work into systems and other people.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **The 4 Ds:** Doing, Deciding, Delegating, Designing; owners must move time toward Designing (the book suggests target ratios, detail uncertain).
- **Queen Bee Role (QBR):** the one function that most drives the company's success; protect it.
- **Capture systems:** record how work is done now instead of writing manuals from scratch.
- **Balance the team:** match people to work and remove bottlenecks.
- **Commit to the clients you serve best** and to your big promise.
- **The Four-Week Vacation:** a forcing test that the business can run without you.

**Use it when:** the owner is overloaded and the business stalls whenever they step away.

**Watch out:** written for owner-run small firms. The vacation goal can be unrealistic early on.

## [G18] Rework, Jason Fried and David Heinemeier Hansson (2010)

**Core idea:** A short, contrarian guide to running a small, profitable, calm company. Keep things simple, small and focused, and ignore much of conventional startup wisdom.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **Planning is guessing:** decide based on now, not long-range plans.
- **Your real competition is your costs:** if you spend less than you make, you need fewer customers to win.
- **Underdo the competition:** do less, but better.
- **Embrace constraints:** limited time and money force good decisions.
- **Hire when it hurts:** do the job yourself first, hire only when necessary. In talks Fried adds a one-year check: knowing what you know now, would you hire this person again?
- **Meetings and interruptions are toxic** to productive work.

**In the author's words:** "if you're making more than you spend, then your competition is your cost and that's what you're really in business against is how much it costs you to stay in business." (T016)

**Use it when:** the owner wants a profitable, lean business without outside money or rapid headcount growth.

**Watch out:** anecdotal, from a small software company. Some lines are slogans that do not suit every industry.

## [G19] High Output Management, Andrew S. Grove (1983)

**Core idea:** A manager's output is the output of the team they run and the teams they influence. Grove applies production thinking to management: find the limiting step, measure with good indicators, and spend time where it gives the most leverage.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **Managerial output and leverage:** pick activities that raise many people's output.
- **Production principles:** find the limiting step and catch problems at the lowest-cost stage.
- **Indicators:** leading indicators, and paired indicators that guard against gaming one number.
- **One-on-ones:** regular meetings driven by the report, for teaching and information.
- **Task-relevant maturity:** adjust how closely you manage based on someone's experience with that task.
- **Decision-making:** open discussion, a clear decision, then full support.

**Use it when:** building a management layer, setting up meetings, or measuring a team.

**Watch out:** 1980s Intel; some parts suit only large firms.

## [G23] The Goal, Eliyahu M. Goldratt and Jeff Cox (1984)

**Core idea:** A novel about a plant manager who saves his factory by learning the Theory of Constraints: the output of any system is limited by its bottleneck. Improving anything else is mostly wasted. Goldratt says it merges three real plants with real numbers, and that the deeper lesson is cause-and-effect reasoning.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **The goal is to make money:** measured by throughput, inventory and operating expense.
- **Bottlenecks and non-bottlenecks:** an hour lost at the bottleneck is lost for the whole system.
- **The Five Focusing Steps:** identify the constraint, exploit it, subordinate everything to it, elevate it, then repeat.
- **Dependent events and statistical fluctuations:** variation accumulates along a chain.
- **Local optimization fools you:** keeping everyone busy can lower total output.

**In the author's words:** "Cause and effect is simple. If you read any of my books, it's embarrassingly simple. But it's so decisive." (T018) "Companies don't know even to handle one bottleneck." (T018)

**Use it when:** throughput is stuck, work piles up, or teams optimize their own part.

**Watch out:** set in manufacturing; services need translation. The novel simplifies real constraints.

## [G24] Who, Geoff Smart and Randy Street (2008)

**Core idea:** Hiring is the biggest management problem, and most firms hire on gut feel. Smart says managers get hires wrong about half the time. The A Method gives a structured process to hire high performers.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **Voodoo hiring:** common bad habits like trusting charm or gut.
- **The A Method:** Scorecard, Source, Select, Sell; four steps, none skipped.
- **Scorecard:** mission, outcomes and competencies for the role, kept separate. Outcomes are numbers with dates and matter most; set the bar so only a small share of candidates could hit it. A scorecard is not a job description, and experience is not a competency.
- **Source:** referrals from your network as the top channel.
- **Select through four interviews:** screening, the in-depth "who" interview on career history, focused interviews, and reference interviews.
- **Sell:** address fit, family, freedom, fortune and fun.

**In the author's words:** "job descriptions are vague scorecards are specific" (T019)

**Use it when:** hiring a key role, or after a string of bad hires.

**Watch out:** time-heavy for small firms. "A player" thinking can turn elitist or biased if the scorecard is weak.

## [G25] The Five Dysfunctions of a Team, Patrick Lencioni (2002)

**Core idea:** A fable about a new CEO fixing a broken leadership team. Teams fail through five linked dysfunctions, each building on the one below.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **The five dysfunctions:** absence of trust, fear of conflict, lack of commitment, avoidance of accountability, inattention to results.
- **Vulnerability-based trust:** people can admit they do not know, need help, or got it wrong; this is different from just predicting how each other will act. The leader goes first.
- **Productive conflict:** open debate on ideas, not people.
- **Commitment through clarity and buy-in,** without consensus: everyone weighs in, then the leader decides (disagree and commit).
- **Peer accountability:** the best accountability is between peers, not only from the boss.

**In the author's words:** "I fundamentally believe that if people don't weigh in they don't buy in" (T020)

**Use it when:** a leadership team avoids hard topics, decisions get revisited, or departments fight.

**Watch out:** a fable with no formal research behind it. Fixes take time and a leader willing to go first.

## [G26] The Art of War, Sun Tzu (c. 5th century BC)

**Core idea:** A short classic on winning conflicts at the least cost. Weigh your position against the opponent's before acting, win through position rather than open battle, keep campaigns short, and adapt to the ground. Public domain; Giles translation (T101).

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **Five constant factors:** the Moral Law (people in accord with their leader), Heaven (timing and seasons), Earth (terrain and distance), the Commander (wisdom, sincerity, benevolence, courage, strictness), and Method and discipline (organization, supply, control of spending).
- **Seven comparisons:** which side has the Moral Law, the abler leader, the advantage of timing and terrain, stricter discipline, the stronger force, better training, and more consistent reward and punishment.
- **Win without fighting:** the best move is to defeat the opponent's plans, next to split their alliances, then to fight their army; attacking fortified positions is worst.
- **Cost of a long campaign:** no state benefits from prolonged war; win fast and live off the opponent's supplies where you can.
- **Avoid strength, strike weakness:** make yourself impossible to defeat first, then wait for an opening; attack where the opponent is unprepared.

**In the author's words:** "Thus do many calculations lead to victory, and few calculations to defeat: how much more no calculation at all!" (T101) "supreme excellence consists in breaking the enemy's resistance without fighting." (T101)

**Use it when:** facing a direct rival, a price war or a new competitor, or deciding whether a fight is worth its cost.

**Watch out:** written for war; customers are not enemies, and the line on deception is about feints, not a license to mislead. Translations differ; the popular "subdue the enemy without fighting" is a paraphrase.

## [G27] Rich Dad Poor Dad, Robert T. Kiyosaki (1997)

**Core idea:** Two father figures: a salaried "poor dad" and a business-owner "rich dad" who taught that the rich build or buy assets that pay them, while others work for money and buy liabilities.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **Assets versus liabilities:** an asset puts money in your pocket; a liability takes it out. Your own home fails this test.
- **Don't work for money:** make money work for you through businesses, property and paper assets.
- **The rat race:** rising income met by rising spending keeps people stuck.
- **Financial education:** learn accounting, investing, markets and law.
- **Debt as a tool:** good debt buys assets that cash-flow; consumer debt does not.

**In the author's words:** "if it puts money in my pocket it's an asset" (T021) "I said your house is not an asset" (T021)

**Use it when:** the owner treats the business as a job or needs a simple test for whether a purchase pays.

**Watch out:** anecdotal, with little detail on how; the existence of Rich Dad has been questioned. Kiyosaki says the book never said to rush into buying houses. His comfort with debt and tax claims can hurt a thin-margin firm; confirm with an accountant and attorney.

## [G28] The Psychology of Money, Morgan Housel (2020)

**Core idea:** Doing well with money depends more on behavior than on knowledge. Short stories on luck, risk, envy, compounding and room for error, and why knowing what is enough beats chasing more.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **Rich versus wealthy:** rich is money you can spend now; wealth is money you have not spent, and it buys independence.
- **Enough:** the hardest skill is stopping the goalposts from moving; expectations you can control matter as much as income.
- **Control of your time:** the highest return of money is being able to do what you want, when you want.
- **Room for error:** save without a specific reason, avoid ruin, and plan for your plans not going to plan.
- **Compounding and staying in the game:** long, uninterrupted runs matter more than high returns.

**In the author's words:** "Wealth is money that you did not spend and maybe you will not spend." (T022) "the hardest financial skill is getting the goal posts to stop moving" (T022)

**Use it when:** the owner chases growth with no idea of enough, runs with no cash buffer, takes on risky debt, or needs to decide between a lifestyle business and a bigger push.

**Watch out:** personal finance stories, not a business method; translate household to company.

## [G29] Extreme Ownership, Jocko Willink and Leif Babin (2015)

**Core idea:** Two former Navy SEAL officers argue that leaders own everything in their world: every failure is theirs to own and fix.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **Extreme Ownership:** no blaming others; the leader owns the mission, the plan and the outcome.
- **No bad teams, only bad leaders:** standards are what the leader tolerates.
- **Believe in the mission:** a leader must understand why before they can explain it.
- **Cover and Move:** teams support each other toward the shared goal instead of winning their own piece.
- **Simple:** plans everyone can understand.
- **Prioritize and Execute:** under pressure, pick the most important problem, act on it, then move to the next.
- **Decentralized Command:** leaders explain intent and limits so junior leaders can decide on their own.

**In the author's words:** "in a leadership position you're not worried about yourself getting hurt or killed you're worried about your guys getting hurt or killed" (T023)

**Use it when:** leaders blame others, a crisis has too many fires, or every decision funnels to the owner.

**Watch out:** military stories make it sound simpler than business is; ownership can turn into a leader doing every task. The interview does not cover these frameworks.

## [G30] The Almanack of Naval Ravikant, Eric Jorgenson (2020)

**Core idea:** A collection of Naval Ravikant's posts and interviews on wealth and happiness. Wealth comes from owning things that earn without your time, built from specific knowledge, leverage, accountability and judgment, compounded over years.

**Key frameworks and ideas:**
- **Wealth is assets that earn while you sleep:** own equity, not just hours. Naval now prefers a broader view: wealth is what you are able to transform in the world, and knowledge is the big multiplier.
- **Specific knowledge:** skills that come from your own curiosity and cannot easily be trained into someone else.
- **Leverage:** labor and capital need permission; code and media scale without it.
- **Accountability (skin in the game):** take risk under your own name and own the results.
- **Play long-term games with long-term people:** compounding in relationships and returns; no get-rich-quick route.
- **Judgment:** in an age of leverage, being right matters more than working hard.

**In the author's words:** "If you pay careful attention, you realize that knowledge is the big multiplier. It's not the capital." (T024)

**Use it when:** the owner trades time for money, is choosing between hiring and building leverage through software or content, or wants a long-term view of what to own.

**Watch out:** aphorisms, not a method; code and media leverage suits some businesses far more than others.
